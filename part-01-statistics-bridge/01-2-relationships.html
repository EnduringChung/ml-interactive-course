1.2 Relationships Between Variables

How do two variables move together? Covariance and correlation quantify co-movement; regression (Part 2) will model it. The skill here is knowing what r measures, what it ignores, and the four ways it lies.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Covariance averages the products of paired deviations:

\[\text{cov}(x, y) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})\]

Positive when variables move together, negative when opposed — but its magnitude depends on the units (expression levels in raw counts vs log-CPM give different covariances for the same relationship). The Pearson correlation fixes units by dividing both SDs:

\[r = \frac{\text{cov}(x,y)}{s_x s_y} \in [-1, 1]\]

Key reading: r measures linear association strength, not slope (slope lives in regression) and not agreement (r = 1 for y = x + 100, which disagrees by 100 everywhere).

Spearman correlation = Pearson correlation applied to ranks. It detects any monotone relationship and shrugs at outliers:

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why is covariance useless for comparing relationships measured in different units, and what exactly does dividing by \(s_x s_y\) fix?
  2. What is the relationship between r and the R² of simple linear regression?
  3. Give two datasets where Spearman = 1 but Pearson < 1.
  4. Does r = 0 mean “no relationship”? Does r = 0.9 mean “x causes y”?
  5. One outlier flips your r from +0.6 to −0.6. Which coefficient is more trustworthy here, and why?
Answers
  1. Covariance’s magnitude scales with the units of both variables (cov(x, y) = 5 for x in minutes is 300 for x in seconds). Pearson divides by the product of SDs — a pure number in [−1, 1] — so relationships are comparable across studies, units, and scalings.
  2. For simple linear regression, \(R^2 = r^2\) — the fraction of y’s variance explained by the best straight line. r = 0.7 → the line explains 49%.
  3. Any perfectly monotone but nonlinear pair: (x, eˣ), (x, x³) over positive x, (age, income) rising with diminishing returns. Ranks are identical; straightness is not.
  4. No and no. r = 0 only rules out linear association (quadratics, U-shapes, interactions can be strong). r measures association; causation requires design (randomization, natural experiments) or much stronger modeling assumptions.
  5. Spearman. A single point moved Pearson’s estimate violently because r is built from raw deviations — the outlier dominates both \(s_x\), \(s_y\) and the cross-products. Spearman caps any point’s influence at “one rank step.”

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1 — Anscombe’s quartet, cell 1. This classic dataset has 4 pairs with (nearly) identical mean, SD, r ≈ 0.816, and regression line — utterly different shapes. Build pair I and pair III, compute their summary statistics, and plot both.

Solution
Identical r, identical fitted line — because r and OLS are single-number summaries that different data structures can satisfy. Summary statistics compress away exactly the things you care about; plotting is not optional.

Exercise 2. Construct data where adding ONE observation flips the sign of Pearson r (from negative to positive), while Spearman barely moves. Report all three r values before/after.

Solution
The added point sits at the extreme corner, contributing huge cross-deviations \((x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\) — enough to outvote 40 points. Spearman only sees “new highest x, new highest y” — one rank step on each side. Any reported Pearson on real data should come with a scatterplot or a rank-correlation cross-check.

Exercise 3. For a bivariate normal sample (n = 30, true ρ = 0), empirically show the sampling distribution of r under the null, and verify that |r| > \(2/\sqrt{n-1}\) (rough 5% rule) happens about 5% of the time.

Solution
≈ 0.05: with n = 30, “noise correlations” of ±0.37 are routine. Two implications: small-n correlation claims need formal tests (1.4), and large correlation matrices (0.3 pandas drills) will always show impressive-looking r values somewhere — that’s multiplication of opportunities, not biology.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Correlation ≠ causation — and neither correlation nor its absence is about linear vs nonlinear: r ≈ 0 coexists with perfect functional dependence (the x² plot).
  • Range restriction and range enhancement: selecting only high-GPA applicants lowers r; mixing two clusters inflates it (Simpson’s paradox lives here — see Complex Questions).
  • Pearson needs rough linearity and tolerates outliers badly; Spearman needs only monotonicity but discards magnitude information (two datasets with identical ranks can have very different practical effect sizes).
  • Correlation matrices in high dimensions (10,000 genes → 50M pairs): extreme r values appear by chance alone (Exercise 3 logic × opportunities). Multiple-testing discipline (Part 12) applies to “top correlated pairs” lists.
  • Averaging correlations across groups is invalid (r is not on an additive scale — use Fisher z, or analyze per group).
  • Bootstrap or Fisher-z CI for r, never “r ± 0.05” from folklore. Formal test: stats.pearsonr returns the p-value alongside.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Show that for simple linear regression the fitted slope is \(b = r \cdot (s_y / s_x)\), and explain the two failure modes of “reading” r as if it were an effect size.

Hint Write the OLS slope for standardized variables; then unstandardize.
Solution Standardize both variables: \(\tilde x = (x-\bar x)/s_x\), \(\tilde y = (y-\bar y)/s_y\). For standardized data the OLS slope is \(\sum \tilde x_i \tilde y_i / \sum \tilde x_i^2 = (n-1)\,\text{cov}/(n-1)\,\text{var} = r\). Rescaling back multiplies by \(s_y/s_x\), giving \(b = r s_y/s_x\). Failure modes: (1) slope from r — r = 0.5 means nothing about units; a weak correlation with huge \(s_y/s_x\) can be a big effect. (2) r as slope — the same physical relationship yields different r if you restrict the x-range (range restriction lowers both spread and r) while the slope \(r s_y/s_x\) is more stable. Report both: r (strength of linear association), slope (effect per unit x), CI each.

Q2. The t-statistic for testing ρ = 0 is \(t = r\sqrt{(n-2)/(1-r^2)}\). Verify numerically that stats.pearsonr’s p-value matches the two-sided t-test with df = n−2, then explain what happens to that p-value’s meaning when you compute it for the largest of 10,000 gene-pair correlations.

Solution
The p-value is calibrated for one pre-specified pair. For the maximum over 10,000 pairs, the test is applied to the extreme order statistic of ~10,000 correlated tests: even under global null, the smallest p-value is routinely ≪ 0.05 (probability of at least one “significant” pair ≈ 1 − 0.95¹⁰⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 1). The honest procedure re-tests the selected pair in fresh data or applies family-wise/FDR correction — the entire subject of Part 12.

7. Summary

  • Covariance is direction × scale; Pearson r standardizes it to [−1, 1]; Spearman replaces values with ranks.
  • r describes linear association only: U-shapes hide at r ≈ 0; monotone curves lose r < 1.
  • r² is explained-variance for a straight line; slope is r·(sy/sx) — never confuse strength with effect size.
  • One outlier can flip Pearson; Spearman caps that influence — report both when data is dirty.
  • Sampling noise gives |r| ≈ 0.3 routinely at n = 30; scanning many pairs manufactures “discoveries.”
  • Per-group correlation, plots, and range questions come before any causal sentence.
  • Association is a description; deciding whether a pattern would reappear in new samples requires probability and inference — 1.3 Probability essentials.
