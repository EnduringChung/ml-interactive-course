1.1 Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics compress a sample into a few honest numbers: where is the center, how wide is the spread, what shape is the distribution. Every model diagnostic, every “significant difference,” every anomaly alert you will ever compute rests on these primitives — so the definitions (including the ones with silent variants, like population vs sample SD) must be reflexes.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Center. The mean \(\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i\) is the balance point; the median is the middle value by rank. They coincide for symmetric data; for right-skewed data, mean > median (a few large values drag the mean).

Spread. The variance is the average squared deviation from the mean. Two flavors:

\[s^2_{\text{pop}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \qquad s^2_{\text{sample}} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2\]

The \(n-1\) (Bessel’s correction) in the sample version compensates for the fact that \(\bar{x}\) was estimated from the same data — deviations from an estimated center are systematically too small. Standard deviation is the square root, back in data units.

Position and shape. Quartiles split the sorted data into quarters; the IQR (Q3 − Q1) is the spread of the middle half — robust to outliers. Skewness measures asymmetry (positive = right tail).

Robustness ranking (how much a single extreme value can move the statistic):

  • mean, SD — sensitive (breakdown point 0%)
  • median, IQR — robust (breakdown point 50%)
  • trimmed mean — tunable middle ground

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why does the sample variance divide by \(n-1\) instead of \(n\)?
  2. Your gene-expression distribution has mean 210 and median 85. What shape do you suspect, and which two numbers would you report?
  3. Is the IQR affected by multiplying all values by 10? By adding one extreme value?
  4. When is the mode the most informative measure of center?
  5. What does ddof=0 vs ddof=1 change in np.std, and which does pandas use by default?
Answers
  1. Deviations are taken from the sample mean, which is itself fitted to the data; the fitted center absorbs part of the variability, shrinking deviations. Dividing by the degrees of freedom \(n-1\) unbias the estimate of the population variance (for normal data, exactly).
  2. Strong right skew (a minority of very high values). Report median and IQR (robust pair); if you must report the mean, report it with the median so the skew is visible.
  3. Multiplying by 10: IQR scales by 10 (it’s in data units). One extreme value beyond the fences: IQR is unaffected as long as it doesn’t cross the 25th/75th percentiles — that’s the point of quartile-based spread.
  4. For categorical or discrete data (“most common category”), and for multimodal continuous data where mean and median both describe nothing real (“typical patient: bimodal, modes at X and Y”).
  5. ddof = delta degrees of freedom subtracted from n. ddof=0 → population formula; ddof=1 → sample formula. NumPy defaults to 0, pandas to 1 — mixing pandas and NumPy without noticing this is a classic silent bug.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Write summarize(x) returning {"n", "mean", "sd", "median", "iqr", "min", "max"} using sample SD. Test it on normal data and on lognormal data; state which summary number changes most between the two and why.

Solution
The mean and SD blow up under lognormal data (they inherit the heavy tail), while median and IQR stay near their symmetric-data values — the empirical demonstration of robustness. Report median/IQR for skewed data.

Exercise 2. IQR outlier rule: flag values outside \([Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR},\ Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}]\) in the dataset below. Then compute z-scores and flag \(|z| > 3\). Compare the flagged sets and explain the disagreement.

Solution
The z-score rule uses the mean and SD — which the outlier itself inflates. One extreme value widened the SD so much that even the outlier isn’t 3 SDs out (masking). Quartile-based rules don’t have this self-blinding property: for small samples, prefer the IQR fence.

Exercise 3. Empirically verify Bessel’s correction: sample n = 5 points from a known distribution many times; for each sample compute variance with ddof=0 and ddof=1; average each. Which average converges to the true variance?

Solution
ddof=0 averages to \(0.8\sigma^2\) here (\((n-1)/n = 4/5\)) — a 20% underestimate, every time, silently. This is why the divisor choice is not a technicality.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • pandas vs NumPy ddof defaults disagree (pandas std/var use ddof=1; NumPy uses ddof=0). Numbers in mixed reports can differ by ~√(n/(n−1)) for no reason.
  • NaN handling differs: np.mean poisons, np.nanmean skips, pandas .mean() skips and warns differently — know which behavior produced a reported number.
  • Percentile interpolation: np.percentile linearly interpolates by default; small-n quartiles depend on this convention (9 conventions exist; R’s type=7 matches NumPy’s linear).
  • SD is not the “typical deviation” for skewed data: with a heavy right tail, most deviations are below the SD. IQR communicates spread more honestly there.
  • The mean minimizes squared error, the median absolute error — which is why least squares regression (Part 2) is mean-focused and least-absolutes-deviations is median-focused.
  • stats.mode behavior changed across SciPy versions (now returns a ModeResult object); pd.Series.mode is the stable, readable choice.
  • Descriptives ≠ inference: a summary is a claim about this sample; Part 1.4 is about claims regarding the population.

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated summary function has three bugs. Run it on the test data and find them.

What’s wrong, and the fixed version
  1. Population SD (ddof=0) — for a sample summary this underestimates spread; specify and use ddof=1.
  2. No NaN policy — the function dies on the NaN (mean → nan, max → nan) without telling the caller whether NaNs were dropped. Make the policy explicit.
  3. range on data containing NaN raises/propagates nan; also “range” is a fragile descriptives choice (a single typo’d value sets it) — min/max separately at most, or a robust span.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove (or verify by simulation) that the sample mean is the value \(c\) minimizing \(\sum_i (x_i - c)^2\), and the sample median minimizes \(\sum_i |x_i - c|\). What does each say about which errors the mean vs median take most seriously?

Hint Differentiate \(\sum (x_i-c)^2\) in \(c\); for the absolute value, argue by moving \(c\) across data points and counting how many terms’ slope changes.
Solution Mean: \(f(c)=\sum(x_i-c)^2\) has \(f'(c) = -2\sum(x_i-c)\), zero exactly at \(c=\bar{x}\) (second derivative \(2n>0\)). Median: \(g(c)=\sum|x_i-c|\) has slope \(= (\#\,\text{points} < c) - (\#\,\text{points} > c)\), which changes sign where the counts balance — the median. So the mean’s optimality is defined in squared error: a single far point contributes quadratically, dominating the objective — hence the mean chases outliers. The median’s objective is linear in each error: one far point costs the same as one near point — hence its robustness. This one paragraph explains robust statistics, why least-squares regression is outlier-sensitive, and why quantile regression exists.

Q2. Two labs each report SD ≈ 2.0 for the same assay. Lab A ran n = 5, Lab B ran n = 5,000. Construct a scenario where both reports are true, and explain which additional number distinguishes them (preview: it is not the SD).

Solution Both labs can honestly report \(s \approx 2.0\) — SD describes individual measurement spread and barely depends on n. What distinguishes them is the precision of their estimated means: the standard error \(s/\sqrt{n}\) ≈ 0.89 for Lab A vs 0.028 for Lab B — a 30-fold difference. SD answers “how noisy is one measurement,” SE answers “how well do I know the center.” Confusing the two (reporting SD where SE is meant, or vice versa) is a notorious publication bug; the inference machinery that formalizes it arrives in 1.4 Statistical inference.

7. Summary

  • Center: mean (balance, sensitive) vs median (rank, robust); skew shows as mean − median.
  • Spread: SD for symmetric data, IQR for skewed; ddof=0 vs 1 is a real bias, and pandas/NumPy disagree by default.
  • Quartiles + IQR fences detect outliers without being blinded by them; z-scores can be.
  • NaN policy is part of the statistic: nan-skipping is a decision, not a default to trust blindly.
  • Mean ↔︎ squared error, median ↔︎ absolute error: robustness follows from the loss function.
  • SD describes single measurements; SE describes the precision of a mean — next pages formalize this.
  • Summaries describe samples; to argue about populations you need probability and inference: 1.2 Relationships first asks how two variables move together.
