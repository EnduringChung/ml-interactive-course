1.4 Statistical Inference

Inference answers the question every experiment asks: is this pattern real, or would chance produce it? Confidence intervals quantify uncertainty about a population quantity; hypothesis tests quantify how surprising the data would be under a “nothing is happening” model. Both are built on one idea — the sampling distribution of a statistic.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The standard error. A sample mean varies from sample to sample with SD ≈ \(s/\sqrt{n}\) — the standard error (SE). Small n means the SE is big means the sample mean is a shaky estimate of the population mean.

Confidence interval. A 95% CI for a mean is approximately

\[\bar{x} \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\]

where the t-quantile widens the interval for small samples. Interpretation (get this exactly right): if the sampling procedure were repeated many times, 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true mean — it is a property of the procedure, not a 95% probability statement about this particular interval.

Hypothesis test logic. State \(H_0\) (no effect) → compute a statistic measuring departure from \(H_0\) → the p-value is P(data at least this extreme | \(H_0\)). Small p = the null struggles to explain the data. It is not P(\(H_0\) | data).

Errors and power. Two ways to be wrong: Type I — declare an effect under a true null (rate = α); Type II — miss a real effect (rate = β); power = 1 − β, driven by effect size, n, α, and noise.

2. Concept Questions

  1. A 95% CI is (2.1, 3.9). Is “P(true mean is in (2.1, 3.9)) = 0.95” a correct reading?
  2. Why does a p-value not tell you the probability the null hypothesis is true?
  3. What does the t in “t-test” buy you over a z-test, and when does the distinction vanish?
  4. Your test on n = 5 has p = 0.20. Which conclusions are valid: (a) “no effect exists”, (b) “effect not demonstrated”, (c) “need more data or a better design”?
  5. Why is equal_var=False (Welch) the sensible default for two-sample t-tests?
Answers
  1. No. The interval is now fixed — it either contains the mean or not. The 95% describes the procedure’s long-run success rate across hypothetical repetitions. (Frequentist probability attaches to procedures, not fixed quantities.)
  2. The p-value is computed assuming the null is true; it can’t then assign that null a probability. It is P(data at least this extreme | H₀) — evidence about the data under H₀, not about H₀ given the data. That reversal is exactly Bayes’ rule, and frequentist testing deliberately doesn’t do it (a prior on hypotheses would be required).
  3. t replaces the known population σ with the estimated s, adding uncertainty that widens critical values (t₀.₉₇₅,₄ = 2.78 vs z = 1.96). As df → ∞, t → z; by n ≈ 30–50 the difference is negligible.
    1. and (c) only. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence; a high p-value at tiny n is uninformative, not exculpatory. Never conclude “no effect” without a power/equivalence argument.
  5. Real biological groups rarely have equal variances, and Student’s pooled t can inflate false positives under inequality; Welch’s correction keeps Type I error near nominal with negligible power loss — so it’s the robust default.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. For the two-sample experiment below, compute (a) Welch t-test, (b) the 95% CI of each group mean, (c) the 95% CI for the difference in means (bootstrap is fine). State the decision and the estimate-with-uncertainty sentence you would put in a report.

Solution
Report sentence: “Treatment increased the marker by an estimated 1.5 units (bootstrap 95% CI 0.3 to 2.7; Welch p = 0.02).” The CI is the quantitative claim; the p-value only grades the surprise under “no difference.” Also note the CI excludes 0 exactly when p < 0.05 (two-sided) — consistency check.

Exercise 2. Multiple testing preview, empirically: simulate 100 independent two-group experiments under the global null (α = 0.05 each). (a) Expected number of “significant” results? (b) Empirical probability that at least one is significant? (c) Repeat at α = 0.001. What family-wise lesson emerges?

Solution
At α = 0.05, ~5 false positives per 100-null family and ≈ 99.4% chance of at least one. The family-wise error explodes with the number of tests — the problem Bonferroni and FDR (12.3, 12.4) solve.

Exercise 3. Demonstrate the effect of skew on the t-test/CI: sample n = 10 from a lognormal; bootstrap the sampling distribution of the mean; compare the bootstrap CI to the t-formula CI. Which is trustworthy here, and what sample size restores the t-approximation (test empirically)?

Solution
At n = 10 the mean’s true sampling distribution is strongly right-skewed — the symmetric t-CI is a poor description (and its 95% coverage is off). The bootstrap CI inherits the skew. At n = 100 the CLT has done its work and the two CIs nearly agree. Lesson: t-methods assume approximate normality of the sampling distribution, which skewed small samples violate; alternatives are log-transform, nonparametric tests, or larger n.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • p-value misreadings (all wrong, all common): P(H₀ true) = p; p = 0.03 means “3% chance the result is chance”; p > 0.05 means “no effect”; p = 0.049 vs 0.051 is a meaningful difference. The p-value is one number about one conditional probability — treat it as such.
  • The t-test compares means; it does not “prove normality” — with n = 5 you cannot even check the assumption well. Prefer Welch + report effect size + CI.
  • Paired vs unpaired is a design property: repeated measures on the same unit demand paired analysis (ttest_rel); pairing halves the noise only if the pairing is real.
  • Effect size ≠ significance: at n = 100,000, a 0.1% difference gets p ≈ 0; at n = 5, a huge effect gets p ≈ 0.3. Report effect + CI, let n decide detectability, not importance.
  • One-sided tests halve the p-value but require pre-registered direction — switching sides after looking at data is p-hacking.
  • Stopping rules break calibration: “collect until p < 0.05” inflates Type I error far above 5% (the simulation drill adapts to demonstrate this).

What’s wrong, and the fixed version

  3. Sequential peeking: continuing until significance guarantees eventual “significance” even under a true null — Type I error ≈ 100%. Fix: pre-set n, or use a formal sequential design (alpha-spending); simplest demonstration — fix n and rerun.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive why the SE of a mean is \(s/\sqrt{n}\) using the variance-addition rule from 1.3, and explain the practical arms race it implies: to halve the SE of the mean, how much more data do you need? To halve the noise of each measurement?

Hint Write Var(x̄) = Var(Σxᵢ/n²) with independence, then take square roots twice.
Solution With independent \(x_i\) of variance \(\sigma^2\): \(\text{Var}(\bar x) = \text{Var}\!\big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum x_i\big) = \tfrac{1}{n^2}\,n\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/n\), so \(\text{SE} = \sigma/\sqrt n\) — square-root law. Halving the SE of the mean requires 4× the data; the SE of the mean is about precision of knowledge, and knowledge is expensive. Halving the noise of each measurement (σ) is an instrument/protocol question, not a statistics question — and it pays off in every subsequent analysis. First optimize measurement noise, then buy n.

Q2. Construct and demonstrate the “optional stopping” inflation: under a true null, run the peeking loop (test after every new pair, stop at p < 0.05 or n = 200). Estimate the realized Type I error. Compare to a fixed-n = 200 test. What does this say about A/B test dashboards that highlight “significance” daily?

Solution
Peeking inflates the false-positive rate from 5% to roughly 30–50% — the more checkpoints, the worse. Daily-looked-at dashboards with “significance” badges are exactly this experiment; the fixes are fixed horizons, sequential designs (alpha spending), or Bayesian monitoring with pre-agreed decision rules.

7. Summary

  • Inference = sampling distributions: SE = s/√n; means shrink noise by √n — 4× data per 2× precision.
  • CI: range of parameter values the data can’t refute (at the chosen level); it’s the procedure that has 95% coverage, not your interval.
  • p-value = P(data this extreme | H₀); never P(H₀), never “chance of being right.”
  • Welch two-sample t is the default; paired designs need paired tests; ranks for non-normal data.
  • Power depends on effect, n, α, noise — underpowered studies “fail” without evidence of absence.
  • Multiple tests and optional stopping break the 5% guarantee; corrections and fixed protocols restore it.
  • When assumptions are shaky, resample the data itself: bootstraps and permutation tests — 1.5 Resampling intuition.
