13.3 Training Pipeline

13.2 built the architecture; 13.1 the objective. This page is the process that turns both into a capable model: pretraining on a massive corpus by next-token prediction, then alignment (SFT → RLHF/DPO). The concepts are the same training loop you already know from 10.4 — what changes is scale, data, and the alignment stage. We run the whole loop live at toy scale, including the beautiful fact that a neural bigram trained by gradient descent converges to the counting estimate of 13.1.

Tier 1 — concept page with light compute; every cell below runs live (NumPy only).

1. Basic Concept

Stage 1 — Pretraining. The model (randomly initialized transformer, 13.2) is trained on next-token prediction over a huge, lightly filtered corpus — trillions of tokens from web pages, books, code. The loss is cross-entropy, which by A.2’s logic is exactly maximum likelihood: the model learns the conditional distribution of text. Everything the model “knows” is statistical structure extracted from this corpus — facts, style, reasoning patterns, and the corpus’s biases.

Stage 2 — Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Pretrained models are bad at following instructions — they continue text, they don’t answer questions. SFT trains on hundreds of thousands of human-written (instruction, response) pairs, same next-token loss, now on the response tokens only. This teaches the format of helpful behavior.

Stage 3 — Alignment (RLHF / DPO). SFT alone produces a model that imitates response style; alignment shapes preferences. RLHF: collect human comparisons of responses, train a reward model, then optimize the policy against it with PPO. DPO: a simpler, stable reformulation that skips the explicit reward model — directly optimizing the policy on preference pairs. Both answer “helpful, honest, harmless” at a level SFT can’t reach from demonstrations alone.

What’s new relative to 10.4’s loop: the loop itself (forward → loss → backward → update) is identical. The engineering that scale demands:

  • Distributed training — data parallelism (each GPU a batch shard), tensor parallelism (split each matmul across GPUs), pipeline parallelism (split layers); a 70B model doesn’t fit on one GPU.
  • Mixed precision — float16/bfloat16 compute with float32 master weights (10.1’s dtype lesson, now load-bearing at scale).
  • Learning-rate schedule — warmup then cosine decay; the single most sensitive hyperparameter.
  • Data curation — deduplication, quality filtering, domain mixing, and contamination control (removing benchmark data from training — 13.7).

Worked example — the entire training loop at toy scale: a neural bigram (one linear layer + softmax) trained by full-batch gradient descent:

The loop is 10.4’s loop: forward, loss, analytic gradient, update. The only LLM-specific piece is the embedding lookup — W[ids[:-1]] gathers rows, so each token’s “embedding” is just its row of \(W\), and the gradient scatters back with np.add.at. A transformer replaces this lookup with the full attention stack; the training mathematics is unchanged.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why is pretraining loss (next-token cross-entropy) a reasonable proxy for “knowledge,” and where does the proxy break down? Connect to 13.1’s compression argument.
  2. SFT teaches format, RLHF/DPO teaches preferences. Why can’t SFT alone produce alignment — what does a preference signal contain that a demonstration doesn’t?
  3. The toy model’s loss decreased from ~2.4 to ~1.9. What is the floor for this loss, and why can’t gradient descent reach zero? (Hint: what does the data itself determine?)
  4. Why does the learning-rate schedule need warmup? What happens to a randomly initialized transformer’s gradients in the first steps with a large constant LR?
  5. Data deduplication: why does training on the same text 10 times hurt, and why is near-duplicate detection (not exact matching) the hard version of the problem?
Answers
  1. Next-token prediction forces the model to compress the corpus’s regularities — facts and reasoning patterns are regularities, so a good predictor must encode them (Shannon: optimal codelength = −log P). The proxy breaks where prediction is easy without understanding (surface statistics, memorized benchmark answers) or where the corpus is wrong/biased — the model learns the data’s errors as faithfully as its facts.
  2. A demonstration shows one acceptable response; a preference pair shows which of two responses is better — a relative signal about quality, tone, safety, and honesty that demonstrations can’t express (the demonstrator only shows their best attempt, never the trade-offs). Preference data is also cheaper at the margin: comparing is easier than composing.
  3. The floor is the entropy of the data itself — the irreducible uncertainty of the next token given the context. The bigram data has genuine ambiguity (after “the”, many words are plausible), so the conditional entropy is positive; zero loss would require the data to be deterministic. This is 1.4’s irreducible error, in language-model form.
  4. At initialization, gradients are large and poorly conditioned — the first steps with a big LR can blow up the activations (loss spikes to NaN). Warmup ramps the LR from ~0 over the first few thousand steps so the model settles into a good region before full-speed training; the cosine decay then anneals it for the final polish.
  5. Repeated text is overweighted — the model spends capacity memorizing duplicates and the effective dataset shrinks (the same data point isn’t new information; it’s 4.3’s leakage in training-set form). Exact dedup is a hash lookup; near-duplicates (a paragraph shared across 10,000 web pages with different boilerplate) need fuzzy matching — MinHash/similarity embeddings — which is why dedup is a research problem, not a one-liner.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. The neural bigram converges to the counting estimate: after training the Section 1 model, extract the model’s implied conditional probabilities \(P_\theta(\text{next} \mid \text{cur}) = \text{softmax}(W)\) and compare them to the 13.1 counting estimate \(\text{count}(w_t, w_{t+1}) / \text{count}(w_t)\) for every (context, next) pair. Report the maximum absolute difference. Then explain why they must agree — connect to A.2: cross-entropy MLE on one-hot inputs is the empirical conditional distribution.

Solution
They agree to optimization error. Why: with one-hot inputs, the model’s conditional is \(\text{softmax}(W_{w_t})\), and cross-entropy MLE for a linear model on one-hot features has the closed form “the empirical conditional distribution” — exactly the counting estimate (this is the multinomial-logit version of A.2: MLE of a linear-Gaussian is the least-squares solution; MLE of a linear-multinomial is the empirical frequencies). Gradient descent finds it because the loss is convex in \(W\). The deep-LLM takeaway: pretraining is the same MLE, just with a nonlinear feature extractor (the transformer) in front of the softmax — the counting model is the degenerate zero-layer case.

Exercise 2. Learning-rate schedule, measured: rerun the Section 1 training with (a) constant LR = 50, (b) constant LR = 0.01, (c) warmup-then-decay (linear warmup over 20 epochs to 5, then cosine decay to 0.5). Plot all three loss curves. Diagnose what (a) does (divergence/oscillation) and what (b) does (slow convergence), and state why the schedule matters more at real scale.

Solution
  1. LR = 50 oscillates and can diverge — steps overshoot the minimum, the loss bounces (at real scale: loss spikes that kill the run, requiring rollback to an earlier checkpoint). (b) LR = 0.01 crawls — 200 epochs isn’t enough; at real scale this is a wasted training run (compute is the budget). (c) Warmup+cosine converges fast and settles lowest. The schedule matters more at real scale because the loss surface is far more ill-conditioned (billions of parameters, sharp minima) and a single bad step costs hours of GPU time — the toy model’s quadratic bowl is forgiving, the real one is not.

Exercise 3. Contamination control, quantified: simulate a training set of 10,000 documents where 200 are exact copies of a test document. Train the Section 1 bigram model on (a) the contaminated set, (b) the deduplicated set, and compare the test document’s perplexity under both. Then state why exact-dedup checking is necessary but not sufficient, and what the standard mitigation is.

Solution
The contaminated model has seen the test document 200 times — its perplexity on it is artificially low (near the document’s own entropy), while the deduped model pays the true price of generalization. Exact-hash dedup catches this case; the insufficient part is near-duplicates (paraphrases, benchmark questions embedded in web pages) and membership leakage (the model regurgitating training text). Standard mitigation: (i) exact + fuzzy dedup at ingestion, (ii) contamination scanning of benchmarks (n-gram overlap with training data), (iii) holding out a clean test set and reporting both contaminated and decontaminated scores — the discipline of 13.7.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Pretraining is 99%+ of the compute; alignment is where behavior comes from. A common misconception is that RLHF “adds intelligence” — it shapes a capability the pretrained model already has. No amount of alignment fixes a capability the pretraining data didn’t support.
  • Catastrophic forgetting is real: fine-tuning on a narrow domain can degrade general ability — the model overwrites pretrained knowledge. Mitigations: mix pretraining data into fine-tuning, lower LR, LoRA (13.6) to freeze most weights.
  • Reward hacking: RLHF optimizes the reward model, not human preference — the policy learns to exploit the reward model’s errors (verbose, sycophantic, weirdly formatted outputs that score high). DPO reduces but doesn’t eliminate this.
  • The data is the model. Architecture differences between GPT-4-class models are second-order compared to data quality and curation. “Garbage in, gospel out” — the model reproduces the corpus’s biases with fluency.
  • Scaling laws are empirical power laws: loss ≈ f(parameters, data, compute) with predictable exponents — which is why the field can plan training runs, and why “just scale it” was a strategy, not a slogan. The flip side: the laws are fit to observed regimes and can break (the “scaling law debate” of 2023–2024).
  • Checkpoints are the unit of recovery: long runs save state every N steps; a divergence means rolling back, not restarting. The schedule, the seed, and the data order are all part of the “model” — reproducibility requires all three.

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated training step has three bugs. Diagnose before running the fixed version.

# AI-generated: one pretraining step (will not run here — spot the bugs by reading)
for step in range(1000):
    x, y = next_batch()
    logits = model(x)                      # (B, T, V)
    loss = cross_entropy(logits, y)        # BUG 1
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()                       # BUG 2
    scheduler.step()
    running_loss = running_loss + loss     # BUG 3
What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Wrong loss reduction: cross_entropy(logits, y) on (B, T, V) logits must flatten to (B*T, V) and average over response tokens only in SFT — averaging over prompt tokens too teaches the model to predict the user’s question, wasting capacity and corrupting the objective. (In pretraining all tokens count; in SFT the mask is the point.)
  2. No gradient zeroing: optimizer.step() without optimizer.zero_grad() first — gradients accumulate across steps (10.1’s accumulation-is-the-default trap), so step 1000 applies the sum of 1000 gradients. The loss curve looks like a random walk.
  3. Graph-carrying accumulator: running_loss + loss keeps the autograd graph alive every step — memory grows until OOM. Use running_loss += loss.item().
# fixed
for step in range(1000):
    x, y, mask = next_batch()
    optimizer.zero_grad()                          # fix 2
    logits = model(x)
    loss = cross_entropy(logits[mask], y[mask])    # fix 1: response tokens only
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    scheduler.step()
    running_loss += loss.item()                    # fix 3

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Pretraining as maximum likelihood, formally: write the pretraining objective for a corpus of sequences, show it decomposes into a sum of next-token cross-entropies, and state the two ways it differs from the A.2 Gaussian story (the noise model, and what “least squares” becomes). Then explain why the same objective, at trillion-token scale, produces emergent capabilities — and what the theoretical objection is.

Hint A.2: Gaussian noise → squared loss. Here: categorical noise → cross-entropy. The objective’s form is unchanged; the output distribution is not.
Solution Objective: \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{(w_{1:T}) \in \text{corpus}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P_\theta(w_t \mid w_{<t})\) — a sum of categorical cross-entropies, one per token. Differences from A.2: (i) the noise model is categorical (multinomial), not Gaussian — the “least squares” analog is cross-entropy, and the MLE is the empirical conditional distribution (Exercise 1’s result, at scale); (ii) the features are learned (the transformer’s hidden states), not fixed — so the model class is a universal approximator conditioned on context, not a linear map. Emergence: the objective is fixed, but the functions the model can represent grow with parameters and data — capabilities like multi-step reasoning are not separate objectives, they’re what a sufficiently good next-token predictor computes internally. Theoretical objection: next-token prediction is a surrogate — nothing in the objective requires truth, only predictability — so the correlation between low loss and genuine reasoning is empirical (scaling laws), not guaranteed; a model can be a perfect predictor of human text and still fail at tasks whose answers humans don’t write down.

Q2. Design the data mix: you are pretraining a 7B model with a 1.4T-token budget. Given the domains below with rough “quality per token” scores (web 1×, books 3×, code 2×, academic 2.5×, Wikipedia 2×), and the constraint that no domain can exceed 60% of the mix, propose a mix and defend it with (a) the quality-weighted objective, (b) the risk of domain collapse (over-weighting high-quality domains starves the model of the long tail of language it must handle), and (c) the repeat strategy (high-quality domains are exhausted — how many epochs is too many?).

Hint This is an optimization with a constraint and a judgment call — there is no closed form, and the answer should cite the objective, the collapse risk, and the epoch budget.
Solution Sketch: web 50% (the long tail — the model must handle all language, and only web has the volume), books 15%, code 10%, academic 10%, Wikipedia 5%, other 10%. (a) Quality-weighted: the mix’s average quality-per-token is ≈ 0.5·1 + 0.15·3 + 0.1·2 + 0.1·2.5 + 0.05·2 + 0.1·1 ≈ 1.45 — a pure-quality play (all books) would score 3.0 but collapse on the long tail. (b) Collapse risk: language is heavy-tailed — rare domains (medical, legal, non-English, dialect) are individually small but collectively most of what the model will be asked; starving them produces a model that’s fluent on average and broken at the edges, which is exactly where users notice. (c) Epochs: books+academic+Wikipedia ≈ 30% of the mix but are high-quality — repeat them 2–3× (they’re exhausted, so repeats buy quality exposure at the Exercise-3 memorization cost); web gets ~1 epoch (it’s big enough to be nearly single-pass). The real field’s answer (Llama, Mistral mixes) looks like this: web-dominated, quality-upsampled, total 1–2 epochs of the effective mix. The general principle: optimize quality-weighted tokens subject to coverage constraints — the same bias–variance logic as 4.4, with domains as the axes.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (training × 10.4 backprop × A.2 MLE). The Section 1 toy model’s gradient (g = p; g[...] -= 1) is the softmax-cross-entropy gradient — derive it from scratch (softmax Jacobian, one-hot target) and verify it against finite differences on a 3×3 \(W\). Then state the general pattern: for any GLM with a canonical link, the gradient of the negative log-likelihood is (prediction − target) × features — the same form as A.3’s logistic gradient. This is why LLM training “just works” with generic autodiff: the loss surface is as well-behaved as logistic regression’s.

C2 (training × 12.2 multiple testing × 13.7). The field’s evaluation problem is 12.2 at industrial scale: every lab evaluates on overlapping benchmarks, selects the best checkpoint/prompt/mix, and reports the winner. Write the critique in the language of Part 12 — selection-as-inference, the winner’s curse, and the pre-registration fix — and state why the training-data version of the problem (contamination, Exercise 3) is the harder one to fix, because the training corpus is a moving target.

7. Summary

  • Pretraining = next-token maximum likelihood on a massive corpus; SFT teaches instruction format; RLHF/DPO aligns preferences — three stages, one objective family.
  • The training loop is 10.4’s loop; scale adds distributed training, mixed precision, and the LR schedule (warmup + cosine).
  • A neural bigram trained by GD converges to the counting estimate — cross-entropy MLE on one-hot inputs is the empirical conditional distribution (the multinomial version of A.2).
  • The loss floor is the data’s own entropy; zero loss means deterministic data, not a perfect model.
  • Data curation (dedup, quality, contamination control) matters more than architecture at the margin; the model learns the corpus’s biases as fluently as its facts.
  • Alignment shapes behavior but can’t create capability; reward hacking is the alignment-stage failure mode.
