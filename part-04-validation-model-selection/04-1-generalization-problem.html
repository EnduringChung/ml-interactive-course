4.1 The Generalization Problem

Training error is a promise the model makes to itself; test error is the promise it keeps to you. The gap between them — optimism — grows with model flexibility and shrinks with data. This page makes the gap visible, measurable, and predictable: the organizing problem of statistical learning.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Three error quantities, precisely:

  • Training (in-sample) error: average loss on the data the model was fitted to. Always optimistic — the fit adapted to this data’s noise.
  • Test (out-of-sample) error: average loss on fresh data from the same process. The honest quantity — what deployment will see.
  • Generalization gap (optimism): test − training. Grows with flexibility (more parameters, smaller k, deeper trees…), shrinks with n.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why is training error guaranteed non-increasing in polynomial degree, while test error is not?
  2. State the direction of each effect on optimism: doubling the number of fitted parameters; doubling n; increasing noise σ.
  3. Your colleague reports train MSE 0.02, test MSE 0.21, σ² ≈ 0.09. Diagnose the model in one sentence and name the two standard remedies.
  4. KNN’s k and polynomial degree are both flexibility dials — which direction is “more flexible” in each, and why are their U-curves mirror images?
  5. A model with training error above the noise floor tells you what, immediately?
Answers
  1. Least squares projects y onto a growing column space: each added polynomial term can only reduce RSS (2.8 Q1). Test error pays for each parameter twice — once through variance in estimating it — so beyond the true structure it climbs.
  2. More parameters → more optimism; more n → less (each parameter is better pinned, and the fit has less room to chase noise); larger σ → more optimism in absolute terms (more noise available to chase) while the noise floor itself also rises.
  3. Overfitting: the gap (0.19) is ≫ the distance to the floor (0.12) — the model has high variance. Remedies: reduce flexibility (lower degree, larger k, regularization) or gather more data; verify by CV either way.
  4. Polynomials: flexibility ↑ with degree (more terms). KNN: flexibility ↑ as k ↓ (locality). So the KNN U-curve is traversed right-to-left relative to the polynomial’s — mirror images of the same phenomenon.
  5. Underfitting (bias-dominated): the model can’t even reach the noise floor, so no amount of validation care will help — increase flexibility before optimizing anything else. It’s the cheap, immediate diagnostic people forget.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. The optimism experiment, quantified: for degrees 1–10, repeat 200 times (fresh n = 30 training sets, fixed large test set), and record the mean training MSE, mean test MSE, and the mean gap. Plot all three vs degree. Verify the two qualitative laws: training error falls in degree, and the gap grows in degree; then check quantitatively how much of the test curve’s left side is bias (mean prediction ≠ truth) vs variance (prediction wiggles across training sets).

Solution
Degree 1: bias² dominates (a line can’t trace a sine). Degree 9: variance dominates (curves swing wildly between training sets). The decomposition is 4.4 previewed with your own data.

Exercise 2. Data-volume rescue: fix the degree-9 overfitting disaster by scaling n (30, 100, 300, 1000, 3000). Report test MSE at each n. Then replot the degree-1-vs-9 test comparison across n. Write the two-sentence law: when does flexibility stop hurting, and what never stops hurting?

Solution
Degree 9’s test error falls toward the degree-1 level (and past it, eventually) as n grows — variance is curable with data, since each coefficient gets pinned by more points. Degree 1’s error never improves past its bias floor — no amount of data fixes a wrong model shape. Flexibility’s danger is finite; underfitting’s is permanent.

Exercise 3. p ≫ n theater: n = 25 samples, 200 pure-noise features, pure-noise labels. Fit (a) plain OLS, (b) ridge with α = 10 (Part 5 preview — Ridge(alpha=10)), (c) KNN with k = 10. Report train and test R²/MSE for each. Which method’s training behavior is the most honest? Connect each to a bias/variance profile.

Solution
Read the middle rows carefully — this is where the intuition usually breaks. OLS interpolates (train 0.000, test 1.168), KNN can’t (train 0.648, test 1.106), and it is tempting to conclude that ridge is “honest by constraint.” It is not. Ridge(1) and Ridge(10) are just as optimistic as OLS on the training number (0.000 and 0.002) because at p = 200 ≫ n = 25 the penalty is far too weak: Ridge(10) still has an effective df of 22.7 out of 25, so it nearly interpolates. Only Ridge(100) (train 0.089, test 1.105) actually collapses toward the null model, and it needed an α tuned to the problem. Shrinkage is not a guarantee of honesty — it is a dial, and an untuned dial can sit anywhere (5.2). The training number only fools those who assume a regularized model must be a modest one.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Optimism is predictable in size: for linear models, in-sample optimism ≈ (2σ²/n)·(number of parameters) — the engine behind Cp/AIC/BIC and adjusted R². It’s why 2.4’s noise columns added ≈ (k/n) worth of fake R².
  • The test set is a budget: every look spends it (0.5); adaptive “tune on test, report test” can make a noise-only pipeline score near-perfectly (try it once, to be cured forever — 4.2).
  • Selection inflates: choosing the best of M models by its CV score reports an extreme order statistic — the winner’s CV score is biased up by roughly (SD of the noise) × E[max of M standard normals]. More candidates = more inflation (4.6).
  • Modern caveat — double descent: beyond the interpolation threshold (p ≳ n), test error can fall again for some overparameterized models (the deep-learning regime). The classical U-curve is the right mental model for classical models; hold it loosely at the extremes.
  • Data leakage resurrects optimism invisibly: preprocessing fit before splitting, duplicates across splits, features derived from the outcome — every one shrinks the apparent gap while worsening the real one. The gap being small is good news only if the pipeline earned it.
  • In-sample diagnostics ≠ out-of-sample validity: residual plots (2.10) certify the fit’s assumptions; only held-out data certifies predictions.

What’s wrong, and the fix

  2. Selected-on-test then reported-on-test: the number is now a selection number, not an estimate. Report on data untouched by selection.
  3. Split-shopping: with enough tries, some split flatters any model; keeping the flattering one is p-hacking with a random seed (1.4’s peeking loop in evaluation clothing).

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. For a linear model with n training points and p parameters, the expected optimism of the training MSE is approximately \(\frac{2\sigma^2}{n} p\). Verify this by simulation (σ = 1, p ∈ {1, 5, 20}, n = 100, truth = zero signal or mild signal), then use it to explain (a) why Cp/AIC-style criteria subtract \(2p\hat\sigma^2/n\) from training error, and (b) why the approximation degrades when p approaches n.

Hint Optimism = E[test error] − E[train error] for the fitted rule; for linear smoothers with hat matrix H, trace-based arguments give the 2σ²p/n form (Mallows’ Cp derivation).
Solution
Agreement is close while p ≪ n. As p → n, the fit interpolates: training MSE → 0, the hat matrix saturates (H → I, df → n), and the linear approximation’s assumptions (moderate leverage) break — optimism explodes non-linearly rather than tracking 2pσ²/n. The formula is a small-p law; the U-curve is the whole truth.

Q2. A pipeline reports “CV error 0.31” as the performance of the selected model, where selection was: 12 candidate models evaluated by the same 5-fold CV, winner = smallest. Design and run the experiment measuring the selection inflation: simulate null data (no signal) where every model’s true error equals σ², run the selection, record the winner’s CV score; compare its average to σ² and to the average of a fixed model’s CV score. Then derive the practical rule (report the winner’s score from a fresh outer CV, or correct by the expected order statistic).

Solution
Even with zero signal, the winner of 12 candidates reports ≈ 0.8–0.9 — selection alone manufactures a fake improvement over the fixed model’s ≈ 1.0. This gap is pure selection inflation and it grows with the candidate count and CV noise. Rules: (1) estimate the selected pipeline’s performance by wrapping selection itself inside an outer CV (nested — 4.6); (2) or subtract the expected order-statistic bonus (knowable by simulation under the null — exactly what you just built).

6. Complex Questions

C1 (generalization × 2.9/3.5 KNN). Build the full 2-D map: for n ∈ {25, 100, 400} and k ∈ {1, 3, 10, 30, 100}, record train and test MSE (regression) or error (classification) on a fixed truth. Produce a heatmap of test error over (n, k) and draw the ridge of optimal k. How does optimal k scale with n, and what does the map predict about KNN’s viability when you can only collect 25 more samples?

C2 (generalization × 3.7 comparison). Six classifiers, one dataset, twenty replications: record each model’s CV accuracy per replication, then compute (a) each model’s mean, (b) the winner-per-replication’s average score, (c) the fixed pre-registered model’s average. Quantify (b) − (c) as measured selection inflation in a non-null setting, and check whether it’s larger or smaller than your null-data estimate from Q2 — then explain the direction.

7. Summary

  • Training error is an optimistic estimator; the optimism grows with flexibility and shrinks with n.
  • The U-curve (test error vs flexibility) is the field’s central picture: underfit left, overfit right, noise floor below.
  • Flexibility dials point opposite ways (degree ↑, k ↓) but the physics is one: bias² + variance + σ².
  • Selection among many models inflates the winner’s score — order statistics, not skill.
  • Test sets are budgets; CV is the renewable subscription (next pages); leakage is counterfeit currency.
  • Underfitting (train error above σ²) is permanent without more flexibility; overfitting is curable with data or constraints.
  • The first remedy for optimism is the simplest split of all: 4.2 The validation set approach.
