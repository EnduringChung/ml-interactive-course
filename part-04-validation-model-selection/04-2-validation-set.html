4.2 The Validation Set Approach

The simplest honest estimator: hold out a chunk of data, fit on the rest, evaluate once on the holdout. It is the prototype of every validation scheme — and its weaknesses (variance, wasted data, single-number confidence) are exactly the weaknesses the rest of Part 4 exists to fix.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Protocol: split rows into training and validation sets (say 70/30). Fit the model on training. Compute the metric once on validation. That number estimates out-of-sample error.

Second, bias from smaller training: validation-error estimates are pessimistic because the model is trained on only 70% of the data — deployment would use 100%:

2. Concept Questions

  1. Your validation MSE is 0.31 on one 70/30 split. What is the (approximate) standard error of that single number if σ² ≈ 0.09 and n_validation = 60 — and what does it say about reporting “0.31” to three decimals?
  2. Why is validation error said to overestimate the error of the final model refit on all data?
  3. Which two competing pressures determine the validation fraction, and what happens at the extremes (10% vs 50% holdout)?
  4. You use the validation set to pick among 15 models, then report the winner’s validation MSE. Which error from 4.1 have you committed, and what’s the minimal fix?
  5. For a time-ordered dataset, what must change in the protocol, and why is a random split actively wrong?
Answers
  1. SE(MSE) ≈ σ²·√(2/n_val) ≈ 0.09 × √(2/60) ≈ 0.0164. So 0.31 ± 0.033 (95%): the third decimal is noise; report 0.31 ± 0.02 or the CI — or average over splits (4.3).
  2. The validation model trained on 70% of data; with less data its error is higher than the refit-on-100% model’s (4.1’s data-rescue law). The estimator is pessimistically biased for the deployment model.
  3. Big validation → precise estimate but badly undertrained model (pessimistic, wasteful); small validation → near-deployment training but noisy estimate. 20–30% is the customary compromise; CV removes the trade-off.
  4. Selection on the validation set converts it into a second training set — the winner’s number is an order statistic, inflated. Minimal fix: a third, untouched test split; the full fix is nested CV (4.6).
  5. Time-ordered data needs forward chaining: train on the past, validate on the future (random splits let the model train on the future — temporal leakage). Metrics then answer “how well do we predict tomorrow,” the only deployment-honest question.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Validation-vs-truth calibration: for a fixed data-generating process, compute (a) the true test MSE of a model trained on 70% of n = 150 (evaluated on 100k fresh points), and (b) the distribution of single-split validation estimates over 300 seeds. Report the mean of (b) vs (a), the SD of (b), and the fraction of 95% intervals (estimate ± 1.96·SE, SE from validation residuals) that contain (a). Is the validation estimator centered where it claims?

Solution
The per-split mean matches the truth (the estimator is centered for the 70%-trained model); within-split SEs describe split-to-split scatter well (coverage ≈ 0.93–0.95). The two failure modes to remember: it’s centered for the smaller training set (pessimism for deployment), and single-split numbers carry ±SE-sized error bars people rarely print.

Exercise 2. The learning-curve use case: for n_train ∈ {15, 30, 60, 120} (from a pool of 600), estimate test MSE by validation on a fixed 200-row holdout, replicating each size 100 times. Plot mean ± SD vs n_train. Identify where the curve flattens (“more data stops helping”) and connect to 4.1 Exercise 2’s law — what does the flatten point say about how much more data to collect?

Solution
The curve falls steeply then flattens: past the elbow, additional samples buy little — the residual error is dominated by noise floor + bias, not estimation variance. The flatten point is the empirical answer to “how much data is enough,” and it shifts left or right with model flexibility (simpler models flatten earlier).

Exercise 3. Validation-set selection inflation, in miniature: 20 candidate polynomial degrees (1–20) evaluated on ONE validation split; record the winner’s validation MSE. Repeat over 300 datasets; report the mean winner-vs-fixed-degree-3 validation ratio under a sinusoidal truth where degree 3 is genuinely near-optimal. Compare with selecting by 5-fold CV instead (averaged within each dataset). How much does honest selection reduce the inflation?

Solution
CV-based selection picks degrees closer to optimal and its winners’ true errors are lower — the single validation split’s noisier estimates select flashy degrees (selected-on-noise). The deeper lesson though: in both cases the winner’s own selection score must not be reported as performance; the numbers here are TRUE errors of refit winners, which is why they’re comparable at all.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • One number, no error bar — the validation estimate’s SE is computable from the validation residuals (drill 4) and should be printed; most published single-split numbers would fail that audit.
  • The holdout is a budget too: every model evaluated on it spends it (0.5); “validation” that saw 15 models is a training set wearing a costume.
  • Pessimism by undertraining — correction strategies exist (e.g., learning-curve extrapolation), but the clean fix is CV, which refits on (K−1)/K of data per fold.
  • Stratification for classification, grouping for clustered data, temporal order for time series — the split’s design is part of the estimator (0.5).
  • Post-selection refit: after choosing on validation, refit on ALL data before deployment — but then the validation number describes the pre-refit model, not the deployed one; only CV/nested schemes price the full pipeline.
  • Validation on a different distribution than deployment (site shift, prevalence shift) measures the wrong thing — the split cannot fix a sampling problem (3.7 C2).

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated pipeline has three bugs. Run, diagnose, fix.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Pre-split scaling leaks validation statistics into training inputs (0.5). Scale inside the training fold only.
  2. Selection and report on the same holdout: winner’s score is an order statistic (4.1 Q2). Reserve a third split, or select by CV.
  3. Score transplant: the validation number belongs to the 70%-trained model; the deployed all-data model’s performance was never estimated. Estimate the full pipeline honestly (CV over the entire choose-and-fit procedure).

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the SE of a validation MSE estimate: given validation residuals e_i with mean-zero assumption violations aside, SE = sd(e²)/√(n_val). Compare, by simulation, this plug-in SE to the observed SD of validation MSEs across 500 splits. When do they disagree (name the distributional cause), and what does the disagreement do to naive “± SE” error bars?

Hint e² has a skewed distribution (χ²-like when errors are Gaussian); its mean’s SE uses e²’s own variance — but heavy tails or heteroscedasticity make the plug-in unstable.
Solution
Under Gaussian errors the plug-in SE is unbiased for the across-split SD (they estimate the same sampling variability); they diverge when errors are heavy-tailed (e² ’s variance is dominated by rare huge residuals, so the plug-in is itself noisy — sometimes 2× off) or heteroscedastic (validation mix shifts). Consequence: treat “± SE” bars on single-split estimates as approximate, and prefer averaging across splits, which makes the across-split SD directly measurable.

Q2. Your deployment population drifts: the validation set was drawn from distribution P, deployment sees Q (same relationship, different x-mix). Design a simulation where the validation estimate is unbiased for P and biased for Q, quantify the bias as a function of the drift’s magnitude, and derive the reporting rule for validation estimates under anticipated drift (report per-stratum estimates or reweight to Q’s mix — never the pooled number alone).

Solution
The validation number is a perfectly good estimate of error-on-P and a biased estimate of error-on-Q; the bias grows with how much Q’s x-mass sits outside (or disproportionately inside) regions where the model misfits (here: extrapolation region x > 1). Rule: validation estimates are distribution-conditional; under anticipated drift, report stratified/reweighted errors — the pooled MSE is a weighted average with weights (P’s) that deployment doesn’t have. This is covariate shift, and it converts “validation” from a statistic into a design decision.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (validation × 0.5 stratification × 3.1.5 AUPRC). On a 1%-positive dataset with n = 1000, compare unstratified vs stratified 70/30 validation for estimating AUPRC: 200 splits each, report the spread (SD and IQR) of the estimates. Which procedure’s estimate would you publish, and what sample size of positives would make the unstratified version tolerable (verify empirically)?

C2 (validation × 4.3 CV preview). Using the same dataset and model, run the head-to-head: 200 independent single 70/30 splits vs 5-fold CV (repeated 40 times) as estimators of the same quantity. Compare (a) bias (vs a huge fresh test truth), (b) variance across repetitions, (c) total compute (fits per estimate). You should find CV’s variance advantage at 5× the compute — the explicit price list that makes 4.3’s conventions legible.

7. Summary

  • Validation = fit on one part, evaluate once on an untouched part — the prototype of honest estimation.
  • Its estimate is noisy (single split) and pessimistic (smaller training); both defects are measurable and both are CV’s motivation.
  • The validation set is a budget: selection on it converts it to training; keep a third split or use nested CV.
  • Print uncertainty: plug-in SEs from validation residuals, or split-to-split SDs — never a bare 3-decimal number.
  • Split design (stratify, group, temporal order) is part of the estimator, not an implementation detail.
  • Estimates are distribution-conditional: drift between validation and deployment breaks the number, not the math.
  • The fix for both defects — reuse every row for validation while keeping folds clean — is cross-validation: 4.3 Cross-validation.
