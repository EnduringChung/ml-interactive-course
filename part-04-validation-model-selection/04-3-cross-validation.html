4.3 Cross-Validation

Cross-validation (CV) keeps the validation idea but recycles the data: split into K folds, hold each out in turn, average. Every row is validated exactly once and trains K−1 times — more precise estimates from the same data, at K times the compute. It is the default referee of applied machine learning.

1. Basic Concept

K-fold protocol: partition rows into K disjoint folds (shuffled, ideally stratified). For k = 1..K: fit on all folds except k, predict fold k, record the metric. Report the mean (and spread) of the K values.

2. Concept Questions

  1. What exactly does “5-fold CV estimate = 0.31 ± 0.04” estimate, and which model (trained on what fraction of data) does the ± describe?
  2. Why is K-fold CV not K times more precise than a single validation split, despite using K folds?
  3. LOOCV is nearly unbiased for the error of models trained on n−1 points. Name its two practical costs.
  4. You’re comparing two models with fold-wise CV errors. Why must the fold splits be identical across models, and what test formalizes the comparison?
  5. Repeated CV (e.g., 10× 5-fold) — what does repetition add, and what doesn’t it fix?
Answers
  1. It estimates the expected test error of the procedure “fit this model type to 4/5 of data” — i.e., of models trained on 80% of the data. The final deployed model (trained on 100%) will typically be slightly better; the estimate carries that small pessimism (4.2).
  2. The K fold errors share training data (any two training sets overlap in K−2 folds) and evaluate on the same finite dataset — the fold errors are strongly positively correlated, so their mean’s variance doesn’t shrink like independent estimates would (no 1/K). Repetition with fresh shuffles reduces, but never eliminates, this.
  3. Compute (n fits, each on n−1 rows — infeasible for expensive models) and high fold-correlation: the LOOCV mean has low bias but its variance isn’t correspondingly tiny, so per-fold SEs are misleading. Also no stratification granularity at K = n for classification (some folds have 1 positive).
  4. Fold difficulty (which easy/hard rows land together) shifts all models’ scores on a given split — pairing cancels it. With identical folds, compare fold-wise differences with a paired t-test (3.7); with different random splits, you’re comparing two noisy marginals.
  5. Repetition with different shuffles averages out partition luck, tightening the estimate (the SD across repeats estimates the partition-noise). It does NOT fix: selection inflation (picking the winner among many models by CV), leakage, or distribution shift — those are pipeline diseases, not partition noise.

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Variance anatomy: estimate the true test error of a degree-3 model (fit on 80% of n = 120) via (a) one 5-fold CV, (b) 20 repeats of 5-fold CV, (c) 200 independent 80/20 splits. For each, report the estimate and an empirical SD across 300 repetitions of the whole scheme. Which scheme’s repetition-SD is smallest, and what does the comparison say about what CV does and doesn’t recycle?

Solution
All three estimate nearly the same thing and their means agree; the SD ranking (single split worst, one 5-fold in the middle, 20× repeats best) quantifies the partition-noise that CV partially — but not fully — recycles. Diminishing returns set in: 20 repeats ≈ 100 fits for a modest tightening, because folds within a repeat are correlated.

Exercise 2. Model selection by CV, done right and wrong: among degrees 1–12 on sinusoidal data (n = 100), (a) select the degree minimizing 5-fold CV MSE; (b) report that minimum as the performance; (c) also compute the true error of the CV-selected degree (big fresh test). Repeat over 200 datasets: compare the distribution of (b) against (c), and against the true error of the fixed degree-3 model. Quantify the optimism of (b) and the quality of CV as a selector.

Solution
The reported minimum sits below the selected model’s true error — that gap is selection optimism, paid for searching 12 candidates. Meanwhile CV-as-selector works well: the selected degrees’ true errors beat (or nearly match) fixed degree-3, because CV’s noise per candidate is small enough that the true winner usually scores near the top. Two hats, one tool: a good selector, an optimistic reporter — never let it wear both hats at once (4.6 formalizes the fix).

Exercise 3. Grouped biological data done properly: 40 patients × 5 visits (200 rows); the signal is at the patient level. Compare three evaluation schemes for a logistic model: (a) row-level StratifiedKFold(5) — patients split across folds; (b) GroupKFold(5) by patient; (c) GroupKFold with LOO-patients (40 folds). Report each scheme’s mean CV AUC and across-fold SD, then evaluate the winning-by-(a) model’s performance on truly unseen patients. Which scheme’s number would you put in a paper claiming “generalizes to new patients”?

Solution
Scheme (a) lets each patient’s rows appear in both train and validation — the model memorizes patient-level drift and reports inflated AUC (“generalizes” only to new visits of known patients). Schemes (b)/(c) answer the new-patient question and their numbers are visibly lower and more honest. The evaluation unit must match the deployment claim: row-level questions (rare in medicine) vs patient-level questions (the usual).

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • K choice folklore: K = 5 or 10 balance bias (training fraction 1−1/K) against compute and fold-correlation. K = 2 gives the most independent folds; LOOCV the least (and is noisier per-unit-cost for unstable learners).
  • Preprocessing belongs inside the folds — Pipeline + cross_val_score is the mechanical guarantee (0.5); a scaler fit before cross_val_score silently invalidates the whole table.
  • Fold-level SEs understate uncertainty (correlated folds); for error bars on the CV mean, use repeated CV across random partitions or bootstrap over rows — not sd(fold scores)/√K alone.
  • CV estimates procedures, not fitted models — after CV, refit on all data for deployment; the CV number is the performance claim, the full-data fit is the artifact (4.2 post-selection note).
  • Group/cluster leakage is the silent killer of biomarker papers: same patient, same batch, same unit across folds (0.5 bug #3, Exercise 3).
  • Time series: shuffle-free TimeSeriesSplit or rolling-origin schemes; random K-fold CV on temporal data answers a question nobody deploys against.
  • Feature selection inside CV only: screening features on the full dataset before CV is leakage — the fold must not know which features exist until it trains (2.8 Exercise 3).

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Pre-CV scaling: every fold’s training statistics include validation rows. Pipeline inside CV.
  2. Unshuffled folds on sorted data: some folds see one class only; several folds can’t even fit. Shuffle (or stratify).
  3. Feature selection outside CV: the folds’ validation rows voted on which features matter — leakage. Selection must live inside the fold pipeline.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Estimate the fold-correlation of 5-fold CV errors empirically: generate 400 datasets; for each, compute the 5 fold MSEs of a fixed model; extract the average pairwise correlation of fold errors. Use the observed ρ to explain why the SE of the CV mean is √((1 + 4ρ)/5)·SD(fold)/√5-ish rather than SD(fold)/√5, and compute the inflation factor at your measured ρ.

Hint For the mean of K equicorrelated estimates, Var(mean) = (σ²/K)(1 + (K−1)ρ).
Solution

Measure the correlation across datasets: standardize each dataset’s fold errors, pool, and correlate fold positions.

Typical ρ lands well above 0 — folds share rows and the same underlying fit. The naive SD/√K understates the CV mean’s uncertainty by the inflation factor; repeated-CV or bootstrap-over-rows error bars are the honest alternative (Q2 of 4.2 for the single-split analogue).

Q2. You have n = 80 rows, an unstable learner (KNN k = 1), and must choose between LOOCV and 5-fold repeated 10 times as your error estimator. Simulate the truth (fresh test sets) and compare both estimators’ bias and variance across 200 datasets. Confirm or refute the folklore “LOOCV has low bias but high variance for unstable learners” and state which you’d use for (a) unstable learners, (b) stable learners (linear regression).

Solution
For the unstable k = 1 learner, LOOCV trains on n−1 ≈ n (low bias) but its per-fold predictions are maximally jumpy and folds nearly identical — high variance across datasets. Repeated 5-fold trades a hair of bias (training on 80%) for substantially lower variance — better for unstable learners, matching the folklore. For stable learners (linear regression), the variance difference largely evaporates and LOOCV’s minimal bias is attractive (compute permitting). The estimator must be matched to the learner’s stability — one more decision CV forces you to own.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (CV × 3.7 comparison × 12.x multiplicity preview). Ten models, five-fold CV, pairwise McNemar on pooled out-of-fold predictions = 45 comparisons. Simulate null data (no signal) and count how many pairs come out “significant” at p < 0.05. Apply Bonferroni (α/45) and re-count. Connect the result to why model-comparison tables in the wild overclaim — and write the protocol sentence your future papers will include.

C2 (CV × 2.4 R²). Out-of-sample R² from K-fold CV: implement fold-wise \(R^2 = 1 - \text{MSE}_{fold}/\text{Var}_{fold}(y)\) vs pooled \(1 - \sum \text{resid}^2 / \sum (y - \bar{y}_{pooled})^2\) on the same CV predictions. Show the two can differ noticeably on small folds, decide which is the defensible summary for (a) balanced folds, (b) drifting fold composition, and produce the exact sklearn-equivalent computation (cross_val_predict + r2_score) to check your arithmetic.

7. Summary

  • K-fold CV: every row validated once; report mean fold error ± honest uncertainty (fold correlation!, repeats).
  • Stratify classification folds; group by patient/batch when deployment means new groups; never shuffle time.
  • Preprocessing, feature selection, everything data-dependent lives inside the fold pipeline.
  • CV is a fine selector and an optimistic reporter of its own minimum — separation of hats is [4.6]’s law.
  • LOOCV: low bias, high compute, high estimator variance for unstable learners; 5/10-fold is the working default.
  • Fold-level SEs understate; repeat partitions or bootstrap rows for error bars.
  • The U-curve of 4.1 has a decomposition — bias² + variance + σ² — that explains why the minimum sits where it does: 4.4 The bias–variance trade-off.
