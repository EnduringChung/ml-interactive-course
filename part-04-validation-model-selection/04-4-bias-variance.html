4.4 The Bias–Variance Trade-off

Every model’s expected error on a new point decomposes into three parts: bias² (systematic wrongness), variance (sensitivity to the training sample), and irreducible noise. The U-curve of 4.1 is this decomposition plotted against flexibility. Here you derive it, then watch it move under your own simulations.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

At a fixed query point \(x_0\), with training set \(T\) treated as random:

\[\mathbb{E}_{T}\big[(y_0 - \hat{f}_T(x_0))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(f(x_0) - \mathbb{E}_T[\hat{f}_T(x_0)]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}_T\big(\hat{f}_T(x_0)\big)}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\varepsilon)}_{\sigma^2}\]

Derivation (one completing-the-square trick): write \(\hat f_T(x_0) - y_0 = (\hat f_T - \mathbb{E}\hat f) + (\mathbb{E}\hat f - f) - \varepsilon\). The cross-terms vanish because ε is independent of T and mean-zero, and \(\mathbb{E}_T[\hat f - \mathbb{E}\hat f] = 0\). Averaged over \(x_0\): total test MSE = bias² + variance + σ².

2. Concept Questions

  1. Where does the irreducible σ² term come from in the formula, and why can no model remove it?
  2. KNN: as k → 1 and as k → n, state the bias/variance limits at a fixed x₀.
  3. Your colleague says “the bias–variance trade-off means you should always pick the middle.” Give a dataset property that pushes the optimum to the flexible end, and one that pushes it rigid.
  4. Why does more training data decrease variance but (mostly) leave bias unchanged — and which classical model is the exception where data shrinks bias too?
  5. Bootstrapped bagged trees average many trees trained on resamples. In decomposition language, what does the averaging buy, and what doesn’t it fix? (Preview of Part 7.)
Answers
  1. ε enters the prediction target \(y_0 = f(x_0) + \varepsilon\); since ε is independent of the training set, its variance adds in full. No function of the training data can predict ε — it’s the noise floor of 2.1.
  2. k = 1: \(\hat f(x_0) = y_{\text{nearest}} = f(x_{\text{near}}) + \varepsilon_{\text{near}}\) — bias ≈ f(x_near) − f(x₀) ≈ 0 for dense data (low bias), variance = Var(ε) plus neighbor-identity jitter (high). k → n: prediction → global mean \(\bar y\) — maximal bias (|f(x₀) − f̄|) and variance → Var(ȳ) ≈ σ²/n → 0.
  3. Flexible-friendly: large n, low noise, complex wiggly f (bias dominates rigid models). Rigid-friendly: small n, high noise, simple f — or high dimension d ≫ n, where variance of flexible models explodes (2.9/3.5 curse).
  4. Variance is estimation noise: more points pin each parameter/local average tighter (∝ 1/n effects). Bias is structural — wrong model class — so extra data from the same process doesn’t help. The exception: KNN and other local methods — more data shrinks neighborhoods, reducing approximation (bias) too; that’s why nonparametric methods converge to truth.
  5. Averaging i.i.d.-ish model fits cuts variance (∝ 1/B for uncorrelated members; less for correlated trees) while leaving each member’s bias intact. Bagging fixes instability, not mis-specification — and boosting (Part 7) attacks bias by sequential fitting instead.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Build bias_variance(model_factory, make_data, x0, reps=1000) returning bias, variance, and total MSE at point x0 for any sklearn-style model. Apply it to KNN with k ∈ {1, 5, 25, 100} on the sinusoid (n = 100, σ = 0.3); produce the k-vs-(bias², variance) table. Verify: total − σ² = bias² + variance within Monte Carlo error, and identify the MSE-minimizing k.

Solution
The table shows the trade exactly: k = 1 has tiny bias, large variance; k = 100 (≈ mean model at n = 100) has huge bias, vanishing variance; the MSE-optimal k sits between. Note bias isn’t monotone in a trivial way at one point — averaging over x (as in Section 1) smooths the picture.

Exercise 2. Noise and flexibility interact: rerun the degree-sweep decomposition (Section 1’s second figure) at σ = 0.05 and σ = 0.6. For each noise level, report the MSE-optimal degree. Write the one-line design rule linking noise to the flexibility you can afford, then connect it to why 2.10’s equal-variance violations matter for model choice and not just inference.

Solution
Low noise → optimal degree near the true complexity (variance is cheap); high noise → optimal degree drops hard (each estimated parameter imports σ² worth of variance). Rule: affordable flexibility scales inversely with noise. Heteroscedasticity (2.10) makes noise region-dependent — so flexible features are locally affordable, one motivation for adaptive smoothers (Part 6) and variance-aware likelihoods (GLMs, 3.6).

Exercise 3. The data-rescue curve, decomposed: fix degree 9 (overparameterized for n = 30) and rerun the decomposition at n ∈ {30, 100, 300, 1000}. Report bias², variance, and total at each n. Which component dies with data, which refuses, and what does the surviving component’s floor equal (compute it analytically for reference)?

Solution
Variance collapses roughly like (number of effective parameters)/n as n grows; bias² barely moves (degree 9 can represent the sinusoid, so its residual bias is small from the start — try degree 2 to see bias refuse to die). The floor is σ² = 0.09, exactly the irreducible term. Data cures variance; only a richer model class cures bias — the two diseases, two medicines.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • The decomposition is for squared loss — classification’s 0-1 loss has bias/variance notions but they’re subtler (multiple decompositions exist; correlations between bias and variance signs). Don’t force the additive story onto accuracy or AUC.
  • Bias and variance need not trade monotonically at every point — the trade is an average statement; at particular x₀, or with correlated estimators, both can move together (and modern double-descent breaks the U entirely beyond interpolation — hold the classical picture for classical models).
  • Ensembles exploit the decomposition, not vibes: bagging attacks variance (averaging correlated fits helps less — trees’ correlation is why random feature subsampling (Part 7) works), boosting attacks bias (sequentially fitting residuals).
  • Regularization (Part 5) is variance purchase with bias currency: ridge shrinks coefficients (bias ↑, variance ↓); the penalty is the exchange rate. Every Part 5 method is a point on this dial.
  • Estimating the decomposition from ONE training set is impossible — bias and variance are properties of the estimator across training sets (4.1 Exercise 1); single-fit diagnostics speak to residuals, not to the decomposition.
  • σ² estimation matters for everything downstream: RSE (2.4), Cp/AIC penalties, and “are we at the floor” verdicts all consume σ̂²; a biased σ̂ (from a mis-specified model) corrupts the whole accounting.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove the decomposition rigorously: define \(\bar{\hat f}(x_0) = \mathbb{E}_T[\hat f_T(x_0)]\), expand \(\mathbb{E}_T[(y_0 - \hat f_T(x_0))^2]\) by adding and subtracting \(\bar{\hat f}(x_0)\), and show both cross-terms vanish — one by independence of ε from T, one by the definition of expectation. Then explain where the proof fails for 0-1 loss (why no clean additive decomposition exists there).

Hint E[(A + B + C)²] with A = ȳ-type zero-mean term, B = bias, C = −ε; check E[A] = 0, E[AC] = E[A]E[C] = 0 by independence.
Solution Let \(A = \bar{\hat f} - \hat f_T\), \(B = \bar{\hat f} - f(x_0)\), \(C = -\varepsilon\). Then \(y_0 - \hat f_T = A + B + C\). Squaring and taking \(\mathbb{E}_T\): E[A²] = Var, B² = bias², E[C²] = σ². Cross-terms: E[AB] = B·E[A] = 0; E[BC] = B·E[C] = 0 (ε mean-zero, independent of everything T-derived); E[AC] = E[A]E[C] = 0 by independence. Sum = bias² + var + σ². For 0-1 loss the indicator \((y_0 \ne \hat f_T)\) doesn’t expand into squares — bias and variance interact (high variance can correct systematic boundary bias — “variance cancels bias” — which squared loss forbids). That’s why classification intuition sometimes rewards instability, and why the clean U-curve is a squared-loss picture.

Q2. Double descent, demonstrated small: with n = 40 points and a linear model in k random Fourier features (X → sin/cos features), sweep k from 5 to 300 and plot train and test MSE. Identify the interpolation threshold (train MSE ≈ 0) and describe test error’s behavior around and beyond it. Then explain in bias/variance language why the classical U-curve story needs extending — and why you should still not casually interpolate your next dataset.

Solution
Around p ≈ n the minimum-norm interpolating fit is unstable — test error peaks (the classical story ends at this wall). Beyond it, the minimum-norm solution among infinitely many interpolants becomes smoother/more benign as p grows, and test error descends again. Bias/variance language extends via “effective capacity” and norm-based complexity rather than parameter count. Practical stance: the descent region requires specific (min-norm/implicit-regularized) solvers and lots of features — for classical tabular work, the U-curve and its remedies (Part 5) remain the operating manual.

7. Summary

  • Test MSE = bias² + variance + σ²; the proof is one completing-the-square; σ² never leaves.
  • Flexibility buys bias down at variance’s price; the U-curve is that exchange plotted.
  • More data kills variance, not bias; local (nonparametric) methods are the exception — neighborhoods shrink.
  • Noise sets the flexibility budget: high σ ⇒ rigid optima (Exercise 2).
  • Bagging/boosting/regularization are the decomposition’s applied children: variance-, bias-, and exchange-machines.
  • The decomposition is a squared-loss, classical-model story — 0-1 loss and interpolation-threshold regimes need amended maps.
  • With error anatomy understood, the bootstrap gives it standard errors without new theory: 4.5 The Bootstrap.
