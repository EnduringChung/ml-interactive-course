4.6 Hyperparameter Tuning

Every model from Part 2–3 has dials you don’t fit from data: polynomial degree, KNN’s k, regularization strength, tree depth. Hyperparameter tuning chooses them by validation — and inherits every trap of 4.1–4.5 in concentrated form, because tuning is selection, and selection is optimism. This page builds the honest tuning machine: grids, pipelines, nested CV, and the reporting discipline that keeps the numbers true.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The separation of hats: hyperparameters are chosen on validation data (inner loop); performance of the whole choose-and-fit procedure is estimated on untouched data (outer loop). Conflating the hats — tuning and reporting on the same data — is the single most common inflated number in applied ML.

The tuning landscape itself — mean CV score per k with its fold spread:

Note the landscape’s flat top: neighboring k values are statistically indistinguishable — the one-standard-error rule (below) exists for exactly this reason.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why is search.best_score_ NOT an unbiased estimate of the tuned model’s performance — describe the mechanism in one sentence.
  2. State the one-standard-error rule and the philosophy behind preferring the simpler model within 1 SE of the best.
  3. Grid vs random search: when does random win, and what property of real hyperparameter landscapes makes it so?
  4. Your pipeline = scaler + feature-selector + model, all tuned. Why must the selector’s parameters also live inside the CV loop?
  5. After nested CV selects and scores the procedure, what model do you deploy, and what number do you quote for its expected performance?
Answers
  1. best_score_ is the maximum over candidates of noisy CV estimates — an order statistic. Even on pure noise it beats the true optimum; its expected value = true performance + selection bonus (measured in 4.1 Q2). Only an outer loop (or fresh test set) prices the selection itself.
  2. Among models whose CV scores lie within 1 SE of the best, choose the most parsimonious (largest k, strongest penalty, fewest features). Rationale: those scores are indistinguishable from the best; the simpler model has less variance, better interpretability, and typically better deployment behavior — you give up nothing measurable and buy robustness.
  3. When the grid is larger than a handful of dimensions or several parameters are irrelevant (low effective dimensionality of the response surface). Landscapes are usually dominated by a few parameters (e.g., k, learning rate) while others barely matter — random points find the important dimensions faster than a lattice that spends equal effort on all.
  4. Because feature selection fits parameters to data (which features look informative): leaving it outside the loop means validation folds were pre-filtered using their own labels — leakage by another name (0.5, 4.3). The fold must contain the entire data-dependent chain.
  5. Deploy the chosen configuration refit on all available data; quote the outer (nested) estimate as the expected performance of the procedure — not the inner best score, not the refit model’s training score.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Two grids, honestly compared: candidate A tunes KNN over k ∈ {1..30}; candidate B tunes k ∈ {1..30} and weights ∈ {uniform, distance} and p ∈ {1, 2} (180 combos). On the same outer 5-fold nested evaluation, compare the two procedures’ nested scores. Does the bigger grid win? By how much — and is the gain inside noise? Then relate the answer to the selection-inflation drill: which procedure pays more selection tax?

Solution
Typical result: grid B edges out A by a fraction of a percent — often within the outer-fold SD. The 6× larger search pays 6× the selection tax on its inner best score; the outer estimate, which prices that tax, shows the true (small) gain. Lesson: bigger grids mostly buy bigger reported inner numbers, not performance — tune what theory says matters, not what the grid can enumerate.

Exercise 2. The one-standard-error rule in action: from Section 1’s GridSearchCV, compute the SE of the best score’s folds, mark the 1-SE band, and list all k whose mean score falls within it. Choose the largest such k and compare its held-out test accuracy to the argmax k’s. Repeat over 30 different random states — how often does the 1-SE model match or beat the argmax on test?

Solution
The 1-SE model matches or beats the argmax most of the time despite (usually) being smoother — its test luck is comparable and it’s the model you’d rather deploy (larger k = stabler). The rule isn’t magic; it’s a tie-breaker that spends a tie on robustness.

Exercise 3. Build nested_compare(procedures, X, y, outer_k=5) where each procedure is an unfitted GridSearchCV — returning each procedure’s outer mean/SD plus a paired comparison of the top two (fold-wise differences, t-test). Demonstrate on (a) KNN with tuned k, (b) logistic with tuned C, (c) LDA (no tuning) on one dataset. Report the ranking, the paired p-value for the leaders, and the honest sentence you’d write about “the best model.”

Solution
Nested comparison answers “which procedure generalizes better,” with pairing across outer folds and a real test — and the usual outcome on modest data is “statistically indistinguishable,” which is a finding, not a failure: it licenses choosing by interpretability, cost, or simplicity (3.7’s ethic, formalized).

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • The tuning metric must be the deployment metric — tuning accuracy to deploy a precision@k system optimizes the wrong thing; set scoring deliberately (roc_auc, average_precision, f1, custom).
  • Grid resolution is a bias-variance choice: coarse grids miss optima (bias), fine grids inflate selection tax and compute. Log-spaced grids for scale parameters (C, α, learning rate) are the efficient default.
  • Search over preprocessing counts too — imputation strategy, scaling flavor, selector’s k are hyperparameters and belong in the grid (inside the pipeline); anything data-dependent outside the loop is leakage.
  • Refit-on-all-data after selection (GridSearchCV does it by default: refit=True) — but the number you report is the outer/nested one, never best_score_.
  • Flat landscapes are informative: if a wide band of hyperparameters scores identically, the data cannot distinguish them — pick by parsimony/interpretability and say so, rather than reporting the argmax as if it were discovered.
  • Compute budgeting: nested CV multiplies fits (outer × inner × grid); for heavy models use coarse inner grids, random search, or successive halving — but never drop the outer layer: that’s the whole page.
  • Tuning on the test set via iteration (“we only looked at the test score a few times”) is optional stopping (1.4) — the test set’s budget is spent by attention, not by fit calls.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive (or verify by simulation) the expected selection bonus for M candidates whose scores are i.i.d. N(μ, s²): it is μ + s·E[max of M standard normals] ≈ μ + s·√(2 ln M). Then measure the bonus empirically for M = 10, 100, 1000 with s = 0.02 and compare against the formula. What does the logarithmic growth imply about adding candidates to a search — and about the honesty of “we searched 10,000 configurations and found X”?

Hint E[max] of M standard normals grows like √(2 ln M); the bonus scales with the noise s, not the effect sizes.
Solution
Agreement is close (√(2 ln M) is an asymptotic approximation, slightly high at small M). Implications: the bonus grows only logarithmically — 10× more candidates buys little extra inflation — but it grows without bound, and it scales with score noise s: noisy small-validation searches suffer most. A “10,000-configuration search” must therefore report performance from an outer/nested estimate; the search’s own best score is guaranteed flattery worth ≈ s√(2 ln M).

Q2. Successive halving / Hyperband-style tuning spends more budget on promising candidates. Design a miniature version for 60 KNN configurations (k ∈ 1..60) on a fixed dataset: start all on 2-fold CV over 200 rows, keep the top 20, then 4-fold over 400 rows, keep top 7, then full 5-fold on all rows. Compare the winner and total fit-count against exhaustive GridSearchCV (60 configs × 5 folds). Does the cheap scheme pick the same k? Implement and report — and state the failure mode it imports (which honest-estimator principle does the multi-stage scoring bend?).

Solution
The cheap scheme usually lands on the same k or a neighbor (flat landscape) at a fraction of the fits. The bent principle: candidates are compared on different amounts of data at different stages — early elimination can kill a config whose advantage only shows at full data (variance-vs-budget trade). Remedies: generous early stages, random restarts, or accepting a small probability of eliminating the true optimum — the same order-statistic humility as everything else on this page.

6. Complex Questions

Write the paragraph connecting the family-wise-error view to the selection-bonus view of 4.1 Q2 — two languages, one phenomenon — and state which controls what in practice.

7. Summary

  • Tuning is selection; selection is optimism; the outer loop (nested CV or a virgin test set) is the only honest scoreboard.
  • best_score_ is a marketing number; the nested estimate is the performance claim; deploy the refit-on-all-data winner.
  • Grid/random: log-spaced scales, few meaningful dimensions, random search for big spaces; the one-SE rule buys robustness for free.
  • Every data-dependent step — scaling, selection, imputation — lives inside the fold pipeline.
  • Selection bonus ≈ s·√(2 ln M): grows with score noise and candidate count; budget score noise (bigger validation folds) and candidate count deliberately.
  • Comparison of tuned procedures needs identical budgets, pairing, and multiplicity awareness — the 3.7 rules at full depth.
  • Parts 2–4 are complete: you can build models, price their uncertainty, and choose among them honestly. Next, Part 5 turns regularization into a first-class citizen — the shrinkage dials this page kept referencing.
