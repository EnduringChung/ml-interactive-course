6.1 Polynomial Regression — Beyond the Line

Part 2’s line was a choice, not a law. Polynomial regression keeps the entire linear-model machinery — closed forms, t-tests, pipelines — but lets the fitted curve bend by replacing \(x\) with powers of itself. This page is where “linear in the parameters, flexible in the features” becomes a design principle (2.8 revisited with modeling intent).

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Replace \(x\) with the basis \(\phi(x) = (x, x^2, \ldots, x^d)\) and fit least squares on the expanded design. The model is still \(\hat y = \phi(x)^\top\beta\) — linear in \(\beta\), polynomial in \(x\). Degree \(d\) controls flexibility; everything from 4.1’s U-curve applies with \(d\) as the dial.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Your true function is cubic but you fit degree 7 with n = 20. Name the disease, its symptom in the coefficients, and its cure.
  2. Why does standardizing x before squaring change anything — isn’t \(x^2\) the same information as \((x/2)^2\) up to scaling?
  3. A polynomial fit looks perfect on [0, 1] and predicts −40 at x = 1.1. Which basis property makes polynomials uniquely catastrophic extrapolators?
  4. Orthogonal polynomials: what numerical problem do they solve, and do they change the fitted curve?
  5. You have two candidate degrees with CV MSE 8.1 and 8.3. Which two considerations decide, and what rule from 4.6 applies?
Answers
  1. Overfitting (variance-dominated). Symptom: huge, alternating-sign high-order coefficients (they encode noise wiggles). Cure: lower the degree, or regularize the expanded features (ridge on the basis — 5.2), or collect data.
  2. The fit is invariant (same column space) but the penalty and numerics are not: with raw scales, \(x^2\)’s column norm dwarfs \(x\)’s, so ridge shrinks them unequally and the normal equations are ill-conditioned. Standardization makes each basis column a fair citizen.
  3. Polynomials are global bases: a coefficient fitted to wiggle inside [0, 1] must express that wiggle via powers that explode outside it — the curve has nowhere to go but vertical. Local bases (steps, splines — 6.2, 6.4) keep misbehavior local.
  4. Ill-conditioning of the Vandermonde matrix \([1, x, x^2, \ldots]\): near-collinear columns make coefficient estimates numerically unstable. Orthogonal polynomials decorrelate the basis — same fitted values, far more stable coefficients.
  5. The gap is within noise (check the SE of the CV difference); prefer the simpler degree unless the extra flexibility earns its keep — the one-standard-error rule, plus interpretation cost.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Model-selection theater, then honesty: for the quartic truth \(y = 1 - 2x + 0.5x^2 - 0.1x^3 + 0.01x^4 + \varepsilon\) (σ = 2, n = 50), fit degrees 1–10 and report (a) training MSE, (b) 5-fold CV MSE, (c) true test MSE on 20,000 fresh points. Identify the CV-argmin and the true-argmin; how often (over 200 data draws) does CV pick the exactly-optimal degree ±1?

Solution
CV lands within ±1 of the true degree in most draws, and its chosen model’s true error is statistically indistinguishable from the oracle’s — degree selection is one of the friendlier hyperparameters. The residual gap between the two rows is selection noise, priced and familiar (4.6).

Exercise 2. The conditioning autopsy: fit degree 8 on x ∈ [0, 100] with (a) raw powers via np.polyfit, (b) standardized powers via the pipeline. Compare the leading coefficients and the fitted values’ SD across 100 bootstrap resamples of the training data. Which parameterization’s coefficients are reproducible, and what does that mean for reporting “the x³ effect”?

Solution
The raw parameterization’s leading coefficient wobbles by orders of magnitude — collinearity of powers makes each coefficient an unstable mixture of all others. The standardized basis (or orthogonal polynomials) spreads the work evenly, so coefficients — and any story attached to them — become reportable. Fitted values were never in danger; your interpretations were.

Exercise 3. Extrapolation guard-rail: build a PolyModel class wrapping the pipeline with (a) predict(x_new) that refuses (with an informative error) points outside the training range, (b) a slope(x) method returning the derivative \(f'(x) = \sum k c_k x^{k-1}\) evaluated via the fitted coefficients. Fit on the sinusoid, verify the derivative against np.gradient at three points, and demonstrate the refusal at x beyond range.

Solution
The class turns two recurring lessons — range-policing (2.1) and derivative-style interpretation — into reusable machinery. The slope check against a numerical derivative validates the standardized-coefficient chain rule.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • **np.polyfit‘s coefficient order** (highest first) versus PolynomialFeatures’ (lowest first) — mixing the two conventions is a classic silent bug.
  • include_bias=True adds the constant column: pair it with LinearRegression(fit_intercept=False) or you get a redundant intercept (harmless with OLS, wasteful with penalized fits).
  • Extrapolation is the polynomial’s worst subject — global basis, exploding tails; guard-rails or local bases (6.4).
  • Degree ≠ complexity exactly: a degree-9 fit with ridge (α tuned) can behave like a degree-3 fit — flexibility is jointly set by basis size and penalty (5.2).
  • Weights: noisy heteroscedastic data wants weighted least squares on the basis (LinearRegression can’t; use sample-weighted estimators) — otherwise high-variance regions dictate the global curve.
  • Numerical creep: degrees above ~15 on raw scales produce garbage even when predictions look fine — check np.linalg.cond of the design, or just standardize/orthogonalize.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Bias column + intercept double-counts the constant: OLS survives (pseudo-inverse absorbs it), but ridge would split the intercept across both — and the coefficient table now lies about which constant is which.
  2. The standardiser was fitted on train+test. This is the real leak, and it is worth being precise about why the previous version of this drill was wrong: it flagged pf.fit_transform(X_te), but PolynomialFeatures is stateless — it has no fitted attributes, so fit_transform and transform on the same rows return bit-identical arrays. Refitting it is a no-op, not a bug. The same is not true of any stateful transformer: StandardScaler (means/SDs), KBinsDiscretizer (bin edges), SplineTransformer (knots) and QuantileTransformer (quantiles) all learn from the data they are shown, so fitting one of those on the full dataset genuinely leaks test information. That asymmetry is exactly why you should stop hand-assembling transformers and put them in a Pipeline — the pipeline fits on whatever the fold hands it, and there is no way to get the order wrong.
  3. 1-D x into cross_val_score — sklearn expects 2-D features; the explicit reshape (or pipeline over the original frame) is required.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Show that fitting degree-d polynomials by OLS is a linear smoother: \(\hat{y} = H^{(d)} y\) where \(H^{(d)}\) is the hat matrix of the expanded design. Use this to (a) explain why training error strictly decreases in d, and (b) compute the effective degrees of freedom as tr(\(H^{(d)}\)) = d + 1 — verifying both claims numerically on a small dataset.

Hint OLS fitted values are projections; nested column spaces give nested projections; trace of a projection = its rank.
Solution

\(\hat y = X_d (X_d^\top X_d)^{-1} X_d^\top y \equiv H^{(d)} y\); the column spaces are nested \(span(X_1) \subset span(X_2) \subset \cdots\), and projections onto superspaces can only shrink residuals — training error is monotone down. \(H^{(d)}\) is idempotent of rank d + 1, so tr = d + 1 — the effective df that Cp charges for (5.1 Cp — same identity).

This linear-smootherness is what lets polynomials, splines, and KNN be compared on one complexity axis (2.9 Q1) — the tr(H) bookkeeping runs the whole show.

Q2. Chebyshev rebalancing: for x ∈ [−1, 1], compare the condition number of the Vandermonde matrix under (a) raw monomials \(x^k\) and (b) Chebyshev polynomials \(T_k(x)\), for d = 10. Then verify that OLS on the Chebyshev basis yields identical fitted values (up to tolerance) with far better-conditioned normal equations. What does this imply about the information content of the two bases — and about which part of the analysis (fit vs interpretation) benefits?

Hint Both bases span the same degree-d polynomial space; conditioning differs, span doesn’t.
Solution
The condition number improves by orders of magnitude while fitted values coincide to float precision: the span is identical, only the coordinate system changed. Interpretation benefits most — coefficients on Chebyshev basis functions are stable, meaningful amplitudes of oscillation, whereas raw-monomial coefficients are numerically arbitrary mixtures. Same moral as 2.8’s standardization, taken to its mathematical conclusion.

7. Summary

  • Polynomials = linear models on a power basis: full inference machinery, one flexibility dial (degree).
  • Train error always falls with degree; test error U-curves; CV chooses — and usually lands near the truth.
  • Standardize (or orthogonalize) the basis: conditioning protects coefficients, penalties, and your interpretations.
  • Global bases extrapolate catastrophically; police the range or move to local bases.
  • tr(H) = d + 1: polynomials, splines, and KNN speak the same effective-df language.
  • Ridge-on-basis decouples flexibility from degree — the penalty is the modern dial.
  • The most local basis of all: cut x into intervals and fit constants — 6.2 Step functions.
