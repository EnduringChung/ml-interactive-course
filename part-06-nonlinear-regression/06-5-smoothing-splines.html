6.5 Smoothing Splines

Instead of choosing where to put knots, put a knot at every data point and pay a penalty for roughness. The smoothing spline minimizes

\[\sum_i (y_i - f(x_i))^2 + \lambda \int \big(f''(t)\big)^2 dt\]

— fit the data, but pay for wiggling. One continuous dial, λ, replaces knot placement: the elegance that makes this the statistician’s smoothing benchmark.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The minimizer of that criterion is a natural cubic spline with knots at every distinct x — the penalty converts “how many knots” into “how much bending,” λ trading fidelity against roughness: λ = 0 interpolates, λ = ∞ returns the least-squares straight line.

UnivariateSpline’s s is a sum-of-squared-residuals allowance (not λ itself): s ≈ nσ² targets the truth’s smoothness. Tune it by CV like any hyperparameter:

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why must the roughness penalty use the second derivative (and not the first or the function itself)?
  2. λ = 0 and λ = ∞: state both endpoints precisely, including what the penalty’s null space contributes.
  3. UnivariateSpline’s s is not λ. What is the relationship, and why does the “s ≈ nσ²” heuristic make sense?
  4. What are the effective degrees of freedom of a smoothing spline, and how does it behave as λ moves?
  5. Your data has 80 points but you know the first 10 are from a different regime (batch artifact). Why is a single global λ the wrong tool, and what are the two standard remedies?
Answers
  1. Penalizing f itself would fight the data’s level; f′ would penalize any slope (linear functions cost infinitely). f″ is the curvature — zero for lines (so lines are free, matching the penalty’s null space) and growing with wiggliness: it measures exactly what “too wiggly” means.
  2. λ = 0: pure interpolation — the natural cubic spline through every point (n df). λ = ∞: f″ ≡ 0 forced → f is any straight line, fitted by least squares (2 df). The linear functions sit in the penalty’s null space and are never penalized.
  3. The criterion balances RSS + λ·∫f″²; s bounds the RSS directly, so s ≈ n·(per-point noise variance) reproduces the λ that an honest bias–variance trade would pick when σ² is known — a heuristic starting point, still tuned by CV.
  4. df(λ) = tr(S_λ) for the smoother matrix S_λ — a continuous quantity sliding from n (λ=0) to 2 (λ=∞). Report it alongside any smoothing-spline fit: it is the model’s honest complexity.
  5. A global λ imposes one smoothness everywhere, but the artifact region needs different smoothness (or exclusion). Remedies: (a) regime-wise smoothing (fit separately / include regime covariates), (b) adaptive/variable-knot penalty methods — or fix the data issue upstream.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. The σ² connection, tested: for σ ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 1.0} (sinusoid, n = 80), tune the smoothing parameter by 5-fold CV and report the chosen s in units of nσ². Does the optimal s/n track σ²? Then verify the converse use: given a well-tuned fit, estimate σ² as RSS/n and check it against the truth.

Solution
The optimal s/n rises roughly with σ² — noisier data tolerates (needs) rougher-penalized, smoother fits. And the tuned fit’s residual variance estimates σ² (slightly downward — the smoother absorbs some noise, the classic df-shrunk underestimate; honest versions divide by n − df).

Exercise 2. Effective df, measured properly: compute the smoothing spline’s hat matrix by evaluating fits at fixed design points across many y-draws (S_λ via covariance: E[ŷ] = S_λ f under fixed x, σ known) — or, cheaply, via spl.get_coeffs() and the B-spline basis. Simpler and rigorous: for 300 draws of y = f + ε at the same x, estimate df(λ) = Σᵢ Cov(ŷᵢ, yᵢ)/σ². Trace df against λ for λ ∈ {small, medium, large} and confirm the slide from n toward 2.

Solution
The covariance estimator (the same E[df] = Σ Cov(ŷᵢ, yᵢ)/σ² used for the lasso in 5.3 Q2) slides smoothly from ≈ 50 down toward the linear model’s 2 — smoothing splines genuinely are a continuous family of models between interpolation and the mean line, with df as the honest complexity meter.

Exercise 3. Confidence bands for the smooth: using the bootstrap (resample rows, refit at fixed λ, percentile bands — 4.5), build 95% bands for the smoothing-spline fit at σ = 0.3. Overlay the pointwise band and check its coverage of the truth across x (fraction of grid points where truth lies inside). Then explain why these are confidence bands for the function and not prediction intervals for new points — and widen one into the other.

Solution
The bootstrap band quantifies uncertainty of the estimated curve (mean response) — narrow, and its truth-coverage is the confidence-band claim. New observations add irreducible σ on top: the widened band is the prediction interval, the same mean-vs-new-point distinction as 2.2 Exercise 3, now for curves.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • s vs λ confusion is the top UnivariateSpline trap: s bounds residual mass (so it scales with n and σ²), while the textbook λ multiplies roughness. Translate once, tune by CV, document which you report.
  • Weights ≠ frequencies alone: w multiplies each squared residual (the objective is \(\sum_i w_i|y_i - f(x_i)|^2\)), so for known heteroscedastic variances pass w = 1/σᵢ² — inverse variance, not inverse sd. (Getting this wrong by a power of 2 is easy: 1/σ under-weights the noisy points relative to 1/σ².) This is the same weighted criterion as 2.10’s repair menu, with the weight applied to the squared term.
  • Replicate x values: smoothing splines assume unique x. Current scipy will not raise on a tie — it silently half-weights the conflicting duplicates (and can return NaN with only a warning). Average within duplicates and pass the multiplicities as w explicitly, so the data reduction is yours and visible.
  • Effective df should accompany every smooth: a fit is not described by λ/s alone; “smoothed with 7.3 df” is the reproducible statement.
  • Boundary behavior: natural-spline structure keeps tails linear, but tail estimates still lean on few points — report where the data actually supports the curve (or shade the bands honestly, Exercise 3).
  • Multivariate smoothing splines exist (thin-plate splines) but explode computationally with dimension — the practical route to multiple smooths is the additive model (6.7).

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Duplicate x with a conflicting y fails silently. UnivariateSpline does not raise on tied abscissae in current scipy — it quietly fits the half-weight average of the two conflicting values (you can see it: the fitted value at the tied x lands between \(y[4]\) and \(y[5]\), not on either). That is a defensible convention, but it is invisible, it silently halves the effective weight of a real observation, and with a large discrepancy the solver can also hit its iteration limit and return NaN with only a warning. Deduplicate explicitly — average \(y\) at tied x and add the multiplicities as w — so the data reduction is yours and visible.
  2. s as λ: s = 0.001 allows almost no residual mass → near-interpolation regardless of n; the allowance must scale like nσ² (then be tuned).
  3. A constant w weights nothing. np.full(100, 0.09) is the same value at every point, so it multiplies every squared residual by 0.09 and the solution is identical to unweighted — the “weights” are decorative. Real inverse-variance weighting must vary per point: since UnivariateSpline minimizes \(\sum_i w_i\,|y_i - f(x_i)|^2\), pass \(w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2\) (inverse variance, not inverse sd) so that high-variance points are pulled down proportionally.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Sketch the variational argument: among all twice-differentiable f minimizing RSS + λ∫f″², argue the minimizer must be a natural cubic spline with knots at the data points. (Hint: on any interval between consecutive x’s, the Euler–Lagrange condition for the integrand (f″)² forces f⁗ = 0 — a cubic; the boundary conditions at the extreme data force naturality.) What role does λ play in this derivation, and what does the result not tell you (name one thing CV still must decide)?

Hint Variation f → f + εg vanishing at data points; integrate ∫f″g″ by parts twice; the ODE f⁗ = 0 emerges between knots with natural boundary conditions f″ = f‴ = 0 at the ends.
Solution Perturbing f by smooth g with g(xᵢ) = 0 and setting the first variation to zero: ∫(f″g″)dx + λ∑-terms give, after integrating by parts, ∫f⁗g = 0 for all admissible g between consecutive data — hence f⁗ ≡ 0 there (cubic pieces). At the boundary intervals the natural conditions f″ = f‴ = 0 appear (no outside data to balance them), making the spline natural. λ scales the trade-off inside this fixed family — the family itself is λ-independent. What the derivation doesn’t decide: σ² / the absolute scale of residual tolerance (where on the λ-path to stop) — that is CV’s job, exactly as in Exercise 1; theory gives the shape of the model space, validation picks the point in it.

Q2. Reinsch/GCV without GCV: the generalized cross-validation score is \(\text{GCV}(\lambda) = \frac{n\,\text{RSS}(\lambda)}{(n - \text{df}(\lambda))^2}\). Implement GCV for the smoothing spline using the covariance-based df estimator (Exercise 2, at reduced R for cost), sweep λ, and compare GCV-chosen s against 5-fold-CV-chosen s over 100 simulated datasets. Report agreement rates and which is cheaper. When do they disagree systematically (heteroscedastic data)?

Solution
GCV is the closed-form cousin of CV (cheaper: one fit per λ, not per fold) and typically lands within a notch of CV on homoscedastic data. Under heteroscedasticity GCV’s implicit uniform-σ assumption biases the choice — the same fragility as every analytic criterion vs the empirical honesty of cross-validation.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (smoothing × 5.2 ridge × 6.4). Prove-by-construction the identity: a smoothing spline is ridge regression on a rich spline basis (fine knot grid) with a penalty tailored to the basis. Implement ridge-on-SplineTransformer (many knots), tune α by CV, and compare against UnivariateSpline tuned by CV: fitted curves, effective df, CV MSE. Where they differ, explain via the penalty’s form (coefficient shrinkage vs derivative integral) and boundary handling.

C2 (smoothing × 3.6 GLM × 6.7 preview). Smoothing for non-Gaussian responses: binary outcome with smooth logit truth logit(p(x)) = sin(2πx). Fit (a) a Gaussian-error smoothing spline to the 0/1 y (wrong likelihood), (b) a binned-response spline (bin x into 15 cells, smooth the empirical logits), (c) plain logistic with polynomial basis. Compare CV log-loss. This ad-hoc tour is exactly the problem generalized additive logistic models solve properly — write down what (b) gets right and where it still falls short of a true GAM.

7. Summary

  • Smoothing splines: knots everywhere, roughness penalized — knot placement becomes one continuous dial.
  • The minimizer is a natural cubic spline through the data; λ slides df continuously from n to 2.
  • s (residual allowance ≈ nσ²) vs λ (roughness weight): know which your library exposes; tune by CV either way.
  • Report effective df — the honest complexity of a smooth.
  • Bands: bootstrap for the function; add σ for new points.
  • GCV is the cheap analytic dial; heteroscedasticity breaks it — CV stays the referee.
  • One smooth of one variable is a toy; real data has many x’s: additive models — 6.7 GAMs — after the local-regression way station: 6.6 Local regression.
