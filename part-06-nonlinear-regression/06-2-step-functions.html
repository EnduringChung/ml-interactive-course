6.2 Step Functions

The most local basis possible: cut the range of x into intervals and fit a separate constant to each. Step functions trade smoothness for robustness and interpretability — and make the knots (cutpoints) an explicit, debatable modeling decision instead of a hidden one.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Choose cutpoints \(c_1 < c_2 < \cdots < c_K\); build dummy columns \(C_k(x) = \mathbb{1}(c_{k-1} < x \le c_k)\) (plus the intercept as the first cell’s level, or all K cells with no intercept — the cell-means trick from 2.6). OLS on these columns fits the mean of y within each bin:

2. Concept Questions

  1. More bins = more flexibility. What are the two failure modes at K → ∞ and K = 1?
  2. Uniform-width vs quantile bins: when does each starve?
  3. Where do discontinuities come from, and when is a discontinuous model the right answer (name two real settings)?
  4. Your step fit jumps at x = 5 but the scatter looks smooth there. What made the jump, and which alternative basis would remove it?
  5. How does the step function’s bias behave within a bin, and why is it worst exactly where the true curve is steepest?
Answers
  1. K → ∞: one observation per bin — the fit interpolates noise (zero bias, maximal variance; the k = 1 KNN of regression). K = 1: the global mean — maximal bias. Between them, the same U-curve as always.
  2. Uniform bins starve where data is sparse (tails: bins with 2 points produce wild constants) and waste bins where data is dense; quantile bins equalize counts (stable constants) but let bin widths vary wildly, so the x-resolution is uneven. Match the strategy to the design: uniform for even designs, quantile for skewed designs.
  3. From the basis’s own discontinuity at each cutpoint — the model cannot be smooth there. Right answer when the world genuinely steps: drug dosing tiers, tax brackets, age-risk categories, tissue types along an anatomical boundary.
  4. The jump is bin-boundary arithmetic (adjacent constants straddling a steep true slope differ by slope × width) — not found structure. Continuous bases (6.4) trade away the discontinuity.
  5. The constant equals the bin’s mean of f — bias at x is f(x) minus that mean: zero at the bin’s “center of mass,” growing toward the edges, maximal where |f′| is large (steep regions sweep across more f-values per bin).

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Piecewise-constant vs piecewise-linear: fit (a) 5-bin step function, (b) 5-bin piecewise-linear (bins + bin×x interaction within each bin — implement via KBinsDiscretizer + manual within-bin centering), on the sinusoid. Compare CV MSE and plot both. Which basis removes the edge bias without adding discontinuities within bins, and what’s the cost?

Solution
Adding (x − bin mean) within each bin upgrades constants to local lines: bias drops where the truth slopes, discontinuities at boundaries shrink (adjacent lines nearly meet), and the price is 2K parameters instead of K. This is, in fact, a crude linear spline — 6.4 does it with proper continuity.

Exercise 2. Quantile vs uniform under skewed x: x ~ lognormal, y = √x + noise. Compare 6-bin uniform vs quantile step fits by CV, and by worst-bin error (max |bias| over bins estimated on a dense grid). Explain the winner’s mechanism, and state when the loser’s strategy would flip to winning.

Solution
Quantile wins: lognormal x piles mass near 0, so uniform bins leave the extreme right tail with a handful of points (variance explosion) and the dense left region over-binned (wasted flexibility). Quantile bins equalize evidence per bin. The flip: if the true function bends most in the sparse tail, uniform bins’ finer resolution out there can win — bin strategy should follow where the curve is interesting, not just where the data is dense.

Exercise 3. The threshold hunt: simulate a true jump at unknown t* (y = 1 + 2·1(x > t) + noise, t = 6.3). Estimate t* by fitting step functions on a fine grid of candidate cutpoints and choosing the cut whose two-cell fit minimizes RSS. Report the estimate over 200 simulations (bias, SD). Then explain why this is change-point detection, how it relates to the basis view, and what the inference caveat is for the chosen t̂*.

Solution
The grid-of-cells fit is a two-bin step model — change-point detection is basis selection with the cutpoint as hyperparameter. Caveat: t̂’s sampling distribution is nonstandard (the cutpoint is a discrete argmin over candidate fits), so naive “±SE” statements about t̂ are unreliable; proper inference uses specialized change-point machinery, and reporting the estimate with a bootstrap distribution is the honest middle ground.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Cutpoint choice is a hyperparameter — chosen on validation data, never by staring at the scatter (4.6); bins chosen from the full data then CV’d leak (the 0.5 law, again).
  • pd.cut on test data with bins from training — freeze the edges: use KBinsDiscretizer fitted in-pipeline, or carry pd.cut(..., bins=frozen_edges); re-deriving quantiles on new data silently changes the basis.
  • Edge handling: include_lowest=True for the left edge; decide explicitly whether bins are (lo, hi]; off-by-one rows at boundaries are classic.
  • Empty or tiny bins produce unstable constants; enforce a minimum bin count (quantile strategy + n_bins sanity) or merge cells.
  • Trees are adaptive step functions (Part 7): they choose cutpoints from the data by RSS reduction — the machinery this page builds by hand, automated and greedy.
  • Dose responses and policy bands are genuinely discontinuous; forcing a smooth basis there underfits the phenomenon — mismatched smoothness is a modeling error in both directions.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Edges re-derived per split: train and test are binned by different boundaries — the feature spaces differ; quantile edges must be frozen from training (that’s what KBinsDiscretizer.fit stores).
  2. Cumulative dummies + intercept: indicators (x > e) are nested, and with an intercept the design is rank-deficient-ish (full redundancy at the outer cells); interpretability collapses. Use interval dummies (one per bin, drop-first or cell-means).
  3. New data outside the training range: pd.cut returns NaN for x = 11 — a silent production crash. Clip, extend the edge bin, or refuse explicitly.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the step-function fit explicitly: with K cells and cell-means parameterization, show the OLS solution assigns each cell its sample mean, that the hat matrix is block-constant, and that tr(H) = K. Then prove the within-bin bias identity: E[μ̂_k] − f(x) = f̄_k − f(x) for x in cell k, and show the integrated bias² over a bin equals the variance of f within the bin (exactly the quantity minimized by optimal binning around steep regions).

Hint Cell dummies are disjoint indicators; each cell’s normal equation decouples (the 2.6 Q1 argument, geometrically). For the bias: integrate (f̄_k − f(x))² over the bin.
Solution Each row of the design has exactly one 1, so the normal equations decouple per cell: μ̂_k = mean of y in cell k, H is block-diagonal with (1/nₖ) blocks — tr(H) = K effective parameters. Bias at x ∈ cell k: E[μ̂_k] = f̄_k (mean of f over the cell under uniform-x), so bias = f̄_k − f(x); integrating its square over the bin gives ∫(f − f̄_k)²dx = nₖ·Var_bin(f) — the bin’s internal variance of the true function. Hence optimal bins concentrate boundaries where f′ is large: equalize f-variance, not point counts (the refined version of Exercise 2’s moral).

Q2. Step functions as the t = 0 limit of trees, and the CV consistency question: implement a one-split “stump” fit (threshold chosen to minimize RSS) on the sinusoid; compare its CV MSE against the best 5-bin step model. Then simulate 200 datasets: how often does the stump’s chosen threshold fall within ±0.2 of the bin boundary with the smallest within-bin bias? What does the answer say about data-driven cutpoints as estimators — consistent, noisy, or both?

Solution
The stump’s threshold estimate has SD ≈ 0.4–0.6 at n = 120 — noisy: single cutpoints are unstable estimators (the reason random forests average many trees’ cuts, Part 7). Meanwhile its 2-cell train MSE beats the fixed 5-bin fit because it’s chosen for this dataset — in-sample selection flattery again. Cutpoints are consistent only in that they concentrate near the steepest region; they are emphatically not stable point estimates, and any narrative built on a specific estimated threshold needs uncertainty attached (Exercise 3’s bootstrap).

6. Complex Questions

C1 (steps × 2.6 categorical × 2.7 interaction). Two-group step surfaces: y depends on x through different step patterns per group. Fit (a) pooled bins, (b) bins + group, (c) bins + group + bin×group, comparing CV MSE. When does (c) beat (b), and what does the bin×group interaction matrix look like (the subgroup version of cell means)? Connect to how decision trees grow group-specific splits (7.1).

C2 (steps × 4.4 bias–variance). Decompose the K-bin step estimator’s error across K (protocol of 4.4, 300 training sets, n = 40): bias², variance, and total. Verify: variance ≈ K·σ²/n-scaled, bias² tracking the integrated f-variance within bins (Q1’s identity). Then overlay the same three curves for piecewise-linear bins (Exercise 1) and state the K-vs-basis trade in one sentence.

7. Summary

  • Step functions = dummies over intervals: maximal locality, minimal smoothness, K effective parameters.
  • Cutpoints are hyperparameters (frozen from training, chosen by CV); quantile vs uniform is an evidence-distribution decision.
  • Bias concentrates within bins in steep regions — the f-variance identity tells you where the basis hurts.
  • Discontinuities are basis artifacts unless the world truly steps (doses, brackets, boundaries).
  • One-split stumps are adaptive step functions — unstable cutpoints, the seed of Part 7’s trees.
  • Adding within-bin slopes fixes edge bias cheaply — the segue to proper splines.
  • Continuous local bases with enforced smoothness: 6.3 Basis functions generalizes, 6.4 Regression splines perfects it.
