8.2 The Maximal-Margin Classifier

Given linearly separable data, infinitely many hyperplanes classify it perfectly. The hard-margin SVM picks the one with the fattest cushion — the maximal margin between the classes — and identifies a handful of support vectors as the only points that matter.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The optimization, in the canonical form:

\[\max_{w, b} \; M = \frac{2}{\|w\|} \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i (w^\top x_i + b) \ge 1 \;\; \forall i\]

— every point correctly outside its margin line; equivalently minimize ‖w‖²/2. The points with yᵢ(wᵀxᵢ + b) = 1 are the support vectors: removing any non-support point changes nothing.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why does the hard-margin SVM require separability — what happens to the constraint set when one point crosses the margin?
  2. If a new data point lands far from the boundary on the correct side, how does the classifier change? What if it lands inside the margin?
  3. Explain “the maximal-margin hyperplane depends only on the support vectors” both computationally and geometrically.
  4. Two candidate hyperplanes both separate the data: one with margin 0.4, one with margin 1.2. Which does the SVM choose, and what implicit belief about future data justifies it?
  5. A support vector sits exactly on the margin line. What is its value of yᵢ(wᵀxᵢ + b), and what does the constraint say about points beyond it?
Answers
  1. The constraints yᵢ(wᵀxᵢ + b) ≥ 1 must be simultaneously satisfiable; one mispositioned point makes the feasible set empty — no solution exists, full stop. (The soft margin of 8.3 relaxes exactly this.)
  2. Far-and-correct: nothing — it’s not a support vector, the classifier is unchanged (this is the SVM’s famous stability-to-far-points, the mirror image of 2.3’s leverage worries). Inside the margin: it becomes a support vector with a constraint violation — in the hard-margin world it would make the problem infeasible.
  3. Computationally: the solution’s KKT conditions force non-support points’ multipliers to 0 — they appear nowhere in w = Σαᵢyᵢxᵢ. Geometrically: the fattest cushion is pressed against the closest points on each side — the boundary of the convex hulls; interior points don’t touch it.
  4. Margin 1.2 — with the justification that future draws from the same distribution will, with high probability, fall in the cushion you measured on this sample (1.3’s logic applied to geometry): maximal margin is maximum-confidence classification under a stability assumption.
  5. Exactly 1 — the constraint is tight (KKT: non-zero multiplier ⟺ tight constraint). Points beyond have yᵢ(wᵀxᵢ + b) > 1 — slack constraints, zero multipliers, no influence.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Maximal margin by brute force: for a tiny 2-D separable dataset (6 points), enumerate a fine grid of candidate (w-angle, b) hyperplanes, keep the separating ones, and maximize the margin. Compare your brute-force winner to sklearn’s SVC(C=1e6) solution (normalize both to the ±1 convention first). This exercise is the SVM, before the optimization theory.

Solution
Scanning directions and centering the slab finds the same solution the QP does — the optimization theory is a fast, principled shortcut through exactly this search. Two conventions have to line up or the numbers disagree by a factor of two or three, which is worth internalizing: (i) the margin is the full width between the two margin lines, i.e. the 2/‖w‖ convention sklearn uses, not the half-width some texts quote; and (ii) project with X @ w and take min over positives / max over negatives. Writing it as y * (X @ w) and then mixing min and max across the two classes silently mixes a projection with a signed score and reports a margin several times too large — the failure is silent because the code still returns a plausible-looking number.

Exercise 2. The fragility experiment: take a separable dataset and its hard-margin SVM. Add one new point far away but on the correct side — measure the change in w, b, and predictions. Then add one point inside the margin (violating) — the hard-margin problem becomes infeasible; observe sklearn’s behavior with C = 1e6 (and connect to 8.3’s fix). Quantify both perturbations: which data points does the SVM care about?

Solution
Far-correct points: zero influence (the support-vector claim, demonstrated). Violating points: infeasibility for the true hard margin — C = 1e6 silently approximates it by allowing tiny slacks at enormous cost, which is exactly the tension the soft margin (8.3) makes explicit and tunable.

Exercise 3. Margin as confidence: for the hard-margin fit, plot decision_function magnitude against distance-to-boundary for 2000 fresh points from the same distribution, and estimate: P(correct classification | |score| > 1) vs P(correct | |score| < 0.2). Then explain in margin language why large-margin points are “safe” — and one way this intuition fails (distribution shift, e.g., the covariate-shift experiment of 4.2 Q2).

Solution
(|score|) is distance-to-boundary in canonical units: points with cushion almost never flip, borderline points flip often — the margin literalizes “confidence.” The failure: under covariate shift the boundary itself may be misplaced relative to the new distribution, and every confidence statement inherits that error — margins quantify position-in-sample geometry, not distributional safety.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Hard margin is a classroom object: real data has overlap; C = 1e6 is a soft margin pretending (8.3) — with numerical instability as a bonus. Use honest C tuning in practice.
  • C=1e6 ≠ hard margin: it’s soft with astronomically expensive slacks — sklearn may warn about convergence; the fix is the soft-margin formulation, not more iterations.
  • Support vector count and dimension: in p ≥ n separable data every point can be a support vector (5.6’s interpolation world) — the “only a few matter” intuition is a low-dimension luxury.
  • SVC’s decision_function is (wᵀx + b) in the canonical scale — margin units, thanks to the ±1 normalization; interpret |score| as cushion honestly only for linear kernels (kernels change the space, 8.4).
  • LinearSVC vs SVC(kernel="linear"): different solvers (liblinear vs libsvm), slightly different numerical answers, same geometry; LinearSVC scales to big n, SVC exposes support vectors directly.
  • Class imbalance: hard margins tilt toward the majority class’s geometry; class_weight rescales the constraints per class (3.1).

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Overlapping classes + C = 1e6: the hard-margin assumption is false; the solver strains to satisfy near-impossible constraints (warnings, poor margin, borderline numerical behavior). Use tuned soft C.
  2. “Margin” from slacked solutions: with slacks, 2/‖w‖ overstates the effective separation — violated support vectors sit inside the reported cushion. Report margins only with the slack picture (fraction violated, C).
  3. Support-vector fraction misread: with heavy overlap, half the points become support vectors — the “efficient summary” story collapses; also SV-based prediction shortcuts are an implementation detail, not a data-efficiency claim.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the support-vector-only representation: the SVM dual has w = Σᵢαᵢyᵢxᵢ with αᵢ > 0 only for support vectors (KKT complementary slackness). Verify numerically that predictions via Σαᵢyᵢ(xᵢᵀx) + b (using only SVs) reproduce decision_function exactly, and explain why this representation is (a) the gateway to kernels (8.4) and (b) a statement about which data the model compresses.

Hint KKT: constraints with slack have αᵢ = 0; tight constraints may have αᵢ > 0. w is a sum over αᵢyᵢxᵢ — zero terms vanish.
Solution

Complementary slackness ties each αᵢ to its constraint’s tightness: yᵢ(wᵀxᵢ + b) > 1 ⇒ αᵢ = 0. So w = Σαᵢyᵢxᵢ sums over SVs only, and predictions need only inner products xᵢᵀx against SVs:

  1. Replacing xᵢᵀx by a kernel k(xᵢ, x) gives nonlinear SVMs without ever materializing coordinates — the dual only ever sees inner products. (b) The model compresses the dataset to its boundary-adjacent skeleton: a data-efficiency statement in separable low-noise regimes, and a warning when everything is a support vector.

Q2. Maximal margin vs logistic boundary, geometrically: on separable data, fit both (SVC C=1e6; LogisticRegression) and compare boundary angles and margin widths across 100 resamples. Which boundary is stable, which drifts, and why does the logistic boundary depend on all points while the SVM’s doesn’t (7.2’s stability lens applied to geometry)? State the resulting division of labor.

Solution
Both boundaries wander (resampled data, overlapping tails), but the SVM’s direction is pinned by a handful of extreme points while logistic’s is pulled by the whole sample — different sensitivity profiles. Division of labor: SVM geometry when the boundary is what matters and data is separable-ish; logistic when probabilities, calibrated rankings (3.7), or full-data robustness are the product. Full comparison: 8.5.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (max margin × 1.3 probability). Margin as a probability guarantee, stress-tested: for separable data with margin M, a classical result bounds future misclassification by (roughly) R²/M²-type ratios (R = data radius). Simulate: vary the noise level to produce margins M ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0}, measure test error over fresh draws, and plot error vs M. Does fatter margin → lower error hold empirically? Where does the monotonicity break (name the two competing effects: margin vs fit-to-distribution)?

C2 (max margin × 2.6 Simpson). The pooled-margin trap: two clusters with different internal geometries where the pooled maximal-margin boundary bisects neither. Construct the data, fit pooled vs per-cluster hard margins, and quantify boundary disagreement. When is a “single global margin” the wrong object — and does the kernel trick (8.4) or explicit group features (2.6) resolve it more honestly?

7. Summary

  • Hard-margin SVM: maximize 2/‖w‖ s.t. every point sits outside its margin line — solvable only when separable.
  • Support vectors (yᵢ(wᵀxᵢ + b) = 1) fully determine the classifier; all other points are deletable.
  • Far-correct points cost nothing; margin-violating points break feasibility — the soft margin’s reason to exist.
  • Margin = measured cushion = confidence geometry — valid in-sample, conditional on the distribution.
  • The dual representation (predict via inner products with SVs) is the on-ramp to kernels.
  • Overlap and p ≥ n dissolve the clean story — tune softness, expect many SVs.
  • Real data overlaps: pay for slack, tune the price — 8.3 The soft-margin classifier.
