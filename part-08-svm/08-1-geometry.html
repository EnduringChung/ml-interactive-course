8.1 Geometry Essentials

SVMs live and breathe linear algebra: hyperplanes, normals, projections, distances, margins. This page drills the geometric vocabulary — every SVM statement in 8.2–8.6 is one of these objects in costume.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

A hyperplane in d dimensions is \(\{x : w^\top x + b = 0\}\) — defined by a normal vector w (its direction) and offset b. The signed distance from a point x₀ to the hyperplane is

\[d(x_0) = \frac{w^\top x_0 + b}{\|w\|}\]

— the quantity SVMs classify by (its sign) and maximize (its magnitude):

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why does the same hyperplane described by (2w, 2b) define a different SVM margin — and what convention fixes w’s scale?
  2. What does the sign of wᵀx₀ + b encode, and what does its magnitude encode before scaling?
  3. Two parallel hyperplanes wᵀx + b = ±1: derive the distance between them geometrically (pick one point on one, compute its distance to the other).
  4. In 2-D, a hyperplane is a line; in 3-D, a plane. What is it in 1-D — and does the SVM machinery care about the dimension?
  5. Why do SVMs standardize features despite being geometric — name the quantity that units distort.
Answers
  1. (2w, 2b) is the same zero-set (same line) but the margin formula 2/‖w‖ halves — margins are only comparable in the canonical convention wᵀx + b = ±1 at the support vectors, which pins ‖w‖ (SVMs solve for w up to this constraint, so the scale ambiguity is absorbed).
  2. Sign = which side (the class in SVMs: +1 vs −1). Raw magnitude = distance scaled by ‖w‖ — proportional to true distance but unit-dependent until normalized.
  3. Take x₊ on {wᵀx + b = +1}: wᵀx₊ + b = 1. Distance to {wᵀx + b = −1}: |wᵀx₊ + b + 1|/‖w‖ = |1 + 1|/‖w‖ = 2/‖w‖. That’s the margin the SVM maximizes.
  4. In 1-D a “hyperplane” is a point (w, b as scalars, w·x + b = 0); the algebra — sign, distance, margin 2/‖w‖ — is dimension-blind. Everything here works in any d, which is why kernels (8.4) can.
  5. Distance and margin are computed in raw coordinates: a feature measured in millimeters vs meters changes ‖w‖ and the geometry of “closeness.” Standardization puts all axes in comparable units — the same law as 5.2, now geometric.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Build signed_distance(X, w, b) returning distances for a matrix of points, and verify three properties numerically on random data: (a) distance of points on the hyperplane ≈ 0; (b) flipping (w, b) → (−w, −b) flips all signs but preserves magnitudes; (c) distances are invariant to adding multiples of w to… (design the check: move a point parallel to the hyperplane and confirm its distance is unchanged).

Solution
Property (c) is the geometric soul of the SVM: moving along the hyperplane costs nothing; only normal-direction movement changes the classification score — which is why the margin direction w is everything.

Exercise 2. From line to margin to classifier: given w = (−1, 4), b = 2, compute (a) the line y(x₂) as a function of x₁, (b) the two margin lines wᵀx + b = ±1, (c) the signed distances of (0, 0) and (3, 2), (d) which side the classifier assigns each. Then plot all four lines with the two points using matplotlib and check the distances visually (aspect=“equal”).

Solution
The three parallel lines are the whole linear-SVM story: classify by side (sign), measure confidence by distance, and the ±1 family is the margin whose width 2/‖w‖ the SVM maximizes (8.2).

Exercise 3. Norms and their margins: for w = (3, 4), compute ‖w‖₁, ‖w‖₂, ‖w‖∞, and the L2-margin 2/‖w‖₂. Then show numerically that among all vectors achieving the same classification of a fixed dataset, scaling changes ‖w‖₂ — and that adding a perpendicular component changes ‖w‖₂ without changing any sign. Which of these two operations would an SVM’s objective permit (relate to how the margin constraint pins the scale)?

Solution
Scaling changes ‖w‖ (and thus the nominal margin) without changing classifications; adding a perpendicular component changes ‖w‖ and keeps signs for this dataset. The SVM’s canonical constraint — margin lines at ±1 with the support vectors on them — pins the scale so the only freedom left is the direction of w; maximizing 2/‖w‖ then chooses the direction with the fattest cushion. The margin isn’t a formula, it’s the normalization that makes the formula meaningful.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • The sign convention: +1 class sits on the wᵀx + b > 0 side by convention of the labels — sklearn’s decision_function sign follows classes_[1]; always check model.classes_ before interpreting signs (3.3’s ordering note).
  • ‖w‖ appears in denominators everywhere — margins, distances, hinge geometry — so feature scaling is not optional for SVMs: raw-unit features tilt the geometry in invisible ways.
  • Hyperplane ≠ classifier: the zero-set classifies; the scaled (w, b) carries the margin information. Two codebases reporting different (w, b) for the “same” SVM may agree completely after normalization — compare decision_function values, not raw parameters.
  • Projections are linear algebra’s workhorse beyond SVMs: residuals (2.2), PCA scores (9.2), backfitting (6.7) — the same perpendicular decomposition everywhere.
  • d(x₀) = 0 points: points on the hyperplane are classified by tie-breaking convention — in practice measure-zero, but in engineered features (integers) it happens; know your library’s rule.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Not a distance: wᵀx₀ + b is the unscaled score; distances divide by ‖w‖. The printed “distance” changes under rescaling of w — distances don’t.
  2. b doesn’t belong in the margin: the gap between the ±1 planes is purely 2/‖w‖; b shifts the pair, doesn’t widen it.
  3. Classifier needs the label convention: sign gives the side, but which side is “class 1” is a training-time fact (classes_), not derivable from geometry alone.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove the signed-distance formula: for hyperplane H = {x : wᵀx + b = 0} and point x₀, write x₀ = p + t·ŵ (p ∈ H, ŵ = w/‖w‖), solve for t, and confirm t = (wᵀx₀ + b)/‖w‖. Then derive the ±1-planes separation: for x₊ with wᵀx₊ + b = +1, dist(x₊, H₋) = 2/‖w‖. Finally explain why SVM optimization may equivalently maximize 2/‖w‖ or minimize ‖w‖²/2 — and why the quadratic form is preferred by solvers.

Hint Enforce wᵀp + b = 0 on the foot point p; subtract the two plane equations for the margin.
Solution Substituting x₀ = p + tŵ: wᵀx₀ + b = (wᵀp + b) + t‖w‖ = t‖w‖, so t = (wᵀx₀ + b)/‖w‖ — the formula, proved. For the margin: x₊ satisfies wᵀx₊ + b = 1; distance to H₋ is |wᵀx₊ + b + 1|/‖w‖ = 2/‖w‖. Maximizing 2/‖w‖ ⇔ minimizing ‖w‖; the objective ‖w‖²/2 is quadratic — smooth, strictly convex, differentiable everywhere (no square-root kink) — which makes the SVM a clean QP for standard solvers. Every SVM formulation you’ll meet is this geometry plus a slack budget.

Q2. Angles between hyperplanes: the cosine of the angle between two hyperplanes equals |w₁ᵀw₂|/(‖w₁‖‖w₂‖). Implement it, then investigate: for two hyperplanes both separating a given linearly-separable dataset, compute their pairwise angles — are all separating hyperplanes within a narrow cone? Connect the cone’s width to the data’s margin geometry, and explain what this predicts about how much freedom the SVM’s margin maximization actually has (foreshadowing why “the” maximal-margin hyperplane is unique).

Solution
All separating planes lie in a cone around the “true” boundary whose width is set by the data’s gap: wide margins → narrow cones (little freedom — the maximal-margin choice barely matters); narrow margins → wide cones (many hyperplanes fit, and the choice among them is genuine model selection). The SVM resolves this freedom by fiat — max 2/‖w‖ — which is why its solution is unique even when infinitely many hyperplanes classify perfectly.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (geometry × 2.x regression). Two views of one object: a linear regression fit ŷ = wᵀx + b also defines a hyperplane in feature space. On a 2-D dataset, fit OLS and draw (a) the regression’s prediction surface as a contour plot, (b) the zero-set hyperplane. For a constructed binary target (sign of a linear function), compare the OLS-zero-set to the maximal-margin hyperplane (8.2 preview — use sklearn’s LinearSVC with huge C). Quantify the angle between them and explain in margin terms why they differ.

C2 (geometry × 6.4 splines × 8.4 kernels preview). Bases make geometry flexible: expand x = (x₁, x₂) into the quadratic basis (x₁², x₂², √2·x₁x₂, x₁, x₂, 1) — a hyperplane in the expanded space is a conic section in the original. Verify numerically: fit a linear hyperplane in the expanded space to XOR-like data, map its decision boundary back to the original 2-D plane (contour of φ(x)ᵀw = 0), and observe the ellipse/hyperbola family. This is the kernel trick’s entire promise — hyperplanes upstairs, curves downstairs — and the reason 8.4 never materializes the basis.

7. Summary

  • Hyperplane = w (normal) + b (offset); zero-set classifies; signed distance = (wᵀx + b)/‖w‖.
  • Canonical convention: support vectors at wᵀx + b = ±1 → margin = 2/‖w‖ — scale pinned by constraint.
  • Maximizing 2/‖w‖ ⇔ minimizing ‖w‖²/2 — the quadratic objective solvers love.
  • Signs classify; scaled magnitudes score; both are unit-sensitive — standardize.
  • Separating hyperplanes form a cone; margin maximization picks its unique fattest member.
  • Basis expansion moves hyperplanes upstairs — curves downstairs, kernels to come.
  • The first SVM proper: make the margin maximal on separable data — 8.2 The maximal-margin classifier.
