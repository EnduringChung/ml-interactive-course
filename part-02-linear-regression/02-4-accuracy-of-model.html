2.4 Accuracy of the Full Model

Beyond individual coefficients, we judge the whole fit: how close are predictions to reality (RSE), and what fraction of the response’s variation does the model explain (\(R^2\))? Both have in-sample and out-of-sample versions — and the gap between them is the heart of model evaluation.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Two classic in-sample summaries of a fit with \(p\) predictors and \(n\) observations:

\[\text{RSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{RSS}}{n - p - 1}} \quad\text{(typical prediction miss, in y-units)} \qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}}{\text{TSS}} \quad\text{(fraction of variance explained)}\]

where \(\text{RSS} = \sum (y_i - \hat y_i)^2\) and \(\text{TSS} = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2\). RSE is absolute (compare to σ), \(R^2\) is relative (compare to the mean-model’s floor of 0 and the perfect fit’s ceiling of 1).

The variance-explained identity. With an intercept, total variation splits exactly:

\[\underbrace{\sum (y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{TSS}} = \underbrace{\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2}_{\text{ESS, explained}} + \underbrace{\sum (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}_{\text{RSS, unexplained}}\]

2. Concept Questions

  1. Your model’s RSE is 3.2 and y ranges from 40 to 60. Is this a good model? What extra number do you need?
  2. Why is in-sample \(R^2\) never decreased by adding a predictor — even a column of pure noise?
  3. What does out-of-sample \(R^2 < 0\) mean, and which two models does it compare?
  4. RSE divides by \(n - p - 1\) while RMSE divides by \(n\). Which is the honest estimate of σ, and when do they converge?
  5. Your in-sample \(R^2 = 0.9\), test \(R^2 = 0.55\). Name the phenomenon and two standard remedies.
Answers
  1. Depends on σ, not the range alone: RSE ≈ σ means you’re at the noise floor (2.1); RSE ≫ σ means real structure is unexplained. Range (40–60 → SD maybe ~4) gives context: missing 3.2 when the total SD is ~4 is barely better than predicting the mean. You need an estimate of σ (replicates, domain knowledge).
  2. Least squares minimizes RSS, so RSS weakly decreases with any added column — noise columns get tiny fitted coefficients that shave a little RSS. \(R^2\)’s denominator doesn’t move. This monotone creep is why adjusted \(R^2\) (or CV) exists; more in 2.5/Part 5.
  3. The test-set version compares your model against the test set’s own mean: \(R^2_{\text{test}} = 1 - \sum (y_{te} - \hat y_{te})^2 / \sum (y_{te} - \bar y_{te})^2\). Negative = your model predicts worse than just predicting the test mean — the model (fit elsewhere) has negative transfer to this distribution or is simply terrible.
  4. RSE (df-corrected) estimates σ̂ unbiasedly; RMSE(n) is the descriptive in-sample miss. They converge as \(n \gg p\). Report RSE (or test RMSE), not in-sample RMSE, when the audience cares about truth.
  5. Overfitting (in-sample optimism). Remedies: more data, regularization/simpler model (Part 5), and honest evaluation via CV (4.3).

Exercise 2. Demonstrate the noise-collector theorem: for the data above, append (a) a genuine second predictor correlated with the signal, (b) a pure-noise column, and (c) five pure-noise columns. Track in-sample R² and 5-fold out-of-sample R² across the four models. Which number climbs monotonically, and which tells the truth?

Solution
In-sample R² only ever rises — five noise columns “explain” variance that pure chance aligned with y. CV R² is flat or falling: out-of-sample, the noise coefficients are random directions that add variance, not signal. This single experiment is the entire business case for honest validation (Part 4) and for adjusted metrics/penalties (Part 5).

Exercise 3. Build regression_report(y_true, y_pred, k_params) returning a dict with MSE, RMSE, RSE (df-corrected), MAE, and out-of-sample R² computed against the given y’s mean. Test it in three regimes — good model, underfit model, extrapolated predictions — and write one sentence naming each regime from the numbers alone.

Solution
Reading: “good” has RSE ≈ 1 ≈ σ and healthy positive R²; “underfit” has R² ≈ 0 (no better than the mean); “extrapolated” has negative R² and an RMSE far above σ — the fit is fine but the domain is wrong. Numbers diagnose regimes only when you know what σ and R² = 0 mean.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • \(R^2\) has no universal “good” — it compares against your response’s variance. In noisy biology, \(R^2 = 0.2\) can be a triumph; in controlled physics, 0.95 can be embarrassing. Context or σ, always.
  • Out-of-sample \(R^2\) is computed against the test mean — a different denominator than training. Two reported R²s with different test sets are not directly comparable; prefer test RMSE/MSE for comparisons.
  • Adjusted \(R^2\) charges each predictor with a df penalty: \(1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}\); it can decrease when noise columns join. It’s a better in-sample guide, but CV is the real referee.
  • Comparing \(R^2\) across transformed responses (\(y\) vs \(\log y\)) is invalid — different denominators, different games. Compare test RMSE on the same scale (back-transform with care: exponentiating log-predictions biases; use smearing or accept the median-level prediction).
  • RSE estimates σ only under correct specification — a quadratic truth fit with a line inflates σ̂ (2.10); your “noise floor” estimate absorbs your model’s bias.
  • \(R^2\) says nothing about calibration in y: predictions can be well-correlated with y yet systematically offset; plot ŷ vs y (0.4).

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove TSS = ESS + RSS for least squares with an intercept, starting from \(y_i - \bar y = (\hat y_i - \bar y) + (y_i - \hat y_i)\) and showing the cross-term vanishes. Which first-order condition kills the cross-term, and why does the identity fail without an intercept (or with ridge, Part 5)?

Hint Expand the sum of squares; the cross-term is \(\sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)(y_i - \hat y_i)\).
Solution Expand: \(\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2 = \sum(\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + \sum(y_i - \hat y_i)^2 + 2\sum(\hat y_i - \bar y)(y_i - \hat y_i)\). The cross-term equals \(2\,\hat{y}^\top (I - H) y\)-type expressions; concretely, OLS with intercept implies residuals are orthogonal to the fitted values: \(\sum \hat r_i \hat y_i = 0\) (follows from \(\sum \hat r_i = 0\) and \(\sum \hat r_i x_i = 0\), the two first-order conditions). Hence ESS \(= \sum \hat y_i^2 - n\bar y^2\) and TSS = ESS + RSS. Without an intercept the condition \(\sum \hat r_i = 0\) is lost, so fitted values need not be orthogonal to residuals — and \(R^2\) computed as 1 − RSS/TSS can even go negative in-sample. Regularized fits (ridge) similarly aren’t orthogonal projections, so the decomposition and the tidy R² story are OLS-with-intercept privileges.

Q2. Two models on the same n = 40 dataset: A has in-sample R² = 0.70 with 1 predictor; B has in-sample R² = 0.74 with 8 predictors. (a) Compute adjusted R² for both. (b) Then decide the winner by CV, implementing it. (c) At what sample size would B’s raw R² edge become credible? Answer with code and one paragraph.

Solution
  1. Adjusted R² flips the ranking: B’s 8 parameters must earn their keep and don’t. (b) CV agrees — B adds nothing out-of-sample. (c) The noise columns’ spurious gains shrink like 7/n: only at n in the hundreds does B’s raw-R² edge fall below noise level. Lesson: raw in-sample R² gains of ≈ (extra predictors)/n are the signature of pure chance, not signal.

7. Summary

  • RSE = absolute miss (in σ units); R² = relative variance explained; both in-sample summaries.
  • TSS = ESS + RSS exactly (with intercept OLS) — R² is the explained share.
  • In-sample R² rises with any added column; adjusted R² charges for parameters; CV tells the truth.
  • Out-of-sample R² uses the test mean as its yardstick and can be negative — worse than the mean.
  • Never compare R² across different response scales; compare same-scale RMSE.
  • RSE ≈ σ means you’ve hit the noise floor — stop tuning, get better data.
  • The fit’s average accuracy is settled; next page scales the model to many predictors — with collinearity and confounding in tow: 2.5 Multiple linear regression.
