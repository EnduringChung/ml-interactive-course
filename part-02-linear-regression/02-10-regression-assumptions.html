2.10 Regression Assumptions & Diagnostics

Every SE, t-test, CI, and F-test in Part 2 rests on assumptions: Linearity, Independence, Normality, Equal variance (of errors). Violations don’t just “break the model” abstractly — each one breaks a specific thing (usually inference, sometimes prediction), and each has a signature plot. This page makes the diagnostics reflexive.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The assumptions, what each buys, and what its violation costs:

Assumption Formal statement Needed for Violation’s cost
Linearity \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\) unbiased β̂, meaningful coefficients biased estimates; everything downstream is about the wrong model
Independence errors uncorrelated across rows valid SEs, tests, CIs SEs wildly wrong (often underestimated); p-values meaningless
Normality \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\) exact t/F reference distributions only mild at large n (CLT); matters at small n and for prediction intervals
Equal variance \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = \sigma^2\) SEs, exact tests wrong SEs; OLS no longer most efficient

Key triage: prediction needs linearity (systematic structure captured); inference needs all four.

The diagnostic quartet, on one simulated misspecification each:

Reading guide: residuals-vs-fitted should show a structureless horizontal band (curvature ⇒ nonlinearity; funnel ⇒ heteroscedasticity); QQ should hug the diagonal (S-curves ⇒ heavy tails/skew); scale-location should be flat (trend ⇒ variance grows with the mean).

2. Concept Questions

  1. Which assumption does a U-shaped residuals-vs-fitted plot violate, and what’s the two-column fix?
  2. Your errors are independent but heteroscedastic. Are your β̂ biased? Your SEs? Name the one-line remedy that fixes the SEs.
  3. Why is normality of errors the least worrying of the four at n = 500, but a genuine worry at n = 8?
  4. Residuals are correlated in time order (a drifting instrument). Which printed numbers become lies, and does the slope estimate itself have to be wrong?
  5. Why must diagnostics use residuals, never the raw y?
Answers
  1. Linearity (systematic curvature left unmodeled). Fix: add \(x^2\) (or a spline basis) — two columns in, assumption restored; refit and re-diagnose (2.8).
  2. β̂: unbiased (linearity intact — OLS is still the right center). SEs: wrong (they assume one σ). Remedy: heteroscedasticity-robust SEs — fit(cov_type="HC3") — or model the variance (GLM, 3.6).
  3. At large n, the CLT makes t/F statistics approximately valid regardless of error shape (influence of each single point vanishes). At n = 8, one outlier is the distribution — the t-reference is fiction, CIs undercover, and no CLT rescue exists.
  4. Every SE, t, p, CI — they all assume independent errors; positive serial correlation makes the effective sample size much smaller than n (n = 200 correlated readings ≈ far fewer independent ones). The slope estimate can remain unbiased but its precision claim is fantasy.
  5. Raw y mixes signal f(x) with error: a “non-normal y” can come entirely from a skewed x-distribution with perfectly normal errors. Residuals isolate ε̂ — the only thing the assumptions speak about.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

The regressor being serially correlated as well is the whole story, and it is easy to miss. If x is iid, the score \(u_i = x_i\varepsilon_i\) has \(\text{Cov}(u_i, u_{i+k}) = E[x_i x_{i+k}]E[\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_{i+k}] = 0\) for \(k \neq 0\) — the regressors decorrelate for you, and the naive SE is already right no matter how badly \(\varepsilon\) is correlated. Make x a time series and the \(x_i x_{i+k}\) cross-terms no longer vanish: the effective sample size collapses, and HAC inflates the SE by roughly 2× here (and 1.5–2× across seeds). This is exactly why the “effective n shrinks” claim applies to panel and time-series designs and quietly does not apply to designed experiments (randomized treatment, iid subjects) — the design, not the noise, does the work.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Build diagnose(fit) — a function taking a statsmodels fit and returning a dict of verdicts: residual–fitted curvature score (correlation of residuals with fitted²), funnel score (Spearman |ρ| of |residuals| vs fitted), and normality p-value (Jarque–Bera). Run it on the four synthetic datasets from Section 1 and confirm it flags the right problem in each. What can a verdict dictionary not tell you that the plot can?

Solution
The scores separate the cases (curvature high only for “nonlinear”, funnel only for “hetero”, JB only for “heavy-tail”). What the dict can’t show: patterns a scalar compresses away — e.g. one wild leverage point inside a clean cloud scores fine numerically but is obvious in the plot, and residual-vs-fitted plots reveal which region of x misbehaves. Numbers triage; eyes diagnose.

Exercise 2. The repair menu, demonstrated: for each mis-specified fit below, apply the indicated fix and re-run the diagnostic that failed. (a) quadratic truth + linear model → add x²; (b) heteroscedastic → log-transform y (verify the funnel flattens) and compare HC3 SEs; (c) heavy-tailed → report the HC3 SE alongside classical and note which coefficients lose significance.

Solution
Each repair targets the assumption its diagnostic flagged — and the diagnostics after repair verify the fix rather than trusting it.

Exercise 3. Leverage vs outlier. Construct a 30-point dataset where one point has extreme x (high leverage, y consistent with the line) and another has extreme y at typical x (outlier, low leverage). Fit with and without each point; report slope changes. Which point is more dangerous to the slope? Then connect to Cook’s distance (get_influence().cooks_distance[0]) — which point does it flag?

Solution
Deletion only makes sense against the design the point was actually in: np.delete(x, 0) on the base 30 removes an ordinary random point and tells you nothing about the special point. Note the asymmetry — removing the leverage point barely moves the slope (it agreed with the line, so it was not doing any work), while removing the outlier restores the baseline almost exactly. Leverage alone is not influence; a high-leverage point only hurts when its y is also discrepant, which is precisely the combination Cook’s distance encodes.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Diagnostics are for the training residuals, decisions are for the study — after repair (transform, robust SEs), refit and re-diagnose; one pass is not a workflow.
  • Testing normality with p-values on residuals (Shapiro/JB) is a weak tool: at small n it lacks power, at large n it flags trivial departures. The QQ plot, read as a shape, is more informative than any single p-value.
  • Independence is the assumption you cannot plot away — it comes from the design (time order, clustering, relatedness). Residual-vs-order plots and HAC/mixed models (the drill) manage known structure; unknown structure needs better data.
  • Heteroscedasticity has two fixes with different goals: robust SEs fix your uncertainty about β̂ (inference), WLS/GLM/log-transform fix efficiency and prediction intervals (the model). Pick by goal.
  • Leverage ≠ outlier ≠ influential (Exercise 3): check Cook’s distance (or DFFITS) rather than residual magnitude, especially before deleting anything — deletion is a modeling decision to report, not hygiene.
  • Don’t diagnose the test set: split integrity (0.5) applies — diagnostics belong to the modeling loop on training data; the test set is spent once.
  • Multicollinearity (2.5) is sometimes listed as a fifth assumption — it is not an assumption violation (the model can be true!) but a data condition that inflates SEs; diagnose with VIFs, not residual plots.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the exact variance function of OLS residuals for a binary-style Bernoulli-mean response and show it is inherently heteroscedastic (\(\text{Var}(\varepsilon \mid x) = p(x)(1-p(x))\)), peaking at p = 0.5. Use this to explain why linear-probability models always show funnel diagnostics and why the logistic/GLM view (3.2, 3.6) resolves rather than repairs the problem.

Hint If y ∈ {0,1} with mean p(x), write y = p(x) + ε and compute Var(ε|x).
Solution y ∈ {0,1}, E[y|x] = p(x) ⇒ ε = y − p(x) takes values −p(x) and 1−p(x), so Var(ε|x) = p(x)(1−p(x)) — maximal (0.25) at p = 0.5, shrinking to 0 at the extremes. The linear-probability model therefore cannot satisfy equal-variance for any σ: the funnel is structural, baked into binary outcomes. No transform or robust SE “fixes” the model; the likelihood that matches the data-generating mechanism (Bernoulli with logit link — logistic regression) makes the variance function part of the model. This is the template for GLMs generally: choose the variance function from the response’s nature, then the mean model — the organizing idea of 3.6.

Q2. A dataset has grouped structure: 30 patients × 5 repeated measurements (150 rows), with patient-level random drift. (a) Demonstrate empirically that OLS SEs are too small (simulate 500 such datasets; compare the empirical SD of β̂ across simulations to the average classical SE). (b) Show that simply averaging each patient’s rows (n = 30) restores honest SEs at the cost of power, and (c) name the two standard remedies that keep all 150 rows (cluster-robust SEs; mixed models) — verify (a)’s lie and one remedy numerically.

Solution
The classical SE assumes 150 independent errors; with 30 patients’ shared drift, effective information ≈ 30-ish units, so the true SD of β̂ is several× the naive SE. Remedies keeping all rows: fit(cov_type="cluster") with patient IDs (fixes inference) and linear mixed models (random patient intercepts — models the structure, often more efficient). Averaging (b) is the transparent sanity check that exposes the lie.

7. Summary

  • L-I-N-E: linearity (unbiasedness), independence (valid SEs), normality (small-n exactness), equal variance (efficient, valid SEs).
  • Residuals — never raw y — are the diagnostic subject: residuals-vs-fitted, QQ, scale-location.
  • Each violation has a repair menu: +x²/splines, transforms, robust/HAC/cluster SEs, GLMs.
  • Influence ≠ leverage ≠ outlier; Cook’s distance is the deletion diagnostic.
  • Serial/grouped correlation silently multiplies your p-values’ confidence — design awareness, not plots, is the defense.
  • Diagnose → repair → re-diagnose; and never diagnose on the test set.
  • Part 2’s regression toolkit is complete. Part 3 turns to categorical responses, where the equal-variance assumption dies by definition — beginning with what classification is and how it’s scored: 3.1 Classification fundamentals.
