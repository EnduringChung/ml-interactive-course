2.2 Simple Linear Regression

Simple linear regression fits a straight line \(\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x\) by least squares. It is the reference point for every model in this course: transparent, closed-form, and already rich enough to demonstrate estimation, uncertainty, and mis-use.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Least squares chooses coefficients minimizing the residual sum of squares \(\text{RSS} = \sum_i (y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i)^2\). Minimizing in each parameter (set derivatives to zero) gives the closed forms:

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\]

The slope is the correlation rescaled into y-units per x-unit (1.2); the line always passes through \((\bar x, \bar y)\).

Reading the coefficients. \(\hat\beta_1\): predicted change in \(y\) per one-unit increase in \(x\). \(\hat\beta_0\): prediction at \(x = 0\) — meaningful only if 0 is in (or near) the data’s range; otherwise it is an extrapolated anchor, not a “baseline effect.”

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why does the least-squares line always pass through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\)?
  2. Data in x = Fahrenheit, y = Celsius is fit with slope \(\hat\beta_1\). Refit in x = Kelvin. What happens to the slope and intercept?
  3. Your slope is 4.2 with r = 0.3. What is \(s_y / s_x\) telling you here, and would you call this a “strong” effect?
  4. Two students fit the same data; one forgets the intercept term (forces \(\hat\beta_0 = 0\)). Whose slope changes, and in which direction is it biased if \(\bar y\) and \(\bar x\) are both positive?
  5. What is the RSS of the perfect-intercept model (\(\hat\beta_1 = 0\))? (Connects to 2.1 Q1.)
Answers
  1. \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x\) rearranges to \(\bar y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 \bar x\) — a direct consequence of the first-order condition in \(\beta_0\) (residuals sum to zero).
  2. Kelvin = Fahrenheit-scaled shift: \(x_K = (x_F + 459.67) \times \frac{5}{9}\). Slope rescales by the x-unit factor: \(\hat\beta_1^{(K)} = \hat\beta_1^{(F)} \cdot \frac{9}{5}\); intercept absorbs the shift via the point \((\bar x, \bar y)\). Slope is unit-sensitive; correlation r is not.
  3. \(s_y/s_x = 4.2/0.3 = 14\) — the slope is large only because y varies 14× more than x. “Strong” is about r (or standardized slope), not \(\hat\beta_1\) alone: r = 0.3 is a weak linear signal with a steep unit conversion. Always report effect in context: 4.2 y-units per x-unit with uncertainty.
  4. Without an intercept, \(\hat\beta_1\) must do double duty (slope + level), and the first-order condition \(\sum r_i = 0\) is lost. With positive \(\bar x, \bar y\), the no-intercept slope is biased upward — it’s pulled toward \(y/x\) ratios rather than the local slope. Force an intercept unless a theory demands the line pass through the origin (and then still check).
  5. \(\text{RSS} = \sum(y_i - \bar y)^2 = \text{TSS}\) — the mean-model. Every slope model is judged by how much it shrinks this number; that comparison becomes \(R^2\) in 2.4.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Compute the least-squares slope and intercept from scratch (sums only, no polyfit/sklearn) for the dataset below, then verify against np.polyfit. Report RSS and compare it to the TSS of the intercept-only model.

Solution
RSS ≪ TSS here: the line explains most of the variation. That ratio — computed again with proper degrees of freedom — becomes \(R^2\) and \(R^2_{\text{adj}}\) in 2.4.

Exercise 2. Regression to the mean, experienced: simulate 200 parents’ heights and children’s heights as child = 0.7*parent + 68 + noise (units: inches). Fit child ~ parent. Report the slope and predicted child height for a parent at 62 in and at 76 in. Explain — in one sentence each — why the predictions sit closer to the population mean than the inputs, and why this is a statistical fact and not a biological force.

Solution
Predictions are pulled toward \(\bar y\) because slope < 1: a parent 4 in below the mean is predicted only ~2.8 in below-average child. The mechanism is pure statistics — the parent’s deviation partially reflects parent-side luck (noise), which doesn’t repeat in the child. Any noisy pair with \(|\rho| < 1\) regresses to the mean; misreading it as causal “correction” is the classic historical error (Galton’s own).

Exercise 3. Prediction interval preview by simulation: fit the line on n = 40 points (\(\sigma = 2\) known truth), then simulate the empirical distribution of predictions at x = 5 over 2,000 re-generated training sets. Report the SD of predictions and compare it to (a) the SD of a single new observation at x = 5 (≈ σ) and (b) the SE of the mean response. Which interval is wider — confidence or prediction — and why?

Solution
The fitted mean response at x = 5 is much more stable than any individual point: individual outcomes add irreducible σ on top of the mean’s uncertainty. Hence the prediction interval (for a new y) is always wider than the confidence interval (for the mean) — quantified properly in 2.3–2.4.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • np.polyfit returns highest-degree first ([slope, intercept] for deg 1) — reversing them is a top silent bug. np.polyval([b1, b0], x_new) matches.
  • sklearn wants 2-D X: model.fit(x, y) with 1-D x raises — x.reshape(-1, 1) or x[:, None] (0.2 broadcasting).
  • Extrapolation — the line continues with full confidence beyond the data; the only protection is range-checking (2.1).
  • Outliers have leverage proportional to \((x - \bar x)^2\) — far-in-x points can rotate the whole line (Anscombe III in 1.2); check the fit with and without suspects.
  • Slope ≠ effect without design: in observational data \(\hat\beta_1\) is an association per unit x; confounding can give any sign (2.5, 2.6).
  • Correlation of residuals is the real assumption — independence, not normality, is what makes the classic SEs valid; time-order or cluster structure breaks it quietly (2.10).

What’s wrong, and the fixed version
  1. polyfit order reversed: it returns [highest-degree, ..., constant], so b0 here is the slope and b1 the intercept — every printed label lies. Fix: b1, b0 = np.polyfit(...) or unpack with names.
  2. Reversed-coefficient prediction then residuals: garbage in, garbage out; also the residual-mean check only works when the intercept is present (it should be ≈ 0 numerically). Fix names, then check.
  3. Self-congratulatory metric: corr(fitted, x) is always ≈ ±1 by construction — it measures the line’s steepness, not fit quality. Fix: compare against y (residual-based: RSS vs TSS, or corr(ŷ, y)).

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove that regressing standardized $y^* $ on standardized \(x^*\) yields slope = r and intercept = 0, and derive from it the identity \(\hat\beta_1 = r\, s_y / s_x\). Why does this make the slope unit-sensitive while r is not?

Hint Standardized variables have mean 0, variance 1; apply the closed-form slope.
Solution With \(x^* = (x-\bar x)/s_x\), \(y^* = (y - \bar y)/s_y\): slope \(= \frac{\sum x_i^* y_i^*}{\sum x_i^{*2}} = \frac{(n-1)\,\text{cov}(x^*,y^*)}{(n-1)\,\text{var}(x^*)} = r\) (both variances are 1); intercept \(= \bar y^* - r\bar x^* = 0\). Rescaling \(y^* \to y\) multiplies the slope by \(s_y\); rescaling \(x^* \to x\) divides it by \(s_x\) — giving \(\hat\beta_1 = r s_y/s_x\). So \(\hat\beta_1\) mixes a dimensionless association strength (r) with a unit conversion (\(s_y/s_x\)): change x to centimeters and the slope divides by 2.54 with no change in reality. This is why cross-study comparisons report standardized coefficients or r, and why “bigger slope = stronger effect” is meaningless across unit systems.

Q2. Under what exact conditions does the least-squares slope equal the causal effect of x on y? Use a simulation where x is correlated with an unobserved driver of y to show \(\hat\beta_1\) ≠ true effect, and connect the size of the gap to the confounder’s correlation with x and y.

Solution

Causal equality requires no confounding: \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0\) — the error (which contains all other causes) uncorrelated with x; in a randomized experiment this holds by design, in observational data it’s an assumption. Simulation:

The naive slope overshoots: part of z’s influence on y rides in through corr(x, z). First-order intuition: \(\hat\beta_1 \approx \text{true} + \beta_z \cdot \delta\) where δ is the x–confounder association. The general cure is adjusting for measured confounders (2.5, 2.6); unmeasured ones limit every observational claim.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (SLR × 1.5 Resampling). Bootstrap the slope of the Exercise 1 dataset (5,000 resamples) and compare the bootstrap SD of \(\hat\beta_1\) to the formula-based SE from 2.3. Now inject one high-leverage outlier (x = 40, y = 10) and redo both. Which SE detects the fragility first, and what does this say about formula-SE trust on real data?

C2 (SLR × 0.3 pandas). Load the cohort-style frame: 4 groups with different slopes on a shared x. Fit (a) one pooled line, (b) separate lines per group via groupby. Under what parameter constellation does the pooled slope equal a (weighted) average of group slopes, and when can its sign contradict all of them? (This is Simpson’s paradox armed with slopes — resolved with dummies in 2.6.)

7. Summary

  • Least squares has closed forms; the line passes through \((\bar x, \bar y)\); slope = \(r\,s_y/s_x\).
  • np.polyfit orders coefficients highest-power-first; sklearn wants 2-D X — mechanical details that produce silent bugs.
  • Intercept = prediction at x = 0: interpret only when 0 is in-range.
  • Slope is unit-sensitive, r is not; neither is causal without design or adjustment.
  • Regression to the mean follows from any \(|r| < 1\) — statistical, not substantive.
  • Confidence interval (mean response) is always narrower than prediction interval (new point): irreducible σ sits on top.
  • A line is a claim; next page asks how precisely the data pins that claim down: 2.3 Accuracy of the coefficients.
