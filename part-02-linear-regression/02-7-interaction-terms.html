2.7 Interaction Terms

So far every model assumed additive effects: each predictor shifts y by a fixed amount regardless of the others. An interaction lets the effect of one predictor depend on another — the model’s most commonly forgotten question, and the difference between “drug works” and “drug works in women under 60.”

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Add a product column:

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3\, x_1 x_2 + \varepsilon\]

Now the effect of \(x_1\) is \(\beta_1 + \beta_3 x_2\) — a function of \(x_2\), not a number. If \(\beta_3 \ne 0\), the lines/planes tilt against each other; there is no single “effect of \(x_1\)” to report.

Read the coefficients directly: male slope = dose = 0.80; female slope = dose + dose:female = 0.80 − 0.12 = 0.68; the female level offset at dose 0 = female = −1.0.

2. Concept Questions

  1. In yield ~ temp * conc, what exactly does the conc coefficient mean now? Why is it “the effect of conc at temp = 0”?
  2. Your interaction p-value is 0.4 but a main effect’s p-value is 0.001. May you drop the interaction and keep the main effect? May you drop the non-significant main effect and keep a significant interaction?
  3. Interaction vs correlation between predictors (2.5 collinearity) — what’s the difference in what each breaks?
  4. The model says the drug×sex interaction is 0.5 (p = 0.01). A headline says “drug works better in women.” What extra number does the headline need?
  5. Why do people center variables before forming interactions, and what changes vs stays the same?
Answers
  1. With the product term present, the conc effect is \(\beta_{conc} + \beta_{temp:conc}\,\text{temp}\) — it has no single value; by the algebra of “hold everything else fixed,” the printed \(\beta_{conc}\) is the conc effect evaluated at temp = 0. Since temp = 0 may be outside the data, that number can be meaningless — center temp and it becomes “at average temp.”
  2. Drop the interaction: yes (back to the additive model — standard hierarchy downward is fine after a null result). Drop a main effect while keeping its interaction: no — the hierarchy principle: an interaction contains the main effects, and removing them forces the model through artificial constraints (e.g., zero effect at the other variable’s zero) that bias everything.
  3. Collinearity is about redundant information (identifying coefficients; inflates SEs); interaction is about structure (whether effects multiply/moderate each other). Two collinear predictors can have zero interaction; two interacting predictors can be perfectly independent. Fixing one never fixes the other.
  4. The two slopes (or the two marginal effects at meaningful values) with CIs: “slope in women = a (b to c), in men = d (e to f)” — the interaction coefficient alone doesn’t say which group is bigger, and “better” needs the scale (also: a significant interaction can mean “less bad in men”!).
  5. Centering (subtracting means) makes main-effect coefficients interpretable at typical values of the moderator and reduces the correlation between the product column and its ingredients (numerical conditioning). It does not change fitted values, R², the interaction coefficient, or its test (with raw quadratic terms, tiny rounding aside).

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Simulate a cross-over interaction: treatment helps at low severity, harms at high severity (y = 10 + 1.0·sev − 0.8·trt + 0.2·sev·trt + noise). Fit the interaction model; compute the treatment effect at severity 2, 5, 8 with CIs (delta-method-free: refit with re-centered severity); find the severity where the effect crosses zero and verify by plotting both lines.

Solution
Re-centering the moderator converts “effect at severity s” into a main effect with proper SE and CI — no delta method needed. The cross-over point (≈ 4 here) is where the treatment recommendation flips; interactions are how subgroup medicine becomes honest instead of a fishing expedition.

Exercise 2. Test the parallel-slopes assumption from 2.6 Exercise 3 properly: fit the age × tissue model on that cohort, use anova_lm to compare against the additive model, and draw the fitted age-lines per tissue on one plot. Then write the sentence the additive model would have printed if the interaction were real but ignored — what goes wrong in it?

Solution
The pooled slope is a precision-weighted average of different per-group slopes; an “age effect” headline from the additive model would be an artifact of group composition — Simpson-adjacent, and invisible without the interaction test.

Exercise 3. Continuous × continuous as a surface: fit yield ~ temp * conc (data regenerated below), then plot the marginal effect of conc (with 95% CI) as a function of temp using the re-centering trick at a grid of temperatures. At which temperatures is conc’s effect significantly positive? This “moderated effect plot” is the professional alternative to staring at coefficient tables.

Solution
The effect grows linearly with temp (that IS the interaction) and its CI clears zero everywhere here — but the plot is what a collaborator can read: “the concentration benefit ranges from ~1.5 at 10° to ~3 at 40°.” Tables report; plots convince.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Hierarchy principle: keep main effects whenever their interaction is present (dropping them constrains effects at the origin and biases estimates). Adding a non-significant interaction downward (simplifying) is fine; building interaction-only models is not.
  • “No significant interaction” ≠ “no interaction” — power for interactions is famously low (you’re estimating a difference of slopes); absence of evidence again (1.4). Check what effect size the CI rules out.
  • Interpretability of main effects dies with the interaction — the printed β̂_x is the effect at moderator = 0, often outside data. Center, or report effects at grid values (Exercise 1/3 pattern).
  • Polynomial terms are self-interactions: x² is “x interacting with x” (curvature), x³ more so — everything on this page (centering, hierarchy, interpretation at values) applies to 2.8.
  • Multiplicative hypotheses hide in logs: log(y) ~ x * g interactions are ratios of ratios — a significant interaction can mean “proportional effect differs,” which looks different back on the raw scale.
  • Multiple interactions multiply the search space — each added product is another test (Part 12’s problem in miniature); pre-specify the interactions theory predicts rather than sweeping all pairs.

What’s wrong, and the fixed version
  1. Two subgroup fits test slopes informally and underpowered: comparing two p-values (“significant here, not there”) is not an interaction test — differences of significant/non-significant are themselves noisy. Fix: one model with dose * sex, interaction tested once with full power.
  2. dose + dose:sex violates hierarchy: no sex main effect forces both groups’ lines through the same intercept — a strong, unjustified constraint that biases the interaction estimate itself.
  3. “+1.0 for everyone” contradicts the model being fit: with an interaction, the dose effect is slope + interaction×sex — the sentence is only true for sex = 0 (and the printed main effect is at sex = 0 by construction).

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Show algebraically that centering both predictors before forming the product leaves the fitted values, R², and the interaction coefficient unchanged, and derive exactly what happens to the main-effect coefficients. Then state the one situation in which centering changes conclusions.

Hint Write \(x_1 = \tilde x_1 + m_1\) (m = mean), expand the product term, and match coefficients between parameterizations.
Solution With \(x_1 = \tilde x_1 + m_1\), \(x_2 = \tilde x_2 + m_2\): \(x_1x_2 = \tilde x_1\tilde x_2 + m_2\tilde x_1 + m_1\tilde x_2 + m_1m_2\). Substituting and regrouping shows the new model is a reparameterization of the same column space (each new column is a combination of old columns), so fitted values and R² are identical; the interaction coefficient is unchanged (\(\tilde\beta_{12} = \beta_{12}\)) while new mains absorb shifts: \(\tilde\beta_1 = \beta_1 + m_2\beta_{12}\) — precisely “the x₁ effect at \(x_2 = m_2\).” The SEs of the mains change (they estimate different quantities at different points). Conclusions change only when you interpret the main effects: at moderator = 0 (raw, possibly absurd) vs moderator = mean (centered, meaningful). Same model, different sentences — which is why centering is an interpretation tool, not a statistics fix.

Q2. An interaction is significant in a discovery cohort (n = 400) and you plan a confirmatory cohort (n = 400). Given that interaction power is low, design the confirmation: what is tested, what alpha, what counts as replication (same sign? CI overlap? pre-registered effect size?), and what does the “winner’s curse” of the discovery estimate imply for the expected confirmatory effect size? Demonstrate the winner’s curse by simulation.

Solution
Design: (1) test the same, pre-registered interaction in the confirmation cohort at one-sided α = 0.05 in the pre-stated direction; (2) replication = significant and same sign, ideally with effect size inside the discovery CI’s plausible range; CI overlap alone is far too weak (overlapping CIs are consistent with significant differences); (3) the winner’s curse: conditional on being selected for p < 0.05 under the null, the discovery estimate averages ~1 SE in magnitude (simulation above: false hits with mean |β̂| ≈ 0.1 SD against truth 0) — so shrink expectations: the confirmatory estimate will typically be smaller than the discovery headline even when real. Report both cohorts’ estimates side by side, never the discovery estimate alone as “the effect.”

6. Complex Questions

C1 (interaction × 2.6 Categorical × 1.2 Simpson). Construct data where: pooled slope is positive, every group’s slope is negative, and the group×x interaction is significant. This is Simpson’s paradox with heterogeneous slopes — show the three nested models (pooled, dummies, dummies×x) and write which model each of three stakeholders (hospital administrator, per-site clinician, meta-analyst) should read.

C2 (interaction × 4.6 Tuning preview). Generate data with a true interaction and compare the out-of-sample performance of (a) the additive model, (b) the correctly specified interaction model, (c) the all-pairs-interactions model (x1:x2, x1:x3, x2:x3, x1:x2:x3 with 4 predictors), across n = 40, 100, 400. Which model wins at each n, and what does this tell you about interaction hunting as a function of sample size — before you ever look at a p-value?

7. Summary

  • An interaction makes an effect a function: β₃ ≠ 0 means “the effect of x₁ depends on x₂.”
  • Report effects at meaningful moderator values (re-center and read main effects) — never the raw table alone.
  • Hierarchy principle: keep main effects with their interaction; simplify downward only.
  • Subgroup p-value comparisons are not interaction tests; one product term, tested once, has the power.
  • Centering changes interpretation, never fit; polynomial curvature is interaction with itself.
  • Interaction power is low; absence of evidence needs the CI’s effect-size statement.
  • Linearity itself is now the last standing structural assumption — 2.8 Nonlinear feature transformations bends the line without leaving the linear-model world.
