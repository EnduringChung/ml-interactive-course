2.9 KNN Regression

K-nearest-neighbors regression abandons functional form entirely: to predict a point, average the responses of its k closest training points. It is the prototype nonparametric method — flexible, assumption-light, and the cleanest lens on the flexibility–data trade before Part 4 formalizes it.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The algorithm for predicting at query point \(x_0\):

  1. Compute the distance from \(x_0\) to every training point.
  2. Take the \(k\) nearest.
  3. Predict \(\hat{y} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i \in N_k(x_0)} y_i\) — their average (optionally distance-weighted).

One hyperparameter, \(k\), controls everything: \(k = 1\) reproduces the training data exactly (maximally flexible); \(k = n\) predicts the global mean everywhere (maximally rigid).

2. Concept Questions

  1. What does KNN with k = n predict everywhere, and which model from Part 2 is it identical to?
  2. Why does KNN require feature scaling, while OLS arguably doesn’t?
  3. Your KNN has excellent training predictions and poor test predictions. Which end of the k dial are you on, and what single change is the first thing to try?
  4. In 1-D with n = 100 and k = 10, roughly what fraction of the x-range does each neighborhood cover? What happens to that fraction as dimensions grow at fixed n and k?
  5. KNN’s fitted curve is wiggly at k = 5 but smooth at k = 50 — restate that sentence in bias–variance language.
Answers
  1. The global average \(\bar y\) at every query — identical to the intercept-only regression of 2.1/2.2 (whose training RSS = TSS). The two “models” at the extremes of flexibility are the same object.
  2. KNN’s only notion of “near” is a distance metric in raw coordinates; a feature measured in thousands (gene counts) swamps one measured in fractions (GC content), so the neighborhood becomes 1-D along the biggest-scaled axis. OLS multiplies each feature by its own learned coefficient — scale is absorbed by β̂ (up to conditioning) — so it is scale-equivariant, not scale-blind.
  3. Small k (overfitting: variance dominant). First change: increase k — then validate properly (4.6); scaling too if features aren’t comparable.
  4. With uniformly spread points, k = 10 of 100 points spans ~10% of the range. In d dimensions with fixed n and k, the same 10 points live in a shell whose radius explodes (concentration of distances) — neighborhoods become huge relative to any one axis: the curse of dimensionality. KNN degrades toward the global mean unless n grows exponentially in d.
  5. k = 5: low bias (local averages track the true curve) but high variance (each neighborhood’s few points swing the estimate) — wiggly. k = 50: high bias (averages over genuine structure) but low variance (many points stabilize it) — smooth. The dial trades one against the other, exactly the 4.4 decomposition.

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Implement full 2-D KNN regression by hand (vectorized distances, argsort, averaging) and verify exact agreement with KNeighborsRegressor on 20 random queries with k = 7. Then switch to distance weighting in both and re-verify (weights ∝ 1/d, handling d = 0 by falling back to that point alone).

Solution
Agreement to float precision (sklearn’s weighting is the same 1/d scheme). Writing KNN once makes its failure modes concrete: ties, zero distances, and the cost — every query scans all n training points (no “fit” to speak of; the data is the model).

Exercise 2 — KNN vs OLS under two truths. Generate 100 training points under (a) a linear truth and (b) a wiggly sinusoidal truth (same noise). Fit OLS and KNN (k chosen by 5-fold CV over k = 1..30) to each; evaluate both on large fresh test sets. Report a 2×2 table of test MSEs. Which method wins under which truth, and what does that table say about “model choice” as a statement about the world?

Solution
Linear truth: OLS wins (KNN wastes effort estimating zero curvature). Wiggly truth: KNN wins (OLS’s bias dominates). Neither method is “better” — methods are hypotheses about structure; CV chooses the hypothesis the data supports. This 2×2 is the whole model-selection story in miniature (Part 4).

Exercise 3 — the curse, quantified. Generate data with signal only in the first 2 of d features (\(y = x_1 x_2 + \varepsilon\), remaining features pure noise, all scaled comparably). For d = 2, 5, 20, fit CV-tuned KNN and OLS on n = 300 and report test MSE on fresh data. Watch KNN degrade with d while OLS merely gets noisier — then explain in neighborhood-radius language.

Solution
In d = 20, “5 nearest neighbors” are no longer local — they’re scattered across a huge radius, so the average mixes unrelated points (bias explodes), while the useful signal lives in a 2-D slice KNN can’t isolate. OLS degrades gracefully: extra noise features only add mild variance to the coefficient estimates. Nonparametric flexibility is bought with locality, and locality is the first casualty of dimension — the reason Part 5’s regularization exists for wide data.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • No training phase, all query cost: prediction is O(n·d) per point (brute force); KD-trees/ball trees help in low d and stop helping in high d. “The model” is a lookup table — deploy size = training size.
  • Scaling is part of the model: put the scaler inside the pipeline so CV/tuning refit it per fold (0.5 leakage rule).
  • k = 1 on training data scores perfectly — reporting it is the canonical overfitting self-own; evaluate on held-out data only.
  • Ties and even k: with classification (Part 3) even k can produce vote ties; with regression ties are harmless (averages), but distance ties in argsort are resolved arbitrarily — reproducibility note for numeric features with many duplicates.
  • Weighted KNN interpolates between k-sensitivity and locality: weights="distance" reduces the discontinuity at neighborhood boundaries but doesn’t fix dimensionality.
  • KNN has no coefficients, no p-values, no global story — it predicts, it doesn’t explain. Using it where inference is the goal is a category error.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Show that uniform KNN regression is a linear smoother: \(\hat{y} = S y\) where \(S = D^{-1} W\) with \(W_{ij} = 1\{x_j \in N_k(x_i)\}\). What are the diagonal entries of \(S\) at k = 1, and how does that make the “training error = 0” fact inevitable? Relate the trace of S to an “effective degrees of freedom” and compute it for k = 1 and k = n.

Hint Each fitted value is an average of a subset of the y’s — write the averaging weights as a matrix.
Solution \(\hat y_i = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j \in N_k(i)} y_j\) ⇒ \(\hat{\mathbf y} = S\mathbf y\) with row i of S equal to 1/k on the k neighbor columns. At k = 1, each row has a single 1 (the point itself is its own neighbor), so S = I: \(\hat y_i = y_i\) exactly — zero training error by identity, and tr(S) = n = “n effective parameters” (maximal flexibility). At k = n, S has every entry 1/n: \(\hat y_i = \bar y\), tr(S) = 1 (one effective parameter, the mean). The smoother matrix’s trace interpolates between — the KNN analog of polynomial degree. (For OLS, S is the hat matrix H with tr(H) = p + 1 — same bookkeeping, which is how KNN and regression become comparable on a flexibility axis.)

Q2. KNN predictions are discontinuous at neighborhood boundaries; kernel smoothing (Part 6) fixes this by distance-decaying weights. Demonstrate the discontinuity: construct a 1-D dataset where shifting a query point by ε changes its prediction by a jump, measure the jump, and then show the same dataset under weights="distance" yields a continuous prediction path. Why might you nevertheless prefer uniform KNN?

Solution
Crossing the boundary where a far point swaps into the neighborhood changes the average by (y_in − y_out)/k — up to 2σ/k. Distance weighting fades the swap continuously. Uniform KNN is still preferred when: interpretability of “local average of the 5 most similar cases” matters, computation must be cheapest, or the distance scale itself is questionable (weights amplify metric artifacts). Smoothness is a virtue for curve display (Part 6’s smoothing splines are the refined endpoint of this line of thought).

6. Complex Questions

C1 (KNN × 4.4 Bias–variance preview). Simulate 300 training sets (n = 60) from a known f; for each k in 1..40, fit KNN on every training set and evaluate on a fixed dense grid. Estimate bias² (mean prediction vs f)², variance (SD of predictions)², and total MSE per k; plot all three. Identify the k minimizing total MSE and verify the bias/variance crossover story — you will reuse this exact experimental design on the theory page.

C2 (KNN × 0.5 Splitting × 4.3 CV). Compare two k-selection strategies on 200 fresh simulations: (a) pick k by CV, report that CV value as the error estimate; (b) pick k by CV, then report a nested outer-CV estimate. How big is the optimism of (a), and does it grow with the number of candidate k values? (This prefigures the tuning trap of 4.6.)

7. Summary

  • KNN predicts by local averaging; k is the single flexibility dial from memorization (k=1) to the mean-model (k=n).
  • Distances make scaling part of the model — scale inside a pipeline, never outside.
  • It is a linear smoother: tr(S) = effective df; k=1 is the identity map (zero training error, zero information).
  • The curse of dimensionality inflates neighborhood radius; nonparametric methods need exponentially more data in high d.
  • KNN vs OLS is bias-vs-flexibility: each wins under the truth it assumes; CV adjudicates.
  • No coefficients, no inference: KNN predicts, it doesn’t explain.
