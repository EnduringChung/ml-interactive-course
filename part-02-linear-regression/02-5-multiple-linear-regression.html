2.5 Multiple Linear Regression

Multiple regression models \(Y\) as a linear function of several predictors at once — which is where regression earns its keep: adjusting for confounders, isolating each variable’s contribution, and predicting from combined structure. It also introduces the two new failure modes that dominate applied work: collinearity and confounding.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The model becomes a vector equation:

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon\]

Fitted by the same least-squares principle, now in \((p+1)\)-dimensional coefficient space: \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf y\) (requires the columns of the design matrix \(X\) to be linearly independent). The key interpretive discipline: \(\hat\beta_j\) is the expected change in \(Y\) per unit of \(X_j\) holding the other predictors fixed — an adjusted association, not a raw one.

2. Concept Questions

  1. In y ~ hours + practice, β̂_hours = 1.8. State the exact operational meaning of that number.
  2. Why can R² rise while every individual p-value falls above 0.05? (Two predictors, strongly collinear, jointly informative.)
  3. What breaks mathematically when two predictors are perfectly collinear (x2 = 2·x1)?
  4. The F-test p = 0.2 but one predictor’s t-test p = 0.03. Possible? What’s going on?
  5. You add a confounder and your key coefficient collapses toward zero. Two explanations — which is the happy one?
Answers
  1. “Increasing study hours by 1 predicts +1.8 in y, for fixed practice problems” — the comparison is between two otherwise-identical subjects. It is not the raw effect of forcing extra hours on a real person (that would include the practice-hours they’d lose — an interaction/mediation issue).
  2. Strongly collinear predictors share their explanatory work: together they lift R², individually neither looks “significant” because each coefficient’s SE is inflated by the redundancy (the model can’t decide which one gets the credit). The F-test (joint) is significant while the t-tests (each alone) are not — the classic collinearity signature.
  3. \(X^\top X\) becomes singular — non-invertible, so \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) doesn’t exist and β̂ is not unique (infinitely many coefficient pairs give identical predictions). Computationally you get huge, unstable estimates; conceptually, the data cannot distinguish the two channels even in principle.
  4. Yes — it’s exactly answer 2’s situation: joint F rejects (the pair helps) while each individual t fails (credit-splitting inflates both SEs). The F-test and the collection of t-tests answer different questions.
    1. Happy: the confounder was inflating the coefficient; your predictor’s adjusted effect is genuinely smaller — this is learning. (b) Sad: the “confounder” is a mediator or a collider, or you adjusted away real signal (overadjustment). Which one depends on the causal structure, not on the regression output.

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1 — the confounder lab. Simulate a classic confounded observational study: hidden z drives both x and y (x = 0.8z + noise, y = 2x + 1.5z + noise). Estimate the effect of x three ways: (a) simple regression, (b) multiple regression adjusting for z, (c) multiple regression adjusting for an irrelevant variable w. Which estimate recovers the truth, and what does (c) demonstrate?

Solution
  1. inherits z’s influence through corr(x, z) and overshoots; (b) recovers 2.0 — adjustment works when the confounder is measured; (c) ≈ (a): adjusting for an unrelated variable changes nothing except a hair of precision. The entire value of multiple regression for inference is (b) — and its limit is that z must have been measured.

Exercise 2 — VIF by hand. Compute the variance inflation factor of each predictor in a 3-predictor model without variance_inflation_factor: regress each predictor on the others, take \(1/(1-R^2_j)\); verify against the library. Then state, numerically, what VIF = 10 does to SE(β̂_j) relative to an uncorrelated design.

Solution
VIF \(j\) = how many times larger \(\text{Var}(\hat\beta_j)\) is than it would be if predictor \(j\) were uncorrelated with the rest; SE inflates by √VIF. VIF = 10 is the common “serious” threshold, but the number to internalize is the mechanism: redundant predictors make individual coefficients unidentifiable in practice even while the model as a whole predicts fine (drill 2).

Exercise 3 — model building discipline. Using the statsmodels formula API on the built-in-style data below, run the classic workflow: (a) fit the full model y ~ x1 + x2 + x3; (b) use a partial F-test (anova_lm) to decide whether x3 earns its place; (c) refit without it if not. Report final coefficients with CIs, and give the one-sentence interpretation of each that a subject-matter reader could use.

Solution
Interpretation template: “holding x2 fixed, each additional unit of x1 predicts +1.2 (95% CI a to b) units of y; similarly for x2 with sign −0.7.” Note what the model doesn’t claim: causality (that needs design) and constancy of the x1 effect across x2 (that’s 2.7 Interaction’s question).

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Adjusted ≠ causal, still. Multiple regression blocks the measured confounders’ backdoors; unmeasured ones survive. The regression is honest; the causal claim needs the design.
  • Collinearity doesn’t hurt predictions, only interpretations: the fitted surface is stable even when individual coefficients seesaw (drill 2). Report predictions confidently, coefficients humbly.
  • The F-test tests the joint null — a significant F with null t-tests means collinearity; a null F with a “significant” t is a red flag (multiple testing or a fluke).
  • Adding predictors always weakly improves in-sample fit (the noise-collector theorem of 2.4); selection — which variables to keep — is a Part 5 topic, and must be judged out-of-sample.
  • Categorical predictors and interactions slot into this machinery as extra columns — dummy variables (2.6) and products (2.7) — so everything on this page applies to them unchanged.
  • Too many predictors relative to n (n ≲ 2p) makes OLS unstable or singular: the high-dimensional regime is a different sport (Part 5).
  • statsmodels vs sklearn: statsmodels gives inference (SEs, tests) and no penalty; sklearn gives prediction machinery and (in LinearRegression) no SEs. Choose by goal — the models are the same math.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Show from \(\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\) that if a predictor column is an exact linear combination of others, β̂ is not unique. Then explain why near-collinearity manifests as large coefficient variance: relate \(\text{Var}(\hat\beta) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\) to a small eigenvalue of \(X^\top X\) and to the VIF.

Hint Rotating to the eigenbasis of \(X^\top X\): coefficients along near-null directions are determined by dividing by a tiny eigenvalue.
Solution Exact collinearity: \(X = X_{-j} c\) for some \(c\) makes \(X\beta = X_{-j}(c\beta_j + \beta_{-j})\); infinitely many β produce identical fitted values, so \((X^\top X)\) is singular and the inverse mapping doesn’t exist. Near-collinearity: diagonalize \(X^\top X = Q\Lambda Q^\top\); the coefficient component along an eigenvector with tiny λ has variance \(\sigma^2/\lambda\) — enormous. The data constrain only the combination of predictors well, not each separately. The VIF is the pairwise-oriented version of the same geometry: \(\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2)\) where \(R_j^2\) is how well x_j is explained by the rest; the two views coincide through \(\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\)’s diagonal. Predictions are unaffected because prediction happens in row space, where near-dependence costs little — only coefficient identification is damaged.

Q2. Construct a suppressor variable: a predictor with zero correlation with y that improves the multiple-regression fit and flips another coefficient’s sign. Explain the mechanism in terms of partialled variables: \(\hat\beta_1\) is the slope of y on x₁ after both are cleaned of x₂ — and cleaning can reveal structure that raw correlation hid.

Solution
Mechanism: x2 is a proxy for part of x1’s noise. Controlling for x2 subtracts that noise from x1 (partialling), unmasking x1’s true coefficient; x2 itself picks up −1 from the shared-noise path. The lesson generalizes: raw correlations are projections onto single axes, multiple-regression coefficients are projections after residualization — different objects that can differ in sign or magnitude. (This is also why “delete predictors with insignificant t-stats” is a bad automatic rule.)

7. Summary

  • Multiple regression estimates each predictor’s association holding the others fixed — an adjusted, not raw, statement.
  • Adjustment fixes measured confounding; unmeasured confounding survives, and causality still needs design.
  • Collinearity damages coefficient identification (VIF, inflated SEs, F-vs-t splits) but not prediction quality.
  • The F-test judges the joint model; t-tests judge individual channels — collinearity decouples them.
  • In-sample fit creeps up with any added column; adjustment, penalties, and CV are the honest referees.
  • Design matters: spread predictors, measure the confounders, and mind n vs p.
  • All predictors so far are numeric; real data has groups and labels — next page: 2.6 Categorical predictors.
