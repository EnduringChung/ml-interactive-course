2.8 Nonlinear Feature Transformations

Linear models fit straight lines in their parameters — but the features can be any transform of x you like: polynomials, logs, splines, binned indicators. This buys curvature inside the same least-squares machinery, at the price of the same old dangers (overfitting, extrapolation) in sharper form.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

“Linear” in \(X\beta\) refers to the coefficients. Replace \(x\) by any derived columns and the model stays linear-in-parameters:

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \varepsilon \qquad \text{or} \qquad \log y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log x + \varepsilon\]

Polynomial regression of degree d is just OLS on the design matrix \([1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^d]\):

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why is polynomial regression still a “linear model”? What precisely is linear?
  2. Degree 12 polynomial through 60 points: fitted values look perfect, predictions on new data are wild. Name the two quantities whose divergence you are witnessing.
  3. Your y spans decades of magnitude with constant relative error. Which transform, and what does the slope mean if you regress \(\log_{10} y\) on \(\log_{10} x\)?
  4. Extrapolation danger: compare a linear fit and a degree-9 polynomial fit extended 20% beyond the data. Which fails more catastrophically, and why — what does the polynomial do outside the data’s support?
  5. log(y) model gives RMSE(log-scale) = 0.2. A colleague multiplies by exp to report “RMSE = 1.22” on the raw scale. What did they actually compute, and what is the right comparison?
Answers
  1. Linear in the coefficients \(\beta\): the model is \(\beta^\top \phi(x)\) for basis functions \(\phi\) (powers, logs of x — transforms of features, not of parameters). Estimation is still OLS on a design matrix.
  2. In-sample error (falls as degree rises — more flexibility always fits training better) vs out-of-sample error (rises once curvature fits noise). The gap is overfitting; Part 4 measures it, Part 5 penalizes it.
  3. log–log (power-law) transform; slope = the power exponent (y ∝ x^slope), intercept gives the multiplicative prefactor. Only after confirming constant relative scatter — the log makes multiplicative errors additive (0.4 Q2).
  4. The polynomial. A line continues its slope; a degree-9 fit’s leading coefficient (estimated from noise) dominates outside the data, swinging to ±astronomical values. Polynomials are global basis functions — a wiggle fitted inside must resolve into a violent curve outside. Local bases (splines, Part 6) behave far better.
  5. exp(0.2) = 1.22 is a median-level multiplicative miss (the error of a typical log-residual), not an RMSE in y-units — RMSE is not transform-invertible (Jensen: E[y − ŷ] ≠ exp(E[log-residual])). The honest comparison back-transforms predictions (accepting median-level bias, or smearing-correcting) and computes RMSE on the raw scale.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. For the quadratic-truth data below, fit degrees 1–7 and collect training MSE and test MSE (fresh points from the same process). Plot both against degree. Mark the argmin of test MSE, then repeat the whole experiment with n = 300 — how does the curve’s shape change and what does that tell you about “more data vs simpler model”?

Solution
At n = 30 the test curve is jagged — degree choice is nearly a coin flip among low degrees. At n = 300 the minimum sharpens around the true degree 2 and high degrees hurt less (more data tames the variance term). Data volume substitutes for model restraint — the trade 4.4 will formalize.

Exercise 2. Linearize a real-ish process: enzyme velocity follows \(v = \frac{V_{max} s}{K_m + s}\) (Michaelis–Menten) with \(V_{max} = 2\), \(K_m = 0.4\). Fit (a) a direct polynomial of degree 2 on (s, v), and (b) the classic Lineweaver–Burk linearization (\(1/v\) vs \(1/s\), whose slope = \(K_m/V_{max}\), intercept = \(1/V_{max}\)) using OLS. Recover \(V_{max}\) and \(K_m\) from each. Which recovers the truth better under noise, and what famous statistical vice does Lineweaver–Burk have?

Solution
The reciprocal transform converts multiplicative-ish errors into heteroscedastic ones and hands all the leverage to the smallest s — the few noisiest points steer the line. The polynomial dodges that trap but estimates \(V_{max}\) as a plateau asymptote. Modern practice: fit the nonlinear model directly with least squares (Part 6 territory) — linearizations are history’s workaround, not a virtue.

Exercise 3. Build poly_cv(x, y, max_degree, k=5) returning mean CV MSE per degree using a Pipeline(StandardScaler-with-PolynomialFeatures, LinearRegression) — scaling before polynomial expansion to keep raw powers numerically sane. Run it on data with a cubic truth; report the chosen degree and the CV curve. Then explain why the pipeline (transform inside each fold) matters — connect to the leakage rule of 0.5.

Solution
If you scaled/expanded before splitting, each fold’s training statistics (means, SDs) leak test-fold information into training — mild here, systematic everywhere. The pipeline runs the transform per-fold inside cross_val_score, guaranteeing the validation fold is touched only by the fitted-for-that-fold transform. The 0.5 rule — “fit preprocessing inside the training fold” — generalizes to every transformer you will ever use.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Global vs local bases: polynomial features are global — every observation informs every coefficient, so one leverage point reshapes the whole curve. Splines/step functions are local (Part 6 covers them properly; the binned drill is a preview).
  • Numerical conditioning: raw powers of x (say x ∈ [0, 1000], degree 6) make columns nearly collinear and ill-conditioned — coefficients become garbage even when predictions look fine. Standardize x first, or use orthogonal polynomials (np.polynomial.legendre.legvander, or sklearn’s scaled pipeline).
  • The polynomial tail kills: never report extrapolated polynomial predictions; if you must extrapolate, extrapolate the physics (a fitted asymptote, a log-linearized regime), not the polynomial.
  • Transforming y changes the error model: log y regression minimizes log-scale squared error — predictions are medians on the raw scale and need smearing if means are wanted; RMSE comparisons across scales are meaningless (2.4).
  • Binning throws away information and induces boundary artifacts; it’s a quick diagnostic (is there structure?) rather than an endpoint. Smooth bases or splines dominate it for inference and prediction alike.
  • Degree choice is a hyperparameter — choosing it by looking at the same data you report performance on is the classic optimism; route the choice through CV now, formally in 4.6.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Show that adding any column \(g(x)\) to a linear model’s design matrix can only decrease training RSS, and quantify the minimum decrease in terms of the residual regression: if \(g\)’s component orthogonal to the current columns explains RSS reduction \(\Delta\), write the updated fit. Use this to explain why degree-9 training RSS < degree-2 training RSS even when truth is quadratic, and what determines whether the test RSS follows.

Hint Projection geometry from 2.4 Q1: OLS projects y onto the column space; a bigger space projects further.
Solution OLS fit = orthogonal projection of y onto span(columns). Adding a column enlarges the span, and projections onto supersets are never worse: new RSS = old RSS − ‖proj of y onto g⊥(old columns)‖² = old RSS − Δ, with Δ ≥ 0. For the noise-only extra powers, Δ = the squared length of the chance alignment of the noise with those directions — positive in every sample, growing with the number of added columns. Test RSS follows only if the true f has a component in the new directions that outweighs the variance those directions import (each extra coefficient costs ~σ²·(df) of variance in predictions — the arithmetic 4.4 makes explicit). In-sample improvement is therefore guaranteed, meaningful only if structural.

Q2. You must model \(y = f(x)\) where f is believed smooth and you have n = 40 points. Compare, in one experiment, (a) global polynomial degree chosen by CV, (b) 10-bin step function, (c) the “transformed linear” model \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 \sin(x)\) when the truth is \(3\sin(x)\). Report test MSEs and state the general lesson about basis choice informed by mechanism vs generic flexible bases.

Solution
The mechanistic two-column model usually wins outright (right basis, 3 parameters); CV-polynomials get close with 5–8 parameters; steps are worst (boundary steps, step noise). Lesson: a small basis derived from how the system works beats generic flexibility at small n — save the flexible machinery for when mechanism is unknown and n is large.

7. Summary

  • “Linear” means linear in coefficients; polynomials, logs, bins, sin — any derived columns — stay in OLS-land.
  • Higher degree always improves training fit and eventually wrecks test fit; degree is a hyperparameter, chosen by CV.
  • Log transforms linearize power laws (slope = exponent) and convert multiplicative noise to additive; back-transform carefully (medians, not means).
  • Polynomials are global bases: numerically fragile raw, catastrophic in extrapolation; standardize before expanding.
  • Transforming y changes the error model and the meaning of every metric; compare models on one scale.
  • Mechanism-informed small bases beat generic flexible bases at small n.
  • The residual from a transformed fit must still be inspected — the full diagnostic toolkit is next: 2.10 Regression assumptions, after a nonparametric detour in 2.9 KNN regression.
