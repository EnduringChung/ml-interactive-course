2.3 Accuracy of the Coefficients

A slope estimate is a random variable — a different sample gives a different line. This page quantifies how much it moves: standard errors, t-tests on coefficients, and confidence intervals. This is the inference layer that separates “there’s a slope” from “we know the slope to ±0.2.”

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Under the model \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon\) with homoscedastic, independent errors, the slope estimator has standard error

\[\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}} = \frac{\hat\sigma}{s_x\sqrt{n-1}} \qquad\text{where } \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n - 2}\]

(The \(n-1\) is not a rounding convention: with \(s_x\) the ddof=1 sample SD, \(\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2} = s_x\sqrt{n-1}\) exactly. Using \(s_x\sqrt n\) understates the SE by \(\sqrt{(n-1)/n}\).)

Three levers, all visible in the formula: more noise (σ) widens; more spread in x (\(s_x\)) narrows; more points (\(\sqrt{n-1}\)) narrows. The coefficient’s t-statistic and CI follow the universal pattern from 1.4:

\[t = \frac{\hat\beta_1}{\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)}, \qquad \hat\beta_1 \pm t_{0.975,\,n-2}\,\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\]

2. Concept Questions

  1. Two datasets have identical σ and n, but dataset A’s x spans 0–100 and B’s spans 0–1. Which has the more precise slope, and which term of the SE formula says so?
  2. What exactly is \(H_0\) when P>|t| for a slope is 0.03? And what is not being tested?
  3. Why do we divide RSS by \(n-2\) (not \(n\)) to estimate σ²?
  4. Your 95% CI for a slope is (0.02, 0.11). A reviewer says “the effect is tiny.” What’s the correct reply?
  5. If you double all y values, what happens to \(\hat\beta_1\), its SE, its t-statistic, and its p-value?
Answers
  1. A — via \(s_x\) (x-spread) in the denominator: the same noise, stretched over a wider x-range, is harder to confuse with a slope. Note it is the spread, not the range per se — a single far point adds range without usable spread (and adds leverage instead).
  2. \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) — “no linear relationship with this predictor, in this model,” tested with data this size. Not tested: the effect’s practical importance (0.03 doesn’t mean “large”), causality, or the effect conditional on predictors not in the model.
  3. Two parameters (\(\beta_0, \beta_1\)) were fitted from the same residuals, consuming 2 degrees of freedom; residuals from a fitted line are systematically smaller than true ε. Dividing by \(n - p - 1\) (here p = 1) corrects the optimism — the same logic as \(n-1\) for a variance (1.1).
  4. “Statistically significant but small, and the estimate is 0.02–0.11 y-units per unit x.” Significance ≠ importance; the CI is the effect-size statement — argue importance against the interval’s values, not its exclusion of zero.
  5. \(\hat\beta_1\) and SE double (both scale with y’s units) → t and p unchanged. Slope SEs live in y-units: 2× the outcome units, 2× the uncertainty — but the same evidence. (This scale-invariance of t is why p-values don’t answer “is the effect big.”)

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Formula SEs rest on all four classical assumptions (linearity, independence, homoscedasticity, rough normality for the t reference). Heteroscedasticity or correlated errors invalidate the SEs even when the slope estimate stays unbiased — the point estimate and its uncertainty fail independently (2.10).
  • Heteroscedasticity-robust (HC) SEs are one line in statsmodels — fit(cov_type="HC3") — and are a sensible default for observational cross-sectional data.
  • Multiple predictors change the SE story: \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_j)\) now depends on collinearity — the variance inflation factor (2.5); a coefficient can be noisy because its partners are redundant, not because n is small.
  • The intercept’s SE is usually the largest (it lives at the edge, \(x = 0\)): don’t be alarmed, and don’t interpret its p-value as a test of “does the model work.”
  • p = 0.049 vs 0.051 — no meaningful boundary; report effects with CIs and treat thresholds as conventions (1.4).
  • statsmodels column names: bare arrays give generic x1, x2...; use DataFrames or formulas (smf.ols) for self-documenting output.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = \hat\sigma / \sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2}\) in two steps: (a) write \(\hat\beta_1\) as a weighted sum of the \(y_i\) with weights \(w_i = (x_i - \bar x)/S_{xx}\), and (b) apply the independent-errors variance rule from 1.3. Which property of the weights (\(\sum w_i = 0\)) makes β̂₀ drop out?

Hint \(\hat\beta_1 = \sum w_i y_i\) exactly; variances add for independent errors and intercept enters only through the centered cross-product.
Solution
  1. \(\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar x) y_i}{S_{xx}}\) (the \(-\bar y\) term vanishes since \(\sum (x_i - \bar x) = 0\)) — a linear combination \(\sum w_i y_i\) with \(w_i = (x_i - \bar x)/S_{xx}\). (b) With independent homoscedastic errors: \(\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \sum w_i^2 \sigma^2 = \sigma^2 \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2}{S_{xx}^2} = \sigma^2 / S_{xx}\). The same centering (\(\sum w_i = 0\)) makes \(\hat\beta_1\)’s sampling distribution independent of where β₀ sits — the two estimates’ covariance is zero under normality (basis of independent slope/intercept CIs). This is the seed of the full covariance matrix \(\sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\) for multiple regression (2.5).

Q2. Estimate the sampling distribution of β̂₁ by bootstrap for the Exercise 1 data and compare: (a) bootstrap SD vs formula SE, (b) bootstrap percentile CI vs t-CI. Now regenerate the data with heteroscedastic noise (\(\sigma \propto x\)) and repeat. Which comparison breaks, and what is the bootstrap version measuring that the formula no longer estimates?

Solution
Homoscedastic: the two agree (formula valid). Heteroscedastic: they diverge — the formula assumes one σ, the bootstrap re-encounters the actual error structure in the resamples, so it estimates the real sampling variability. The bootstrap is the general-purpose audit of any formula SE; when they disagree, trust the bootstrap and fix the assumptions (robust SEs or a GLM).

6. Complex Questions

C1 (coefficient accuracy × 0.5 Data splitting). Fit the same SLR on 200 different 80/20 splits; collect slope estimates and their formula SEs each time. Plot SE(slope) against the x-spread of the training split. What relationship emerges, how does it connect to the SE formula, and why does stratifying or blocking matter more for slope stability than for intercept stability?

C2 (coefficient accuracy × 2.5 Multiple regression preview). Generate \(y = 2x_1 + 0 \cdot x_2 + \varepsilon\) with \(x_2 = 0.95 x_1 + \text{noise}\) (collinear partners). Compare the SE of the \(x_1\) coefficient in (a) the single-predictor model and (b) the two-predictor model. Quantify the inflation and explain it as redundancy: the data cannot tell the two “explanations” apart, so uncertainty per coefficient explodes — the multicollinearity story of 2.5.

7. Summary

  • \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = \hat\sigma/(s_x\sqrt{n-1})\): less noise, wider x-spread, more n — tighter slopes.
  • t = β̂/SE against \(H_0{:}\,\beta = 0\); the CI, not the p-value, is the effect-size statement.
  • σ̂ uses \(n - p - 1\) df: fitted parameters consumed the residual information.
  • t-tests are scale-invariant (double y → double β̂, SE; same t) — they measure evidence, not size.
  • Formula SEs assume linearity, independence, homoscedasticity; robust SEs and the bootstrap are the practical audits.
  • Wide x-design is the cheapest precision you will ever buy.
  • One coefficient is one part of the model; next page scores the whole fit: 2.4 Accuracy of the full model.
