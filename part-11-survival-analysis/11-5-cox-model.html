11.5 Cox Proportional Hazards Regression

The Cox proportional hazards model regresses survival on covariates without assuming any baseline hazard shape:

\[h(t \mid x) = h_0(t)\, e^{\beta^\top x}\]

h₀(t) stays unspecified (a nonparametric nuisance); β’s exponentials are hazard ratios. The partial likelihood — which conditions on the risk sets, discarding h₀ — is what makes this possible, and the binary-covariate Cox is literally the log-rank test (11.3).

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The partial log-likelihood (Breslow ties):

\[\ell(\beta) = \sum_{i: \delta_i = 1} \Big[ \beta^\top x_i - \log \sum_{j \in R_i} e^{\beta^\top x_j} \Big]\]

— each event compares its covariates against everyone still at risk. Fitted with statsmodels, interpreted as hazard ratios:

2. Concept Questions

  1. What does “partial” likelihood partially out, and why doesn’t the unspecified h₀(t) bias β?
  2. State the proportional-hazards assumption in terms of two patients’ hazards — and what it implies about their hazard ratio over time.
  3. HR = 1.4 per SD of a biomarker. Write the full clinical sentence — including the word “instantaneous” and where it sits.
  4. Why does Cox regression use only event-ordering information (the partial likelihood throws away actual time values), and what stays valid — estimation of β or prediction of S(t)?
  5. A covariate violates PH (effect fades after year 2). Name three legitimate responses, in escalating modeling effort.
Answers
  1. It partials out the baseline hazard h₀(t): each event’s likelihood compares the covariate pattern of the failer against the risk set at that moment — h₀(t) multiplies everyone in both numerator and denominator and cancels. β estimation needs only the ordering structure of events and risk sets.
  2. h(t | x₁) / h(t | x₂) = e^{βᵀ(x₁−x₂)} — constant over time. Their hazard ratio never changes; their absolute hazards both follow the same unspecified h₀ shape.
  3. “Each additional SD of the biomarker multiplies the instantaneous event rate by 1.4 at every time point, holding other covariates fixed” — a relative-rate statement whose absolute benefit depends on the (unmodeled) baseline hazard (11.4 Q3).
  4. The partial likelihood discards information about how long follow-up intervals were and where events sit on the time axis (only their order vs risk sets matters). β estimation: fully valid — it’s the MLE of the Cox model. Absolute survival prediction S(t|x): NOT available without estimating h₀ (Breslow baseline) — a real limitation for risk-prediction uses.
    1. Split follow-up at the divergence time (time-dependent effect via epoch dummies); (2) add time-varying/interaction terms (x × log t); (3) switch to models with non-PH machinery — AFT, RMST-based comparisons, or stratified Cox (baseline per group).

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. The partial likelihood, by hand: for 4 subjects with covariates x = (0, 1, 2, 1), events at times 1, 2, 3 (subject 4 censored at 4), write the partial log-likelihood as a function of β: at each event, numerator e^{βxᵢ} and denominator the risk-set sum. Then maximize it numerically (scipy.optimize) and verify against PHReg on the same data. Which risk sets appear in each term — and which subject appears in no denominator?

Solution
Risk sets: event at t = 1 → everyone (all ≥ 1); t = 2 → subjects 2, 3, 4; t = 3 → subjects 3, 4. Subject 4 appears in all three denominators (censored after everything) — a censored last subject contributes plenty of partial-likelihood mass despite never eventing. The correction to the printout’s tease: censoring removes subjects from later risk sets, never from earlier ones.

Exercise 2. PH diagnostics with Schoenfeld residuals: simulate Cox data (β = 0.8, PH holds) and non-PH data (β decays: 0.8·exp(−t/2) effect via two hazard regimes). Fit PHReg to each and compute the trend of Schoenfeld residuals against event time (correlation of residual × event-time). Verify: near-zero trend under PH, clear trend under violation. Then state the decision this diagnostic drives (time-varying term vs stratification vs RMST).

Solution
Under PH the Schoenfeld residuals scatter unsystematically around 0 (trend ≈ 0); under violation they drift with time — the residual is the time-varying beta signal. Decision: a flagged trend → add x × log(t) interaction, or stratify, or present RMST — but always with the diagnostic plot attached, not just the test p-value.

Exercise 3. Risk prediction from Cox: Breslow baseline + linear predictor. Fit Cox on training data; extract fit.baseline_cumulative_hazard_ (statsmodels provides the baseline estimate); build S(t|x) = exp(−H₀(t)·e^{βᵀx}) for two new patients; validate calibration on test data (predicted vs KM-observed survival in risk-score terciles). This closes the “Cox can’t predict absolutely” gap from Concept Question 4 — with the baseline hazard doing the absolute work.

Solution
The Breslow baseline converts relative risks into absolute survival — the bridge from “HR = 2.46 per SD” to “this patient has 63% predicted survival at 2 years.” Tercile calibration (predicted vs observed within risk groups) is the minimum reporting for any clinical risk model: without it, HRs and AUCs say nothing about whether the level of predicted risk is right.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Ties: Efron’s method handles tied events more accurately than Breslow and is the recommended default (statsmodels accepts ties=; survival software defaults vary — check yours).
  • PH violation handling ladder: (1) verify (log-minus-log plots, Schoenfeld tests — 11.4 Exercise 2); (2) split follow-up or add interactions; (3) stratified Cox (separate h₀ per stratum — loses that stratum’s HR but keeps others); (4) time-varying coefficients or alternative models (AFT, RMST).
  • Stratified Cox: baseline hazards per stratum, shared β — adjust for strong confounders without requiring their PH; can’t give HRs for the stratification variable itself.
  • Time-varying covariates: values that change during follow-up need the counting-process (start–stop) data format (PHReg supports exit/entry); naive “baseline value carried forward” biases toward baseline-era effects.
  • Cox doesn’t extrapolate h₀: predictions beyond observed follow-up inherit the baseline’s endpoint — the same range-policing as every model (2.1), plus a baseline-estimation caveat.
  • Report alongside HRs: absolute survival curves by covariate pattern (Exercise 3), event counts, median follow-up — HR-only reporting is a review flag.

What’s wrong, and the fix

  2. No PH check in the workflow: even when this simulation is PH, production code must run the Schoenfeld/log-minus-log diagnostics — the model’s central assumption deserves a test, not hope.
  3. HR as risk reduction: HR is an instantaneous rate ratio — “risk reduced by 27%” conflates rate with cumulative risk (11.4 Q3); report “hazard ratio” with survival curves for absolute statements.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Show that the binary-covariate Cox score test equals the log-rank test: with a single 0/1 covariate, write the partial-likelihood score U(β) = Σᵢ∈events [xᵢ − weighted mean of x over Rᵢ] at β = 0 and verify it equals Σⱼ(O₁ⱼ − E₁ⱼ) from 11.3’s table. Verify numerically that the Cox score statistic and log-rank χ² agree to machine precision without ties. What does the derivation explain about the Cox model’s relationship to every stratified/weighted log-rank variant?

Hint At β = 0 all weights in the risk-set averages are equal (1/nᵢ), so x’s weighted risk-set mean is the risk-set’s group-1 fraction — exactly E₁ⱼ’s building block.
Solution

At β = 0, the risk-set mean of x in Rᵢ is n₁ᵢ/nᵢ (fraction of at-risk in group 1), so U(0) = Σ_{events i}(xᵢ − n₁ᵢ/nᵢ) = Σⱼ[(# events in group 1 at j) − dⱼn₁ⱼ/nⱼ] = Σⱼ(O₁ⱼ − E₁ⱼ) — the log-rank numerator, term by term; the variances match likewise (both hypergeometric per risk set).

The identity explains the whole family: stratified log-rank = Cox with strata; weighted log-ranks = Cox variants with time-weighted score contributions; and “Cox with covariates” is simply the log-rank generalized to continuous adjustment. Learning Cox is learning the generalization of the test you already built.

Q2. Partial likelihood vs full likelihood — what’s lost: write the full likelihood for censored exponential data and the Cox partial likelihood; identify the h₀-dependent factor the partial version drops. Then demonstrate the practical consequence: fit Cox and exponential-AML (accelerated) on Weibull-generated data; compare β estimates (should be similar-ish) and absolute survival predictions (where they diverge). State the rule: β questions vs S(t) questions route to different models.

Solution

Full likelihood: ∏ᵢ [h₀(tᵢ)e^{βxᵢ}]^{δᵢ} · exp(−H₀(tᵢ)e^{βxᵢ}) — the second factor (cumulative-hazard term) involves h₀ and is what the partial likelihood discards by conditioning on risk sets. Consequence demo:

β estimates agree in direction and roughly in magnitude under this exponential truth (Cox’s β = −AFT’s b), but absolute S(t|x) predictions require the baseline: Cox needs the Breslow estimate (previous page’s exercise), AFT carries its scale parameter natively. Rule: questions about relative hazards (does the biomarker matter?) → Cox partial likelihood; questions about predicted survival times → parametric/AFT or baseline-augmented Cox. Choosing the wrong one means answering a different question with high confidence.

7. Summary

  • Cox: h(t|x) = h₀(t)e^{βᵀx}; h₀ unspecified; partial likelihood conditions on risk sets — β without distributional assumptions.
  • HRs: e^β multiplicative, time-constant under PH; absolute predictions need the Breslow baseline.
  • PH is testable and central: log-minus-log plots, Schoenfeld residuals; violations have a response ladder.
  • Ties: Efron preferred; the binary-covariate Cox is the log-rank (score-test identity).
  • Time-varying covariates need start–stop formatting; stratified Cox trades a stratum’s HR for assumption safety.
  • Wide-data Cox needs penalization (11.6 builds it); HR-only reporting is incomplete — absolute curves required.
  • With classical survival in hand, the ML frontier: penalized/predictive survival models — 11.6 Survival ML.
