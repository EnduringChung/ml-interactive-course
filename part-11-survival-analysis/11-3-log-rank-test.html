11.3 The Log-Rank Test

Two KM curves look different — is the difference real? The log-rank test compares observed vs expected events at every event time and combines them: the standard nonparametric two-group survival comparison, null-calibrated by construction, and here built from its risk-table atoms.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

At each event time tⱼ: observed events in group 1 (O₁ⱼ) vs expected under the null of equal hazards (E₁ⱼ = dⱼ·n₁ⱼ/nⱼ). Sum over times:

\[\chi^2 = \frac{(O_1 - E_1)^2}{V}, \qquad V = \sum_j \frac{d_j (n_j - d_j)\, n_{1j}\, n_{2j}}{n_j^2 (n_j - 1)} \sim \chi^2_1 \text{ under } H_0\]

2. Concept Questions

  1. “Expected events” E₁ⱼ assumes what null hypothesis exactly — and why does the at-risk ratio n₁ⱼ/nⱼ make the test robust to censoring-pattern differences between groups?
  2. Why combine (O − E) over all event times instead of running one χ² per time — what does the summation buy statistically?
  3. The log-rank weights all event times equally. Name a weighting alternative and the alternative-hypothesis shape it targets.
  4. Your log-rank is “significant” with p = 0.04 — the KM curves cross at year 3. What’s the caveat, and which plot accompanies the p-value?
  5. The test is unadjusted. What does that mean in a randomized trial vs an observational cohort — and which page’s machinery adjusts (11.5)?
Answers
  1. H₀: both groups share the same underlying hazard function at every time (S₁(t) = S₂(t) identically). Under it, each event time’s dⱼ events distribute across groups in proportion to at-risk counts — hypergeometric expectation with the n₁ⱼ/nⱼ ratio — so censoring that differs by group is absorbed through each time’s risk sets.
  2. Per-time counts are ~1 event — no power. Summing accumulates signal across all times into one statistic with a known χ²₁ null (hypergeometric variances add independently across times): the Mantel–Cox construction.
  3. Weighted log-rank variants: Gehan–Breslow (weight nⱼ — early times), Tarone–Ware (√nⱼ), Fleming–Harrington (s-specific: late-heavy with (p, q) versions). Targets: early vs late hazard differences — the choice is a hypothesis about when curves diverge.
  4. Log-rank has best power for proportional hazards (constant separation); crossing curves can cancel (excess early events in one group, late in the other → O ≈ E overall). Always present the KM plot — and consider crossing-specific tests or restricted mean survival time (RMST) comparisons.
  5. In a randomized trial, unadjusted is valid (randomization balances covariates in expectation). In observational data, confounding contaminates the comparison — Cox regression with covariates (11.5) is the adjusted analog.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Build logrank_table(t, e, group) returning the per-event-time risk table with O₁ⱼ, E₁ⱼ, Vⱼ columns, plus the totals — the transparent version of the test. Verify against statsmodels.survdiff on real-ish data. Then answer: which single event time contributes the most to (O₁ − E₁), and what does that time represent in the KM plot (11.2)?

Solution
The largest contributor is typically where the KM curves gape widest — mid-follow-up where at-risk sets are still large but separation has developed. The table turns the test into readable evidence: each row is a moment of comparison.

Exercise 2. Crossing curves defeat log-rank — demonstrated: arm A has high early hazard then low; arm B constant. Simulate via piecewise exponentials (A: rate 1.5 for t < 1, then 0.2; B: rate 0.6 throughout). Run log-rank (expect weak/insignificant despite a real, clinically huge difference) and then compare restricted mean survival time (RMST) up to τ = 3 by integrating each KM curve (trapezoid on your 11.2 step curves). Report log-rank p vs RMST difference. Write the rule about crossing hazards.

Solution
Crossing curves: early excess deaths in A cancel late ones, so the log-rank statistic — which weights all times equally — washes the difference out. RMST (expected event-free time within τ) summarizes the whole curve and answers “how much survival time does the treatment buy within 3 years” — a clinically direct quantity immune to the crossing pathology. Rule: check curves for crossing before trusting log-rank; crossing → RMST or time-specific comparisons.

Exercise 3. Log-rank as a score test — the Cox connection: the log-rank test is the score test of β = 0 in a Cox model with a single binary group covariate (11.5). Verify numerically: fit PHReg on the binary covariate and compare its score test (or the Wald z² of β̂) to your hand log-rank χ². Report both p-values on several datasets and note when they diverge slightly (ties!). What does this equivalence tell you about what Cox regression is to the log-rank test?

Solution
With no ties they coincide exactly (score test identity); ties introduce small divergences because the Cox default handles them differently than the log-rank’s hypergeometric variance. The equivalence’s meaning: Cox regression generalizes the log-rank test — the binary-covariate Cox is log-rank, and adding covariates turns it into the adjusted comparison (11.5); the test you built by hand is the score test of the model you’re about to fit.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Significant ≠ clinically big: with n = 5,000, a trivial hazard separation achieves p < 0.001 — report the KM curves, median difference, or RMST alongside (1.4’s effect-size law, survival edition).
  • Log-rank assumes PH under H₁ — it’s most powerful there; crossing/tapering effects need RMST or weighted variants (Gehan/Tarone–Ware/Fleming–Harrington), chosen before looking (4.6 discipline).
  • Stratified log-rank: for a known confounder (stage, site), strata-specific (O−E)/V sums preserve within-stratum comparisons — the log-rank’s adjustment-before-Cox option.
  • Ties: log-rank’s hypergeometric variance handles ties natively (it was born on discrete event tables); Cox needs Efron/Breslow corrections — one place the “simple” test is more exact than the model.
  • Competing risks: log-rank on the cause-specific event censors competing deaths — the comparison is about cause-specific hazard, not the patient’s observed experience; say which you’re testing (11.1’s competing-risks note).
  • Multiple arms/pairs: k > 2 groups need an overall k−1 df test first, then pairwise tests with multiplicity correction — the Part 12 machinery (12.3) arriving on schedule.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. t-test on times with censoring: censored observations aren’t observed event times — a t-test on mixed (event, censor) times answers nothing (and ignores the censoring mechanism entirely — 11.1). Log-rank consumes (t, status) pairs.
  2. A 2×2 cut at an arbitrary time: this is a Fisher-style “survived past 3?” table — a different, cruder question that discards timing within bins and the censored rows’ partial information. Use the risk-table log-rank.
  3. Smallest per-timepoint p: pure optional stopping/looking (1.4 Q2, 12.2) — the minimum of many correlated tests is not a p-value at any valid level.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the log-rank’s expectation and variance: under H₀, conditional on the risk set at time j, the dⱼ events are a random sample of the nⱼ at-risk — so E[O₁ⱼ] = dⱼn₁ⱼ/nⱼ (hypergeometric mean) and Var(O₁ⱼ) = dⱼ(nⱼ−dⱼ)n₁ⱼn₂ⱼ/(nⱼ²(nⱼ−1)) (hypergeometric variance, finite-population corrected). Then verify by simulation: at a fixed risk configuration, the empirical variance of O₁ⱼ over repeated hypergeometric draws matches the formula.

Hint Drawing dⱼ events without replacement from nⱼ subjects of which n₁ⱼ are group-1: mean and variance of the hypergeometric.
Solution

The null randomizes which at-risk subjects event: O₁ⱼ ~ Hypergeometric(N = nⱼ, K = n₁ⱼ, draws = dⱼ), with E = dⱼK/N and Var = dⱼ·(N−dⱼ)·K·(N−K)/(N²(N−1)) — the formula. Simulation:

Var → σ²; summed over times (conditionally independent given risk sets) the χ²₁ calibration follows. The log-rank is hypergeometric arithmetic stacked over a risk table — no likelihood, no parametrics, exactly calibrated.

Q2. Power and sample-size intuition via simulation: for a log-rank comparing Exp(2) vs Exp(3) groups, estimate power at n per arm ∈ {50, 100, 200, 400}. Verify Schoenfeld’s approximation — events needed ≈ 4(z_{α/2} + z_β)²/(log HR)² — against your simulated requirement (interpolate n to the 80% power point, convert to events ≈ n). What does the log(HR)² denominator say about detecting small effects — and why do survival trials chase events rather than patients?

Solution
Power climbs with n and with (log HR)² — halving the hazard ratio quadruples the required events. Survival trials size on events, not enrollment: 1,000 patients with 50 events is underpowered; 200 patients with 150 events is not. That inversion — vs every Part 2 sample-size intuition — is the survival-specific fact this derivation fixes.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (log-rank × 12.3–12.4). Biomarker-defined subgroups: run log-rank within each of 20 biomarker-defined subgroups (one truly benefits, 19 null); report how many “significant” subgroup findings appear at α = 0.05 raw, with Bonferroni, and with the hierarchical “overall test first, subgroup only if overall significant” design. Simulate 1,000 trials to estimate each strategy’s false-positive rate — the regulatory standard (overall-then-subgroup) implemented and defended (12.3 machinery, applied).

C2 (log-rank × 9.4 clusters × 4.6 tuning). Clustering-then-testing, honestly: cluster patients on baseline features at k ∈ {2, 3, 4}, run log-rank per k, select the best — the naive pipeline. Then the honest version: select k by clustering-internal criteria only (silhouette/stability, blind to survival), then test. Simulate 500 datasets (some with survival-relevant clusters, some without) and compare both pipelines’ false-positive rates and power. The result is the unsupervised→survival multiplicity lesson (12.2) with a survival face.

7. Summary

  • Log-rank = Σ(O − E) over event times with hypergeometric variance: χ²₁ under “equal hazards at all times.”
  • Risk-set arithmetic makes it robust to (non-informative, group-specific) censoring patterns.
  • Best power under proportional hazards; crossing curves cancel — check the KM plot, consider RMST.
  • Weighted variants (Gehan/Tarone–Ware/Fleming–Harrington) target early/late differences — pre-specify.
  • It’s the score test of the binary-covariate Cox model — the on-ramp to adjusted survival analysis.
  • Unadjusted means “valid under randomization”; observational data needs Cox’s covariate machinery.
  • The Cox model itself — partial likelihood, hazard ratios, PH diagnostics: 11.5 Cox proportional hazards regression, after hazards in 11.4.
