11.1 Why Survival Data Is Special

Clinical and biological data rarely records whether something happened — it records when, or that it hadn’t yet (right-censoring). Naive tools break on this: deleting censored cases biases every estimate, and “average time” is undefined while events are still accruing. Survival analysis is the machinery for time-to-event data, and this page installs its vocabulary and the first simulation engine you’ll reuse all part.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Each subject contributes a pair: observed time \(T_i = \min(T_i^{\text{event}}, C_i)\) (event or censoring time, whichever comes first) and status δᵢ = 1 if the event was observed, 0 if censored. The key assumption that makes everything work: non-informative censoring — censored subjects’ future risk resembles those who remain under observation.

2. Concept Questions

  1. A subject exits the study at year 3 alive, when the median survival is year 6. What do the naive strategies (“drop them”, “count 3 as their time”) each assert about that subject — and why is both wrong?
  2. Why is the complete-case mean of observed times biased downward here, while the naive mean was biased too — what did each include that it shouldn’t?
  3. State non-informative censoring in one sentence, and give one censoring mechanism that violates it.
  4. Which quantity does survival analysis target instead of the mean when censoring dominates, and why is it estimable under censoring?
  5. Your dataset has status ∈ {0, 1} and time ≥ 0 with 40% censored. What three checks would you run before any survival model?
Answers
  1. Dropping asserts “their risk profile is uninformative and can leave” — deleting the longest survivors biases the survival curve downward at later times. Counting 3 as the event time asserts “they had the event at 3” — fabricating an outcome. Survival analysis treats them as known to be alive at 3, event status afterwards unknown — exactly what censored likelihood encodes.
  2. Naive mean included censored times as if events occurred at censoring (they didn’t) — diluting/pulling the mean depending on censoring time. Complete-case mean kept only those who had events before censoring — a systematically shorter-tailed (sicker) subgroup. Both misuse the censoring information; the KM estimator uses it correctly (11.2).
  3. “Censoring time is independent of event time given covariates” — censored subjects carry the same future risk as those still followed. Violation: censoring because the patient worsened (transferred to hospice) — censoring then tracks the event, and all standard estimators bias downward.
  4. The survival function S(t) = P(T > t) (and its derived median): S(t) is estimable under right-censoring because each event time contributes (1 − d/n) using everyone still at risk — censoring only reduces the risk set, correctly.
    1. status ∈ {0, 1} with 0 = censored (not “no event happened” — semantics!); (b) time > 0 everywhere and censoring isn’t informative (why do people leave?); (c) enough events (censoring fraction vs event count drives power — 1.4).

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Build the survival dataset constructor make_survival(n, scale, censor_cap, seed) returning (obs_time, status) from exponential events with uniform censoring. Then plot the censoring fraction as a function of censor_cap ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} and overlay the theoretical value 1 − E[min(T,C)]-implied curve shape. At what cap does censoring fall below 10% for scale = 2? Compute analytically: P(T > C) with T ~ Exp(2), C ~ U(0, cap).

Solution
Censoring falls below 10% when cap ≈ 5.2 (solve (2/cap)(1 − e^{−cap/2}) = 0.1). The analytic formula — integrate the exponential survival function against the uniform censoring density — is exactly the calculus every censoring-fraction question reduces to; simulation verifies the algebra.

Exercise 2. Informative censoring damage, quantified: simulate frailty-driven data (as in drill 3) where both event and censoring hazards depend on frailty, and estimate “median survival” by the complete-case median vs the true population median (computable: T ~ mixture of exponentials by frailty… use a large no-censoring run as ground truth). Show the complete-case median is biased even though the censoring is independent given frailty — i.e., conditionally non-informative but marginally informative. What does this say about the word “independent” in the assumption?

Solution
Censoring independent of T given frailty is still dependent on T marginally (both track frailty), and complete-case analysis conditions on nothing — it inherits the selection. The vocabulary precision matters: “non-informative” must be judged against the analysis population, and covariate-adjusted methods (Cox, 11.5) are how conditional independence gets used rather than assumed away.

Exercise 3. The counting-process view: build the at-risk/event table by hand for a small dataset (10 subjects with times and statuses): at each distinct event time, compute n at risk, d events, c censored. Then verify two identities: (a) Σd = number of events; (b) events + censored + still-at-risk-at-end = n. This table is the data structure beneath KM, log-rank, and Cox — implement it as risk_table(t, e) returning a DataFrame.

Solution
The risk table is the counting-process backbone: KM multiplies (1 − d/n) over its rows, log-rank compares (O − E) across groups on its rows, and Cox’s partial likelihood conditions on its risk sets. Every survival method in this part is arithmetic over this table.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Status semantics flip across software: 1 = event in most Python/R tools, but some datasets code “1 = alive” — relabel and re-check frequencies before anything else; a flipped status silently inverts every survival curve.
  • Administrative vs loss-to-follow-up censoring: administrative censoring (study ends) is typically non-informative by design; loss-to-follow-up often isn’t. Report which kind your censoring is.
  • Interval censoring (event known only between visits) needs different likelihoods (interval-censored methods); treating interval-censored as right-censored biases toward visit times.
  • Left truncation (subject enters study at risk only from a later age): handled via entry times (entry= in statsmodels) — ignoring truncation biases toward including time-at-risk that was never observed.
  • Competing risks: death precludes relapse — naive KM treats competing events as censored and overestimates the relapse curve; cumulative-incidence estimation (CumIncidenceRight in statsmodels) is the correct object when competing risks exist.
  • Censoring fraction ≠ information: a dataset can be 90% censored yet well-powered (long follow-up, few but late events) or 30% censored and hopeless (no events yet). Report event count, not just censoring fraction.

What’s wrong, and the fix

  2. Complete-case median: it’s the median of the shortest follow-ups, not survival. Use the Kaplan–Meier median (11.2).
  3. Dropping censored rows: discards legitimate at-risk information and biases toward fast events. Keep (time, status) pairs — regression on times isn’t even the right frame; Cox regression (11.5) consumes the pairs.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove that the complete-case mean of observed times is not consistent for E[T] under independent censoring: with T ⊥ C, E[T | T ≤ C] = E[T·1(T ≤ C)]/P(T ≤ C) ≠ E[T] — show the bias direction depends on the censoring distribution’s support. Then verify: for T ~ Exp(2), C ~ U(0, c), compute the exact complete-case mean E[T · 1(T ≤ C)]/P(T ≤ C) analytically and match it by simulation for c ∈ {2, 4}.

Hint Condition on C: E[T·1(T ≤ C) | C = c] = ∫₀^c t·f_T(t)dt; integrate over c, and P(T ≤ C) = E[1 − e^{−C/scale}].
Solution

With T ~ Exp(λ) (λ = 1/2), C ~ U(0, c): ∫₀^c t·λe^{−λt}dt = (1/λ)(1 − e^{−λc}(1 + λc)). Averaging over C: numerator = (1/λ)[1 − (1 − e^{−λc}(1+λc))/(λc)·… ] — the closed form is messy; the point is it’s strictly less than E[T] = 1/λ whenever censoring bites, because events beyond the censoring window are systematically excluded:

The complete-case mean converges to ≈ 1.24 vs true 2.0 at c = 4 — a 38% underestimate with independent censoring, purely from conditioning on T ≤ C. Consistency fails because each sample answers a different question (“mean of the uncensored”). Survival analysis exists because these conditioning events are the data.

Q2. Design the censoring-diagnostic report: your dataset has 35% censoring. Build (code skeleton + reasoning) the diagnostic panel: (a) censoring-time distribution (histogram of censoring times among censored), (b) censoring vs covariates (logistic of censored-on-covariates), (c) KM of censoring (treat censoring as the event!) per subgroup, (d) reverse KM for median follow-up (Snaith’s method: flip status). For each, state the decision it informs (e.g., “censoring times bunched at study end → administrative, benign”). Implement the panel on simulated data with a known informative-censoring contamination and show it flags the contamination.

Solution
Panel logic: (a) a spike at the study end declares administrative censoring (benign); (b/c) censoring related to patient state flags informativeness (adjust or restrict); (d) median follow-up contextualizes the S(t) curve’s trustworthy range. This panel runs before any model — it’s the survival-data analog of 0.4’s EDA protocol.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (survival × 4.4 bias–variance). The censoring-information trade: with fixed n = 100 events budget, compare designs — (a) 100 subjects, 0% censored; (b) 300 subjects, 67% censored (same event count) — by the variance of the KM estimator at t = median (simulate 500 datasets each). Which design wins, and what does the answer say about why clinical trials maximize follow-up time before sample size?

C2 (survival × 9.3 missing data). Censoring as missingness: map the survival problem onto the missing-data taxonomy — status = 0 is “event time missing, censoring time observed.” Which mechanism (MCAR/MAR/MNAR) does non-informative right-censoring correspond to? Then show that KM is a special-purpose analogue of the MAR machinery: construct a tiny dataset where complete-case deletion vs KM differ, and explain the mapping in the 9.3 vocabulary.

7. Summary

  • Survival data = (observed time, status) pairs; censoring means “alive at exit,” not “no event.”
  • Naive means and complete-case analyses are biased under censoring — in opposite directions.
  • Non-informative censoring is the load-bearing assumption; conditionally-independent censoring can still be marginally informative — adjust for covariates.
  • S(t) is the estimand of choice under censoring; the risk table (n at risk, d events) is the data structure beneath every estimator.
  • Diagnose censoring (panel: distribution, covariates, reverse KM) before modeling; report event counts, not censoring fractions.
  • Competing risks and interval censoring need their own machinery — naive KM misstates both.
  • The first estimator that gets all this right: 11.2 Kaplan–Meier survival curves.
