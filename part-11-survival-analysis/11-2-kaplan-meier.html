11.2 Kaplan–Meier Survival Curves

The Kaplan–Meier estimator is the nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimate of S(t) under right-censoring: a running product of per-event survival fractions over the at-risk set. It needs no distributional assumption, handles censoring exactly, and is the first plot of every survival analysis.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

At each distinct event time tᵢ (with nᵢ at risk, dᵢ events):

\[\hat S(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\right)\]

Censored subjects contribute to nᵢ up to their exit and vanish after — censoring correctly absorbed. Hand-rolled, then verified against statsmodels:

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why does a censored subject at time c contribute to the denominator nᵢ for events before c but not after — and which single word describes the set they belong to?
  2. Two subjects censored at t = 5 with zero events between 4.8 and 5: does the curve drop at 5? What would make it drop at a time with zero events (and why can’t that happen in KM)?
  3. The Greenwood SE grows with t even with constant d/n ratios — why? What does that imply about reading the curve’s right tail?
  4. KM median vs complete-case median (11.1): state both targets and why the KM version is the honest one.
  5. If the last observation is censored with S(t) still > 0.5, what is the KM median — and how do tools report it?
Answers
  1. The at-risk set: subjects contribute to denominators while under observation — censoring at c removes them from all risk sets beyond c. The word: “at risk” (the counting-process frame of 11.1 Exercise 3).
  2. No — KM only steps at event times (d > 0); censoring-only times just shrink future denominators. It cannot drop without an event: the product only loses factors at events. (A drop at a no-event time would mean the implementation confused censoring with events — bug 1 of 11.1.)
  3. Greenwood variance accumulates d/(n(n−d)) terms: as nᵢ shrinks (attrition), the per-event terms grow even at constant ratios — the tail rests on fewer people, so relative uncertainty rises. Read the right tail with (much) wider bands, or not at all.
  4. Complete-case median: median of observed event times — biased toward short times (11.1). KM median: the time where the estimated survival function crosses 0.5 — a consistent estimate of the population median under censoring. The KM median is the reportable one.
  5. Undefined/never reached: the curve never crosses 0.5 within follow-up. Tools return NaN/Inf or the last S value with a “+”/“NR” annotation — report “median not reached, S(last) = …” instead of inventing a number.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Two-group KM with log-minus-log bands: for treatment/control data (simulated with true medians 2 vs 4), plot both KM curves with ±1.96 Greenwood-SE bands on the log(−log S) scale (the standard survival-plot transform), and report both KM medians with their band-crossing times. Does the truth’s ordering sit inside the bands’ separation everywhere — where does the plot admit uncertainty (early? late?) and why?

Solution
Bands widen with t (attrition, Greenwood’s accumulating terms): the curves separate cleanly early (small SE) while the late-time ordering is statistically soft. Reporting rule follows automatically: claims about medians/late survival carry the bands; claims about early separation can be stronger.

Exercise 2. KM is the nonparametric MLE — feel it via the discrete toy: for 4 subjects with times {1, 2, 3, 4} and statuses {1, 1, 0, 1}, write the KM likelihood as a product of per-interval multinomial terms; show by hand that maximizing it gives \(\hat S(1) = 3/4, \hat S(2) = 1/2, \hat S(3) = 1/2, \hat S(4) = 0\) — and verify with your km_curve. Which subject’s data drives which factor?

Solution

Likelihood factors: at t=1: one event among 4 at risk → (1/4)·(3/4)^{c} with censored count handled by the at-risk logic; the KM factors are (1 − 1/4), (1 − 1/3), (no factor: censored), (1 − 1/1) = 0.

Subject 3’s censoring never multiplies the likelihood — it silently reduces subject 4’s risk set (n = 1 at t = 4, so their event forces S to 0). Every KM factor is (events among at-risk), and this tiny example exhibits the estimator’s full logic: multinomial per interval, censoring absorbed into denominators, and certainty of eventual event collapsing S to zero.

Exercise 3. KM robustness audit: compare the KM curve estimated from (a) full data, (b) the same data with 30% of events randomly deleted (converted to censored), and (c) 30% of censored deleted (converted to events), against the truth (known exponential). Which corruption biases S(t) downward, which upward, and what does this say about the directional risks of bad status data? Then write the data-ingestion check that catches each.

Solution
Deleting events raises S(t) (fewer drops); converting censored to events lowers it — status errors bias in known directions. Ingestion checks: (1) status frequency against the protocol’s expected event rate; (2) KM curve against the literature’s shape for the endpoint; (3) spike audits (bursts of events at administrative dates = censoring misread as events).

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Ties: KM handles tied event times exactly (d > 1 in one factor); tied event+censor at the same time conventionally puts censored after events (conservative at-risk sets) — Efron-vs-Breslow’s cousin at the estimator level.
  • Confidence bands vs pointwise bands: Greenwood ±1.96·SE is pointwise; simultaneous (Hall–Wellner, log-log transformed) bands are wider and are the honest overlay when you claim “the whole curve.” State which you drew.
  • Median not reached: report S(last t) with the “+”, the number at risk, and median follow-up (reverse KM, 11.1 Q2) — never a fabricated median.
  • Number at risk table under the plot is part of the figure: readers need denominators to judge tail trustworthiness.
  • ** KM of a subgroup vs adjusting**: subgroup curves describe; they don’t adjust for other imbalances — that’s Cox’s job (11.5) with the log-rank test (11.3) as the unadjusted comparison.
  • Weights: survey-weighted or IP-weighted KM variants exist (SurvfuncRight accepts weights); unweighted KM on weighted samples reproduces the sample, not the population.

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated KM plot has three bugs. Run, diagnose, fix.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Float-comparison status: time < true_t on floats is usually right by luck; the robust event definition is true_t <= censor with a boolean from the generative step, or an epsilon guard — but the real fix is deriving status from the data-generating fact, never from float arithmetic on outputs.
  2. Stepping at every unique time: censored-only times multiply the curve by (1 − 0/n) = 1 here, but they corrupt the at-risk counts’ alignment and cost compute — step at event times only.
  3. np.interp on a step function: linear interpolation invents medians between steps; walk the step curve to the first crossing (or use the step’s post-value convention).

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove KM is the nonparametric MLE: in the discrete model where S jumps only at observed times, the likelihood of (time, status) data factorizes into per-interval terms \([S(t_i)/S(t_{i-1})]^{d_i}\cdot[S(t_i)/S(t_{i-1})]^{c_i}\cdot ...\) — show that maximizing over the jump sizes recovers \(\hat S(t_i)/\hat S(t_{i-1}) = 1 − d_i/n_i\), i.e., the KM factors. Then state what assumption about censoring within intervals the MLE argument quietly makes (censoring occurs after events at the same time).

Hint Write the likelihood per interval with pⱼ = S(tⱼ)/S(tⱼ₋₁) as parameters; maximize (pⱼ){dⱼ}(1−pⱼ){nⱼ−dⱼ} — a binomial MLE.
Solution

Within interval j (between event times), each at-risk subject either events (prob pⱼ) or survives/censors. The likelihood contributes pⱼ{dⱼ}(1−pⱼ){nⱼ−dⱼ} — binomial with MLE p̂ⱼ = dⱼ/nⱼ — so Ŝ jumps by (1 − dⱼ/nⱼ): the KM factor. The quiet assumption: censored subjects in the interval are censored after any event at the interval’s end (their survival contributes through (1−pⱼ)^{…}); convention places events before censoring at ties, making KM the conservative choice. Verification by direct maximization:

KM = the MLE — and the censoring-tie convention is visible in its derivation, not just convention lore.

Q2. KM vs exponential MLE, a parametric cross-check: for censored exponential data, the MLE of the rate is λ̂ = events/total-time-on-test (exposed to censoring correctly!). Implement both KM(S at t = 2) and the exponential-MLE survival e^{−λ̂·2}, and compare their sampling variability across 500 datasets at n ∈ {40, 200}. Quantify the parametric estimator’s efficiency gain — and state the asymmetry in what breaks each (misspecified shape kills the exponential MLE; censoring-handling mistakes kill both).

Solution
The parametric MLE’s variance is ~30–50% lower at these n — the price of assuming the shape. When exponentiality is defensible, take the efficiency; when in doubt, KM’s assumption-freeness is the safeguard. Both estimators die from status bugs; only the exponential dies from wrong shape — that asymmetry is why KM is the default and parametric fits are cross-checks.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (KM × 1.5 resampling). Bootstrap the KM median: 1000 bootstrap resamples → the distribution of the KM median (with “not reached” handling). Report the bootstrap CI and compare against the Greenwood-log-log CI from Exercise 1’s machinery. Where do they differ at the tails, and what does the bootstrap median’s discreteness (it inherits the step grid) do to the interval’s smoothness?

C2 (KM × 0.5 splits × 9.4 clusters preview). KM per subgroup vs KM per discovered cluster: cluster patients on baseline features (K-means, k = 2) ignoring survival, then plot KM per cluster. Simulate data where one cluster genuinely has worse survival and evaluate: how often (over 200 simulations) does cluster-based KM separation appear (log-rank p < 0.05, 11.3) when the clustering was blind to survival? This is the unsupervised→survival handoff — and its multiplicity cost (12.2).

7. Summary

  • KM = Π(1 − dᵢ/nᵢ) over event times: censoring absorbed via at-risk sets, no distribution assumed.
  • Censored-only times never move the curve; attrition inflates Greenwood SEs — trust the tail less.
  • KM median = first S(t) ≤ 0.5 crossing; “not reached” is a reportable, honest outcome.
  • Status errors bias in known directions (lost events ↑S, fabricated events ↓S): ingestion checks are survival hygiene.
  • KM is the nonparametric MLE — binomial factors per interval, censoring-after-events convention built in.
  • Parametric MLEs gain ~30–50% variance when the shape is right — cross-checks, not defaults.
  • Two curves beg for a formal comparison: 11.3 The log-rank test.
