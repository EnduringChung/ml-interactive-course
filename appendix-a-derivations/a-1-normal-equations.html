A.1 Normal Equations for Linear Regression

Every model page in this course uses \(\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\) — this appendix page derives it from first principles, so the formula is a conclusion rather than an incantation. The derivation is three lines of calculus; the interesting parts are the assumptions underneath and the numerical warnings on top.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live (sympy for the symbolic derivation, NumPy for the check).

1. Basic Concept

The least-squares problem: given \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) (design matrix, one row per observation, one column per feature, plus an intercept column of 1s) and \(y \in \mathbb{R}^n\), find \(\beta\) minimizing the residual sum of squares:

\[\text{RSS}(\beta) = (y - X\beta)^\top (y - X\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^\top \beta)^2\]

The derivation, symbolically:

The gradient of RSS is \(-2X^\top(X\beta - y)\); setting it to zero gives the normal equations \(X^\top X \beta = X^\top y\), and when \(X^\top X\) is invertible:

\[\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y\]

The geometric reading (the one to keep): \(\hat y = X\hat\beta\) is the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the column space of \(X\) — the residual \(y - \hat y\) is perpendicular to every column of \(X\), which is exactly what \(X^\top(y - X\hat\beta) = 0\) says. Least squares = closest point in the feature subspace.

2. Concept Questions

  1. The normal equations are \(X^\top X \beta = X^\top y\). What is the \(i\)-th row of this system, in words — what does each equation say about the residuals?
  2. Why is \(X^\top X\) invertible if and only if \(X\) has full column rank? What does rank deficiency mean for the data (two ways it happens)?
  3. The geometric reading says \(\hat y\) is the projection of \(y\) onto \(\text{col}(X)\). What is the projection matrix \(H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\) called, and what are its two key algebraic properties?
  4. Why does the course recommend solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y) over inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y — what is the numerical difference?
  5. The derivation never mentioned probability. What assumption about the data-generating process would make \(\hat\beta\) the maximum-likelihood estimate? (Forward reference: A.2.)
Answers
  1. The \(i\)-th row says \(\sum_j (X^\top X)_{ij} \beta_j = (X^\top y)_i\) — but the meaning is in the residual form: \(X^\top(y - X\hat\beta) = 0\) says the residual vector is orthogonal to every feature column, i.e., no feature has any linear association with the residuals left. If a feature did correlate with the residual, you could reduce RSS by moving \(\beta\) along it — so the equations are the “no exploitable linear signal left” condition.
  2. \(X^\top X\) is invertible iff its columns are linearly independent iff \(X\) has full column rank (\(p\) independent columns). Rank deficiency happens when: (i) two features are collinear (one is a linear combination of others — e.g., including both “cm” and “inches” of the same measurement), or (ii) \(n < p\) (more features than observations — the 5.6 regime, where infinitely many \(\beta\) fit perfectly).
  3. The hat matrix (it “puts the hat on \(y\)”). Properties: idempotent (\(H^2 = H\) — projecting twice is the same as projecting once) and symmetric (\(H = H^\top\) — the projection is orthogonal). Its trace is \(p\): the “degrees of freedom” of the fit.
  4. inv explicitly forms \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) — an \(O(p^3)\) operation that squares the condition number (the inverse of a nearly-singular matrix is numerically unstable). solve factors \(X^\top X\) (Cholesky) and solves the system directly — same answer, half the condition-number exposure. At \(p\) = 10,000 features the difference is the difference between an answer and NaN.
  5. Gaussian noise: if \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)\), the likelihood is maximized exactly when RSS is minimized — least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian errors. That is A.2, the next page.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Derive the normal equations for simple linear regression without matrices: minimize \(\sum (y_i - a - b x_i)^2\) over \((a, b)\) by setting both partial derivatives to zero, and show the solution is the familiar \(\hat b = \text{Cov}(x,y)/\text{Var}(x)\), \(\hat a = \bar y - \hat b \bar x\). Then verify the matrix and scalar forms agree on a random dataset.

Solution

The two equations are \(n a + b\sum x_i = \sum y_i\) and \(a\sum x_i + b\sum x_i^2 = \sum x_i y_i\). Solving: subtract \(\bar x\)×(first) from the second to get \(b = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2} = \text{Cov}(x,y)/\text{Var}(x)\), and \(a = \bar y - b\bar x\) — the covariance-over-variance form you know from 2.2, now derived. The matrix form with the intercept column of 1s produces exactly these two equations — the scalar and matrix derivations are the same calculus in different notation.

Exercise 2. The projection view, verified: for a random \(X\) and \(y\), compute \(\hat y = X\hat\beta\), the residual \(r = y - \hat y\), and verify (a) \(X^\top r = 0\) (orthogonality), (b) \(\hat y\) is in the column space of \(X\) (it is \(X\hat\beta\) by construction — verify it’s unchanged by re-projecting: \(H\hat y = \hat y\)), (c) the Pythagorean decomposition \(\|y\|^2 = \|\hat y\|^2 + \|r\|^2\). State why (c) is the geometric content of \(R^2\).

Solution
  1. is the geometric \(R^2\): the total sum of squares splits into the explained (projected) and residual (orthogonal) parts — because the projection is orthogonal, the Pythagorean theorem applies, and \(R^2\) is the fraction of \(\|y\|^2\) lying in the feature subspace. This is why \(R^2\) is a geometric quantity, not just a formula: it’s the squared cosine of the angle between \(y\) and its projection.

Exercise 3. The singular case and its fix: construct \(X\) with \(n < p\) (more features than observations), show that \(X^\top X\) is singular and infinitely many solutions achieve RSS = 0, then show that adding \(\lambda I\) (ridge, A.4) makes the system solvable for any \(\lambda > 0\). State the statistical moral.

Solution
With \(n < p\) the system is underdetermined: a \(p\)-dimensional coefficient space, only \(n\) equations — a \((p-n)\)-dimensional affine space of exact solutions (all achieving RSS = 0, all overfit). Ridge’s \(\lambda I\) adds \(p\) independent equations’ worth of curvature, making \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) positive definite and the solution unique — at the cost of a nonzero RSS. The statistical moral: when the data can’t identify the model, you must supply the missing information — ridge supplies it as a prior belief (“coefficients are small”), which is A.4’s Bayesian reading. The normal equations are a identification result first and a fitting result second.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number. If \(X\) has condition number \(\kappa\), \(X^\top X\) has \(\kappa^2\) — near-collinear features (the drill-4 example) make the normal equations numerically treacherous. The stable path is QR or SVD factorization of \(X\) itself (never forming \(X^\top X\)); np.linalg.lstsq does this, which is why it’s the default recommendation.
  • The intercept is a column of 1s — including it makes the first normal equation \(\sum r_i = 0\) (the residuals sum to zero). Forgetting the intercept forces the regression line through the origin and biases every slope.
  • Centering changes the conditioning, not the fit: with an intercept, the slopes are identical whether or not you center \(X\) — but the numerical problem is better conditioned when features are centered (the intercept column is orthogonal to the centered features). Same math, kinder floating point.
  • The normal equations are not the definition of least squares — they’re the first-order condition. RSS is convex (its Hessian \(2X^\top X\) is positive semidefinite), so the stationary point is the global minimum — but only when \(X^\top X\) is invertible is it unique. Convexity is why the derivation “just works”; invertibility is why it sometimes doesn’t.
  • Weighted least squares (heteroscedastic errors, 2.10): replace \(X^\top X\) with \(X^\top W X\) and \(X^\top y\) with \(X^\top W y\) for a diagonal weight matrix \(W\) — the same derivation, reweighted. The normal equations are a template, not a one-off.

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated regression solver has three bugs. Diagnose before running the fixed version.

# AI-generated: least squares via the normal equations (will not run here — spot the bugs)
def fit(X, y):
    beta = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y     # BUG 1
    return beta

def predict(X, beta):
    return X @ beta + np.mean(y)                # BUG 2

X = np.array([[1, 1], [1, 2], [1, 3]])          # BUG 3
y = np.array([2, 3, 4])
print(fit(X, y))
What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Explicit inverse: inv(X.T @ X) forms the inverse — squares the condition number and is slower than solving. Use np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y) (or lstsq for the rank-deficient case).
  2. Prediction adds the mean of the training y: the model’s prediction is X @ beta — the intercept is already in \(\beta\) (via the column of 1s). Adding np.mean(y) double-counts the intercept and shifts every prediction.
  3. The design matrix is integer-typed: np.array([[1, 1], ...]) creates an int64 array — X.T @ X is integer arithmetic, and the solve can silently truncate. Cast to float: X = np.array(..., dtype=float).
# fixed
def fit(X, y):
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    return np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)

def predict(X, beta):
    return np.asarray(X, dtype=float) @ beta

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. The normal equations as a template: derive the weighted least squares solution for heteroscedastic errors \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2/w_i\), showing the solution is \(\hat\beta = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W y\) with \(W = \text{diag}(w_i)\). Then show the transformation trick: WLS is OLS on the transformed data \(\tilde X = W^{1/2}X\), \(\tilde y = W^{1/2}y\) — and state why this means every OLS diagnostic (residuals, hat matrix, \(R^2\)) has a weighted version.

Hint Multiply both sides of each equation by \(\sqrt{w_i}\) — the weights become a change of units.
Solution With \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 W^{-1}\), the MLE minimizes the weighted RSS \(\sum w_i (y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2 = (y - X\beta)^\top W (y - X\beta)\). The gradient is \(-2X^\top W(X\beta - y)\); setting it to zero gives \(\hat\beta = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W y\). The transformation: let \(\tilde X = W^{1/2}X\), \(\tilde y = W^{1/2}y\) — then the weighted RSS is exactly \((\tilde y - \tilde X\beta)^\top(\tilde y - \tilde X\beta)\), the unweighted RSS of the transformed data, so the WLS solution is the OLS solution on \((\tilde X, \tilde y)\). Consequence: every OLS result transfers — the hat matrix becomes \(\tilde X(\tilde X^\top \tilde X)^{-1}\tilde X^\top\), residuals and \(R^2\) are computed on the transformed scale, and the geometry (projection, orthogonality) is unchanged because \(W^{1/2}\) is just a change of inner product. The deep point: weights are a geometry, not a correction — WLS is OLS in a space where the axes are scaled by the precision.

Q2. The normal equations in the \(n \to \infty\) limit: show that \(X^\top X / n\) converges to the feature second-moment matrix \(\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[x x^\top]\) and \(X^\top y / n\) to \(\mathbb{E}[x y]\), so the population normal equations are \(\Sigma \beta = \mathbb{E}[xy]\). Then state what this says about what least squares estimates (the best linear predictor, not the true causal effect) and connect to 2.10’s assumptions.

Hint The law of large numbers turns sums into expectations — the normal equations become a statement about the population, not the sample.
Solution Dividing the normal equations by \(n\): \(\frac{1}{n}X^\top X \beta = \frac{1}{n}X^\top y\). As \(n \to \infty\), \(\frac{1}{n}X^\top X \to \mathbb{E}[x x^\top] = \Sigma\) and \(\frac{1}{n}X^\top y \to \mathbb{E}[x y]\) (law of large numbers, assuming i.i.d. draws), so the population solution solves \(\Sigma \beta^* = \mathbb{E}[xy]\) — the best linear predictor of \(y\) from \(x\) in the population, minimizing \(\mathbb{E}[(y - x^\top\beta)^2]\). The crucial distinction: \(\beta^*\) is the best linear prediction rule, which equals the true causal effect only under 2.10’s assumptions (exogeneity: \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon | x] = 0\)). With omitted variables or reverse causality, \(\Sigma\) and \(\mathbb{E}[xy]\) are still well-defined, the normal equations still have a solution — but it estimates the projection of \(y\) onto \(x\), not the effect of \(x\) on \(y\). The normal equations are a prediction device; causality is a modeling assumption you add on top. This is the gap between 2.5’s “the coefficients” and what they mean.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (normal equations × A.4 ridge × 5.2). The ridge solution \((X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\) is the normal equations of a modified problem — derive what problem (add \(\lambda\|\beta\|^2\) to RSS), then show the two solutions coincide in the limit \(\lambda \to 0\) (when \(X^\top X\) is invertible) and that ridge is the unique solution when \(X^\top X\) is singular. The normal equations are the fixed point of this whole family: OLS, ridge, WLS, and (via the KKT conditions) lasso (A.5) are all “solve a linear system” at heart.

C2 (normal equations × 9.2 PCA × A.6). PCA’s first component is the unit vector maximizing \(w^\top X^\top X w\) — the same matrix as the normal equations’ \(X^\top X\), but maximizing a quadratic form instead of solving a linear system. Show that the PCA solution is the top eigenvector of \(X^\top X\) while the OLS solution is \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) applied to \(X^\top y\) — then state the unifying view: both are spectral statements about the same matrix, one about its largest eigenvalue, the other about its inverse. This is the bridge from regression to dimensionality reduction.

7. Summary

  • Least squares = minimize RSS; the gradient is \(-2X^\top(X\beta - y)\); the normal equations \(X^\top X\beta = X^\top y\) are the “no linear signal left in the residuals” condition.
  • \(\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\) when \(X\) has full column rank; the solution is the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the column space (the hat matrix, idempotent and symmetric).
  • Rank deficiency (collinearity or \(n < p\)) breaks invertibility — ridge’s \(\lambda I\) is the fix (A.4).
  • Use solve/lstsq, never inv — \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number.
  • The population limit is the best linear predictor \(\Sigma\beta = \mathbb{E}[xy]\) — prediction, not causation (2.10).
