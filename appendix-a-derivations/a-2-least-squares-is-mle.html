A.2 Why Least Squares Is Maximum Likelihood

A.1 derived \(\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\) as the minimizer of RSS. This page derives the same formula as the maximum-likelihood estimate under Gaussian noise — the reason least squares is not just a geometric convenience but a statistical model, and the reason its failures (outliers, heteroscedasticity) are exactly the failures of the Gaussian assumption.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live (sympy for the symbolic derivation, NumPy for the check).

1. Basic Concept

The model. Assume the data-generating process is

\[y = X\beta + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)\]

— the mean is linear in the features, and the noise is Gaussian, independent, and identically distributed. Then \(y \sim N(X\beta, \sigma^2 I)\), and the likelihood of the parameters given the data is the product of \(n\) Gaussian densities. The log-likelihood:

\[\ell(\beta, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\log\sigma^2 - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(y - X\beta)^\top(y - X\beta)\]

The key observation: the only term involving \(\beta\) is the last one, and it is negative RSS. So maximizing the likelihood over \(\beta\) is exactly minimizing RSS — for any \(\sigma^2\):

The full MLE, including \(\sigma^2\):

The MLE of the noise variance is \(\hat\sigma^2 = \text{RSS}/n\) — the average squared residual. Note the divisor: \(n\), not \(n - p\). The MLE is biased (it ignores the \(p\) degrees of freedom spent fitting \(\beta\)); the unbiased estimator divides by \(n - p\) — the 2.4 correction, now derived rather than asserted.

2. Concept Questions

  1. The log-likelihood’s \(\beta\)-dependent term is \(-\text{RSS}/(2\sigma^2)\). Why does this mean the MLE of \(\beta\) doesn’t depend on \(\sigma^2\) — and what breaks if the noise is not i.i.d. (heteroscedastic)?
  2. The MLE of \(\sigma^2\) divides by \(n\); the unbiased estimator divides by \(n-p\). Derive the bias: show \(\mathbb{E}[\text{RSS}] = (n-p)\sigma^2\) (hint: the residual is the projection of the noise onto the orthogonal complement of the column space, which has dimension \(n-p\)).
  3. Least squares is the MLE under Gaussian noise. What is the MLE under Laplace (double-exponential) noise, and what does that imply about which estimator is robust to outliers?
  4. The Gaussian assumption gives least squares; the sampling distribution of \(\hat\beta\) (its standard errors, 2.3) also comes from it. What exactly does the Gaussian assumption buy beyond the point estimate?
  5. Why is “least squares = MLE” not a justification of least squares for causal questions — what does the likelihood say about the data-generating process, and what doesn’t it say?
Answers
  1. Because \(\sigma^2\) enters only as a positive constant multiplier of RSS in the \(\beta\)-dependent term — the argmax over \(\beta\) is unchanged by any fixed \(\sigma^2\). With heteroscedastic noise (\(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma_i^2\)), the likelihood weights each observation by \(1/\sigma_i^2\) — the MLE becomes weighted least squares (A.1 Q1), and unweighted OLS is no longer the MLE (still unbiased, but inefficient — not minimum-variance).
  2. The residual \(r = (I - H)\varepsilon\) where \(H\) is the hat matrix; \((I-H)\) is a projection onto an \((n-p)\)-dimensional space, so \(\|r\|^2 = \varepsilon^\top(I-H)\varepsilon\) has expectation \(\sigma^2 \text{tr}(I-H) = \sigma^2(n-p)\). The fit “absorbs” \(p\) dimensions of the noise — the residuals are smaller than the noise by exactly the degrees of freedom spent.
  3. Under Laplace noise the log-likelihood is \(-\sum |y_i - x_i^\top\beta|/\text{scale}\) — the MLE is least absolute deviations (LAD), which is robust to outliers (the absolute loss doesn’t square the big residuals). The correspondence is exact: Gaussian noise → squared loss → OLS; Laplace noise → absolute loss → LAD. The loss function is the noise model, read backwards.
  4. The sampling distribution: under the Gaussian model, \(\hat\beta \sim N(\beta, \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1})\) — which is where the standard errors, \(t\)-tests, and confidence intervals of 2.3 come from. The point estimate needs only the mean structure; every uncertainty statement needs the distributional assumption. (And by the CLT, \(\hat\beta\) is approximately normal even under non-Gaussian noise — the intervals are more robust than the exact theory.)
  5. The likelihood describes \(p(y \mid x, \beta)\) — the distribution of the data given the parameters. It says nothing about whether \(\beta\) represents a causal effect: that requires the exogeneity assumption \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0\) (2.10), which is a property of the data-generating process, not of the likelihood. The MLE is the best fit to the conditional distribution; “best fit” and “true effect” coincide only under assumptions the likelihood can’t verify.

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Derive the MLE of \(\sigma^2\) jointly with \(\beta\): show that plugging \(\hat\beta\) into the likelihood and maximizing over \(\sigma^2\) gives \(\hat\sigma^2 = \text{RSS}(\hat\beta)/n\), and verify the second derivative is negative (a maximum, not a minimum). Then compute the bias factor \((n-p)/n\) for the course’s typical regimes and state when the bias matters.

Solution
The first derivative is \(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{\text{RSS}}{2\sigma^4}\); setting it to zero gives \(\hat\sigma^2 = \text{RSS}/n\), and the second derivative at that point is \(-\frac{n^3}{2\text{RSS}^2} < 0\) — a maximum. The bias factor: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}}] = \frac{n-p}{n}\sigma^2\). For \(n\) = 10,000 and \(p\) = 10 the bias is 0.1% — irrelevant; for \(n\) = 50 and \(p\) = 10 it’s 20% — the difference between a calibrated and a badly miscalibrated uncertainty estimate. The rule: the MLE’s bias matters exactly when \(p/n\) is non-negligible — the small-sample, many-feature regime (5.6), which is also where the unbiased correction is most needed for honest standard errors.

Exercise 2. The loss-is-the-noise-model dictionary: for each noise distribution below, write the log-likelihood and name the resulting estimator — (a) Gaussian, (b) Laplace, (c) the contaminated Gaussian \(\varepsilon \sim 0.95 N(0,1) + 0.05 N(0, 100)\) (a few large outliers). Then simulate: fit OLS and LAD (via iteratively reweighted least squares or a simple subgradient descent) on data with 5% outliers, and compare the slope estimates.

Solution
OLS’s slope is dragged toward the outliers (the squared loss makes a 15-unit residual cost 225 units of gradient); LAD’s is nearly unaffected (the absolute loss gives the same gradient magnitude to a 15-unit residual as to a 1-unit one). The dictionary in action: choose the loss by choosing the noise model you believe — and when you don’t believe any of them, Huber (quadratic near zero, linear in the tails) is the standard compromise that keeps the best of both.

Exercise 3. The Gauss–Markov theorem, verified by simulation: under the assumptions of 2.10 (linearity, exogeneity, homoscedasticity, no collinearity), OLS is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) — minimum variance among all linear unbiased estimators, not just among OLS-type ones. Simulate: compare the variance of \(\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}}\) against an alternative linear unbiased estimator (e.g., a weighted average of the per-observation slopes with non-optimal weights) over 10,000 datasets, and verify OLS has the smaller variance.

Solution
The alternative estimator is linear and unbiased (its expectation is the true slope) but has several times the variance — Gauss–Markov verified empirically. The theorem’s content: among linear unbiased estimators, OLS’s variance \(\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) is the minimum — no reweighting, no clever alternative beats it. The caveat the theorem doesn’t tell you: “best” is within the linear-unbiased class — a biased estimator (ridge, A.4) can have lower mean squared error by trading variance for bias, which is the entire bias–variance trade-off of 4.4 in one sentence.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • The Gaussian assumption buys the intervals, not the point estimate. \(\hat\beta\) is unbiased under mere exogeneity (\(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|x]=0\)); the standard errors and \(t\)-tests need the distribution — but the CLT makes them approximately valid under much weaker conditions. The exact finite-sample theory needs Gaussianity; the asymptotic theory needs far less.
  • MLEs are not automatically unbiased — \(\hat\sigma^2\) divides by \(n\) and is biased low; the MLE of a nonlinear function is generally biased (Jensen’s inequality). “Maximum likelihood” is a consistency and efficiency claim, not an unbiasedness claim.
  • The loss function is a modeling assumption, not a default. Squared loss = Gaussian noise = outlier-sensitive. Before reaching for OLS, ask what the noise is: heavy tails (financial returns, biological measurements) argue for Huber or LAD; count data for Poisson GLMs (3.6); binary outcomes for logistic (A.3).
  • Least absolute deviations has no closed form — the absolute value isn’t differentiable at zero, so LAD needs iterative methods (IRLS, linear programming). The same non-differentiability that makes lasso sparse (A.5) makes LAD robust — the kink is doing statistical work in both.
  • The likelihood is a function of the model, not the truth. Maximizing it finds the parameters that make the data most probable under the model — if the model is wrong (wrong noise, wrong mean structure), the MLE is the best answer to the wrong question. Model checking (2.10’s residual diagnostics) is how you find out.

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated likelihood function has three bugs. Diagnose before running the fixed version.

# AI-generated: Gaussian log-likelihood for regression (will not run here — spot the bugs)
def log_likelihood(X, y, beta, sigma):
    resid = y - X @ beta
    n = len(y)
    ll = -n/2 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - n/2 * np.log(sigma) - np.sum(resid**2) / (2 * sigma)  # BUG 1
    return ll

def fit(X, y):
    beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y)[0]
    sigma = np.sqrt(np.mean((y - X @ beta)**2))
    return beta, sigma, log_likelihood(X, y, beta, sigma)   # BUG 2

def aic(X, y, beta, sigma):
    ll = log_likelihood(X, y, beta, sigma)
    return 2 * len(beta) - 2 * ll                            # BUG 3
What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. \(\sigma\) vs \(\sigma^2\): the Gaussian density has \(\log\sigma^2\) and divides by \(\sigma^2\) — the code uses \(\sigma\) in both places (missing the square). The likelihood is wrong by a factor that depends on \(\sigma\), so any comparison across models is corrupted.
  2. The MLE of \(\sigma^2\) is RSS/\(n\), not the mean of the squared residuals with the wrong divisor — actually np.mean(resid**2) is RSS/\(n\), so this line is right; the bug is that fit returns the likelihood computed with the unsquared \(\sigma\) from bug 1 — the two functions are inconsistent. (The deeper issue: the code never checks that the returned likelihood is the maximum — it just evaluates at the OLS \(\beta\), which is correct for \(\beta\) but the \(\sigma\) handling is broken.)
  3. AIC’s parameter count: AIC counts all estimated parameters — \(\beta\) and \(\sigma^2\) — so the penalty is \(2(p+1)\), not \(2p\). The missing \(+2\) is small but it’s the difference between the correct criterion and a slightly-too-generous one.
# fixed
def log_likelihood(X, y, beta, sigma2):
    resid = y - X @ beta
    n = len(y)
    return -n/2 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - n/2 * np.log(sigma2) - np.sum(resid**2) / (2 * sigma2)

def fit(X, y):
    beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
    sigma2 = np.sum((y - X @ beta)**2) / len(y)      # MLE: RSS/n
    return beta, sigma2, log_likelihood(X, y, beta, sigma2)

def aic(X, y, beta, sigma2):
    return 2 * (len(beta) + 1) - 2 * log_likelihood(X, y, beta, sigma2)

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. The MLE under a misspecified noise model: suppose the truth is Laplace noise but you fit OLS (the Gaussian MLE). Show that OLS is still consistent for the conditional mean (if the mean structure is right) but is no longer the minimum-variance estimator — and compute the asymptotic relative efficiency of OLS vs LAD under Laplace noise (the answer is a famous number). Then state the general principle.

Hint Under Laplace noise, the asymptotic variance of the LAD estimator is \(b^2 / n\) (scale squared), while OLS’s is \(\sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\) with \(\sigma^2 = 2b^2\) — compare.
Solution Under Laplace noise with scale \(b\): \(\sigma^2 = \text{Var} = 2b^2\), so OLS’s asymptotic variance is \(2b^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) per unit; the LAD (MLE) asymptotic variance is \(b^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) — the ratio is 2, so OLS uses half the data efficiency (asymptotic relative efficiency \(1/2\); the famous result is that under Laplace noise, LAD is twice as efficient as OLS — and under Gaussian noise, OLS is infinitely more efficient than LAD in the tail sense). The general principle: the MLE is efficient under the true model; under misspecification, the pseudo-MLE (OLS here) remains consistent for the mean but loses efficiency — the loss is bounded and computable, and the robust choice depends on how wrong you think the model is. This is the 2.10 diagnostic program turned into a theorem: check the residuals, and if they’re heavy-tailed, the Gaussian MLE is leaving efficiency on the table.

Q2. From likelihood to Bayes: place a prior \(\beta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\) on the coefficients and derive the posterior mode. Show it equals the ridge solution \((X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\) with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\) — the Bayesian reading of A.4. Then state what the prior means (in words a reviewer would accept) and why this makes ridge’s \(\lambda\) interpretable rather than arbitrary.

Hint The posterior is proportional to likelihood × prior; the log-prior is \(-\|\beta\|^2/(2\tau^2)\) — a quadratic penalty.
Solution \(\log p(\beta \mid y) \propto \ell(\beta) - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\beta\|^2 = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\text{RSS} - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\beta\|^2 + \text{const}\). Maximizing over \(\beta\) is minimizing \(\text{RSS} + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\|\beta\|^2\) — ridge with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\). The prior means: “before seeing the data, I believe the coefficients are centered at zero with spread \(\tau\)” — a statement about effect sizes, not a fudge factor. The interpretability: \(\lambda\) is the ratio of noise to signal — large \(\lambda\) means the data is noisy relative to your prior belief in the coefficients, so shrink hard; small \(\lambda\) means trust the data. This is why ridge’s \(\lambda\) isn’t arbitrary to choose: it’s the noise-to-prior ratio, and cross-validation (4.6) is just estimating that ratio from the data. The Bayesian view also explains ridge’s one weakness: the prior is centered at zero, which is right for “no effect” but wrong when effects are known to be large — the empirical-Bayes fix (estimate \(\tau\) from the data) is the next step.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (MLE × A.3 logistic × 3.3). Logistic regression is the MLE under a Bernoulli model — derive its log-likelihood and gradient the same way this page derives OLS’s, and note the structural parallel: both are GLMs with a canonical link, both have gradients of the form (prediction − target) × features, and both need iterative solvers because the mean is a nonlinear function of the parameters. The Gaussian case is the special one where the linearity makes the MLE closed-form.

C2 (MLE × 12.5 resampling × 2.3). The bootstrap is a likelihood-free way to get the sampling distribution of \(\hat\beta\) — resample the rows, refit, repeat. Contrast with the Gaussian theory: the bootstrap needs no distributional assumption but costs \(B\) refits; the theory is exact under Gaussianity but approximate otherwise. Run both on the same dataset (the drill-3 simulation) and state when each is preferable — the 12.5 answer, now with the Gaussian benchmark to compare against.

7. Summary

  • Under Gaussian noise, the log-likelihood’s \(\beta\)-term is \(-\text{RSS}/2\sigma^2\) — so the MLE of \(\beta\) is the least-squares solution, for any \(\sigma^2\).
  • The MLE of \(\sigma^2\) is RSS/\(n\) — biased low by \((n-p)/n\); the unbiased estimator divides by \(n-p\) (the degrees of freedom the fit spends).
  • The loss function is the noise model read backwards: Gaussian → squared, Laplace → absolute (robust), contaminated → Huber.
  • Gauss–Markov: OLS is minimum-variance among linear unbiased estimators — but biased estimators (ridge) can win on MSE (4.4).
  • The Gaussian assumption buys the intervals; the point estimate needs only exogeneity, and the CLT covers the rest approximately.
  • Ridge is the posterior mode under a Gaussian prior — \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\), the noise-to-prior ratio (A.4).
