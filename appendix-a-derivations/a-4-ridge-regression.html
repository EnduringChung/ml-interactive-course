A.4 Ridge Regression: Deriving the Closed Form

A.1’s normal equations break when \(X^\top X\) is singular; A.3’s likelihood breaks under separation. Ridge regression is the one-line fix for both: add \(\lambda\|\beta\|^2\) to the objective. This page derives the closed form, shows why \(\lambda I\) is exactly the right repair, and reads the solution through the SVD — where ridge’s behavior becomes visible.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live (sympy for the symbolic derivation, NumPy for the check).

1. Basic Concept

The objective. Ridge minimizes the residual sum of squares plus a penalty proportional to the squared coefficient magnitude:

\[\hat\beta_\lambda = \arg\min_\beta \; \underbrace{(y - X\beta)^\top(y - X\beta)}_{\text{RSS}} + \lambda \underbrace{\beta^\top\beta}_{\|\beta\|^2}\]

The derivation — A.1’s calculus with one extra term:

Setting the gradient to zero: \(-2X^\top(X\beta - y) + 2\lambda\beta = 0\), i.e.

\[(X^\top X + \lambda I)\,\beta = X^\top y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat\beta_\lambda = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\]

Why \(\lambda I\) is exactly the right repair: \(X^\top X\) is positive semidefinite — its eigenvalues are \(\ge 0\), and singularity means some are exactly zero (the collinear directions of A.1). Adding \(\lambda I\) shifts every eigenvalue up by \(\lambda\) — the zero eigenvalues become \(\lambda > 0\), the matrix becomes positive definite, and the inverse exists for any \(\lambda > 0\). The penalty doesn’t just regularize — it repairs the algebra.

2. Concept Questions

  1. The ridge solution is \((X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\). What happens as \(\lambda \to 0\) and as \(\lambda \to \infty\), and why are both limits the “right” answers?
  2. The SVD view: with \(X = UDV^\top\), show \(\hat\beta_\lambda = \sum_j \frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + \lambda} (u_j^\top y) v_j\). Which singular directions does ridge shrink most, and why is that the right thing to shrink?
  3. Why is the intercept (and generally, any unpenalized coefficient) excluded from the penalty? What breaks if you penalize it?
  4. Ridge requires standardized features — why does the penalty’s scale-dependence make standardization a mathematical requirement, not a convenience?
  5. The Bayesian reading (A.2 Q2): ridge is the posterior mode under \(\beta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\). What does \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\) mean in words, and how does it tell you when to use a large \(\lambda\)?
Answers
  1. \(\lambda \to 0\): \(\hat\beta_\lambda \to (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\) — the OLS solution (recovered when \(X^\top X\) is invertible). \(\lambda \to \infty\): every coefficient \(\to 0\) — the penalty dominates and the model predicts the mean. Both limits are the correct extremes: no regularization and total shrinkage.
  2. The shrinkage factor is \(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\) (multiplying the OLS coefficient’s component along \(v_j\)). Directions with large singular values \(d_j\) (strong, well-identified signal) are barely shrunk; directions with small \(d_j\) (weak, noisy — the collinear ones) are shrunk toward zero. Ridge spends its bias budget exactly where the data is weakest — the 4.4 trade-off made visible in the spectrum.
  3. The intercept represents the mean of \(y\) — penalizing it shrinks the predictions toward zero, which is wrong whenever the outcome’s mean isn’t zero (it forces the other coefficients to compensate, biasing them). The calibration guarantee (\(\sum r_i = 0\), A.2) also breaks. Standard practice: center \(y\) and \(X\), fit without an intercept, add the mean back.
  4. The penalty \(\lambda\|\beta\|^2\) is in units of the coefficients — and the units depend on the feature’s scale. A feature measured in millimeters has coefficients 1000× smaller than the same feature in meters, so the penalty treats them 1000× differently. Standardizing (mean 0, variance 1) puts every feature in the same units, making \(\lambda\) comparable across coefficients — without it, \(\lambda\) is not one knob but a different knob per feature.
  5. \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\) is the noise-to-prior ratio: \(\sigma^2\) is how noisy the data is, \(\tau^2\) is how large you believe the coefficients are a priori. Large \(\lambda\) = noisy data or small believed effects = shrink hard. This makes \(\lambda\) interpretable: it’s not a magic number but a statement about signal-to-noise, and cross-validation (4.6) is just estimating that ratio from the data.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Derive the SVD form of the ridge solution: with \(X = UDV^\top\) ( \(D\) diagonal with \(d_j\) ), show \(\hat\beta_\lambda = V \,\text{diag}\!\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\right) U^\top y\), and verify it against the direct solve on a random matrix. Then state what happens to the coefficients along each singular direction as \(\lambda\) grows.

Solution
The SVD form: \(X^\top X = VD^2V^\top\), so \((X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} = V\,\text{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\right)V^\top\), and \(\hat\beta_\lambda = V\,\text{diag}\!\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\right)U^\top y\). The OLS solution has factor \(1/d_j\) along direction \(j\) — which explodes for small \(d_j\) (the collinear directions). Ridge’s factor \(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\) is bounded by \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}\) — no explosion, ever. As \(\lambda\) grows, every direction shrinks toward zero, but the small-\(d_j\) directions shrink first (their factor hits half-max at \(d_j = \sqrt{\lambda}\)). Ridge is a spectral filter: it keeps the well-identified directions and damps the noisy ones.

Exercise 2. The Bayesian derivation: place the prior \(\beta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\) on the coefficients of the A.2 Gaussian model and derive the posterior mode. Show it equals the ridge solution with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\), and verify numerically that the posterior mean (which equals the mode here) shrinks toward zero as the prior tightens.

Solution
The posterior is Gaussian (likelihood Gaussian × prior Gaussian = Gaussian), so the mode equals the mean, and it is exactly the ridge solution with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\). The prior’s meaning: “before the data, I believe the coefficients are zero-centered with spread \(\tau\).” The posterior is a precision-weighted compromise: data precision \(1/\sigma^2\) pulls toward the OLS solution, prior precision \(1/\tau^2\) pulls toward zero, and \(\lambda\) is the exchange rate between them. Numerical check: as \(\tau^2\) decreases (tighter prior), the posterior mean shrinks toward zero — the prior’s pull strengthens.

Exercise 3. The \(\lambda\) path: compute ridge solutions for \(\lambda\) on a log grid from \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^4\) on a collinear dataset, and plot each coefficient against \(\lambda\) (a regularization path). Verify three behaviors: (a) as \(\lambda \to 0\), the coefficients approach the OLS solution (which may be wild under collinearity); (b) as \(\lambda\) grows, all coefficients shrink smoothly to zero; (c) the collinear pair’s coefficients share the signal as \(\lambda\) grows (they move together) — ridge’s “fairness” toward correlated features.

Solution
The path is the bias–variance trade-off (4.4) made visible: at the left edge, OLS’s variance is on full display (the collinear coefficients swing wildly); as \(\lambda\) grows, the variance collapses and the bias grows smoothly. The collinear pair’s “sharing” is ridge’s answer to 5.1’s selection problem: where lasso picks one of the correlated features, ridge keeps both and splits the effect — the coefficients move together, and the sum is stable even as the individual split wobbles.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • \(\lambda\) is in units of the data. The objective is \(\text{RSS} + \lambda\|\beta\|^2\) — RSS is in units of \(y^2\), so \(\lambda\)’s meaning depends on \(n\) and the feature scales. This is why every implementation standardizes first and why \(\lambda\) values aren’t comparable across datasets. The path (Exercise 3) is the scale-free way to see it.
  • Don’t penalize the intercept — and more generally, don’t penalize coefficients you want at their natural value (e.g., a known confounder). Partial penalization (penalty factors) is the generalization.
  • Ridge never zeroes a coefficient — the L2 ball is round, so the solution is always interior (all coefficients nonzero). If you need selection (exact zeros), that’s lasso’s L1 diamond — A.5 derives why the shapes differ. Elastic net (5.4) mixes the two.
  • The SVD view is the right mental model: ridge = spectral filter. Small singular values = directions the data doesn’t identify = noise amplification; ridge damps them. When you understand the solution as “OLS with the noisy directions filtered,” the hyperparameter choice becomes intuitive.
  • Generalized ridge: any positive definite matrix \(P\) can replace \(I\) — \(\text{RSS} + \lambda\beta^\top P\beta\) encodes structured priors (e.g., \(P\) = a graph Laplacian for spatial smoothness). The derivation is identical; only the meaning of the penalty changes.

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated ridge solver has three bugs. Diagnose before running the fixed version.

# AI-generated: ridge regression (will not run here — spot the bugs)
def ridge(X, y, lam):
    n, p = X.shape
    beta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(p), X.T @ y)   # BUG 1 (below)
    return beta

def standardize(X):
    return X / X.std(axis=0)                    # BUG 2

X = np.array([[1, 2], [1, 4], [1, 6]], dtype=float)   # BUG 3
y = np.array([1, 2, 3], dtype=float)
What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. The intercept is penalized: the np.eye(p) adds \(\lambda\) to the intercept’s diagonal too — shrinking the mean of \(y\) toward zero and biasing every slope. Fix: penalize only the non-intercept coefficients — add \(\lambda\) to the submatrix, or center \(y\) and \(X\) and fit without an intercept.
  2. Standardization without centering: X / X.std() scales but doesn’t center — the features keep their means, so the intercept column (if present) isn’t orthogonal to them, and the penalty still distorts the fit. Standardization is both: (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0).
  3. The design matrix is integer-typed: np.array([[1, 2], ...]) without dtype=float creates int64 — X.T @ X is integer arithmetic and the solve can silently truncate. Always cast.
# fixed
def standardize(X):
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    return (X - X.mean(axis=0)) / X.std(axis=0)

def ridge(X, y, lam):
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float); y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
    yc = y - y.mean()
    Xc = standardize(X[:, 1:])                     # center + scale the features
    beta = np.linalg.solve(Xc.T @ Xc + lam * np.eye(Xc.shape[1]), Xc.T @ yc)
    return np.concatenate([[y.mean()], beta])     # intercept = mean (unpenalized)

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Ridge as a spectral filter, quantified: using the SVD form, compute the effective degrees of freedom of a ridge fit: \(\text{df}(\lambda) = \sum_j \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\). Show it decreases from \(p\) (at \(\lambda = 0\)) to \(0\) (as \(\lambda \to \infty\)), and verify it against the hat matrix trace \(\text{tr}(H_\lambda)\) where \(H_\lambda = X(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\). Then state why this is the right complexity measure for the bias–variance trade-off.

Hint The hat matrix’s trace counts the “number of parameters the fit effectively uses” — for OLS it’s exactly \(p\); for ridge it’s the sum of the shrinkage factors.
Solution \(H_\lambda = X(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top = U\,\text{diag}\!\left(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\right)U^\top\), so \(\text{tr}(H_\lambda) = \sum_j \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\). At \(\lambda = 0\) the sum is \(p\) (OLS uses all \(p\) directions); as \(\lambda \to \infty\) it decays to 0 (the fit uses nothing). Each direction contributes its shrinkage factor — the well-identified directions count almost fully, the noisy ones count not at all. This is the right complexity measure because the variance of the fit is \(\sigma^2 \text{tr}(H_\lambda^2)\) and the effective number of parameters controls the variance–bias balance: \(\text{df}(\lambda)\) is the dial of 4.4, set by \(\lambda\). It’s also what makes generalized cross-validation (5.2) cheap: GCV’s formula only needs \(\text{tr}(H_\lambda)\), not the full hat matrix.

Q2. The limit connection — ridge as OLS on augmented data: show that the ridge solution is the OLS solution on the augmented system \(\tilde X = [X; \sqrt{\lambda}\,I]\), \(\tilde y = [y; 0]\) — stack \(\sqrt{\lambda}\) rows of “the coefficients should be zero” onto the data. Then state the two readings this gives: (a) the penalty as data (pseudo-observations pulling \(\beta\) to zero), and (b) why this makes ridge’s solution always exist even when \(X^\top X\) is singular.

Hint The augmented RSS is \(\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|^2\) — exactly the ridge objective. The normal equations of the augmented system are the ridge normal equations.
Solution The augmented OLS problem \(\min_\beta \|\tilde y - \tilde X\beta\|^2\) has objective \(\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \|\sqrt{\lambda}I\beta - 0\|^2 = \text{RSS} + \lambda\|\beta\|^2\) — the ridge objective, so the solutions coincide. Reading (a): the penalty is \(p\) extra observations saying “the coefficient vector is zero,” each with weight \(\sqrt{\lambda}\) — the prior made literal. Reading (b): the augmented design \(\tilde X = [X; \sqrt{\lambda}I]\) has full column rank for any \(\lambda > 0\) (the appended \(\sqrt{\lambda}I\) supplies the missing directions), so \(\tilde X^\top \tilde X = X^\top X + \lambda I\) is always invertible — ridge is OLS on a system the data alone couldn’t identify. This is the cleanest statement of what regularization is: adding just enough information to make the problem well-posed, whether that information is a prior, a penalty, or pseudo-data.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (ridge × A.5 lasso × 5.3). Ridge’s L2 ball gives smooth shrinkage; lasso’s L1 diamond gives exact zeros. Derive both from the same constrained form — \(\min \text{RSS}\) s.t. \(\|\beta\|_q \le t\) — and show the constraint’s shape (round vs pointy) is the whole difference: the round ball’s smooth boundary is never axis-aligned, so the constrained solution never lands exactly on an axis; the diamond’s corners are on the axes, so the solution lands there and zeroes coefficients. The geometry of the constraint is the geometry of the solution.

C2 (ridge × A.6 PCA × 5.5). Ridge and PCR both damp the small singular directions of \(X\) — ridge smoothly, PCR by truncation. Show that PCR (regress \(y\) on the top-\(k\) principal components) is the \(\lambda \to \infty\) limit of ridge per direction: ridge with \(\lambda\) between \(d_{k+1}^2\) and \(d_k^2\) keeps directions \(1..k\) almost fully and damps the rest — a soft truncation. Then state when you’d prefer each (interpretability vs smoothness), and why partial least squares (5.5) — which finds directions that matter for \(y\), not just \(X\) — is the \(y\)-aware version of the same idea.

7. Summary

  • Ridge: \(\hat\beta_\lambda = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\) — A.1’s normal equations with \(\lambda I\) added.
  • \(\lambda I\) shifts every eigenvalue up by \(\lambda\): the singular directions become invertible — the penalty repairs the algebra.
  • The SVD view: ridge shrinks each singular direction by \(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\) — strong directions kept, weak directions damped. OLS’s \(1/d_j\) explosion is bounded by \(1/(2\sqrt{\lambda})\).
  • Standardize first (the penalty is scale-dependent); never penalize the intercept.
  • Ridge is the posterior mode under \(\beta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\) — \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\), the noise-to-prior ratio.
  • Ridge = OLS on augmented data \([X; \sqrt{\lambda}I]\), \([y; 0]\) — regularization as information, not magic.
