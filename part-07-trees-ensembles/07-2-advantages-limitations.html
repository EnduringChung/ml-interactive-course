7.2 Advantages and Limitations of Trees

Before averaging trees into powerhouses, inventory what a single tree gives and costs — because the ensembles of 7.3–7.6 inherit every item on this list, transformed. Knowing which properties survive averaging (and which don’t) is the difference between using forests well and misreading them.

2. Concept Questions

  1. List four tree properties that survive bagging/boosting, and two that are transformed or lost.
  2. Why is “trees capture interactions automatically” only half-true in shallow trees?
  3. Trees are insensitive to outliers in y for classification but not for regression — explain precisely.
  4. Name a setting where a single small tree is the right deliverable, and what you sacrifice.
  5. Which limitation is fixed by averaging many trees, and which is merely hidden by it?
Answers
  1. Survive: scale-invariance, mixed/unnormalized data handling, axis-parallel boundary bias, automatic interaction handling (given depth), no extrapolation (flat leaves). Transformed/lost: single-tree interpretability (a forest is not readable), instability (averaged away — the point), piecewise-constant roughness (averaging smooths it).
  2. Interactions require depth: an interaction between x₁ and x₂ needs splits on both — reachable at depth 2 only in the best case, and greedy search may not find the pair at all (7.1 Q1). “Automatically” = “reachable given depth and data volume,” not “guaranteed.”
  3. Classification: a huge y-outlier is just another correctly-classified point — impurity measures don’t see magnitude. Regression: leaf means are arithmetic averages, so one enormous y in a leaf drags every prediction in it (1.1’s mean-vs-median story; robust leaves exist but aren’t default).
  4. Clinical/policy triage rules, credit-decline reasons, A/B guardrail rules — anywhere a human must read and apply the rule verbatim. Sacrificed: accuracy vs ensembles/linear models on smooth truths, and stability of the exact thresholds.
  5. Fixed by averaging: variance/instability (and much of the jaggedness). Hidden, not fixed: axis-parallel bias (all trees in the ensemble share it — ensembles approximate diagonal boundaries as finer and finer staircases, not lines), and no-extrapolation.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. The stability experiment, quantified: for 100 bootstrap resamples of one dataset, fit (a) a depth-3 tree, (b) depth-3 tree with max_features=1 (random split choice), (c) a linear regression. Measure prediction SD at fixed test points and the disagreement rate between fits (fraction of test points where two fits differ by > 0.5·noise). Rank the three by instability and connect to why 7.3’s averaging works best on the unstable ones.

Solution
Linear regression barely moves (stable learner — averaging buys ~nothing, as 7.3 Q2 will show formally); random-split trees wobble most; plain trees sit between. Bagging’s payoff is a function of instability — that’s the entire design logic of the next two pages.

Exercise 2. Interpretability deliverable: fit a depth-3 classification tree on the cohort-style data below and produce (a) the text rules, (b) a one-paragraph “policy explanation” a non-statistician could follow, (c) the same explanation from a 200-leaf tree — then reflect on what “interpretable” means operationally. Verify the small tree’s predictions on 3 hand-traced patients.

Solution
The depth-3 tree’s rules are literally readable (“marker above ~11 AND age above ~55 → positive”), each prediction traceable by hand — the deliverable in clinical/policy settings. The 200-leaf tree’s “explanation” is a document no human reads; interpretability is a property of the fitted size, not the class. That’s why small trees remain legitimate tools despite inferior accuracy — and why ensembles export separate explanations (importances, partial dependence) rather than rules.

Exercise 3. Limitation stress-test: three data pathologies — (a) smooth trend with time (y = 0.5t + noise), (b) linear boundary at 45°, (c) heavy-tailed y in regression — each evaluated for a pruned tree vs its natural rival (linear model / logistic / median-regression-style robust tree via criterion="absolute_error"). Report the paired test-MSE/accuracy gaps and write the “when not to use a tree” checklist.

Solution
Checklist: don’t reach for a single tree when (i) the signal is smooth/linear/trending — linear models interpolate it for free while trees staircase and flatline; (ii) the boundary is oblique — axis-parallel greediness pays a staircase tax; (iii) regression y is heavy-tailed — squared-error leaf means chase outliers (the absolute-criterion tree is the in-class remedy); (iv) the deliverable is calibrated probability or extrapolation — neither is native. Each limitation has an ensemble-level patch, but patches work on the ensemble, not the story you tell about a single tree.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Bias list (survives ensembling): axis-parallel splits; no extrapolation; leaf-mean predictions; greedy (not optimal) split search. Variance list (averaging fixes): instability to resamples, threshold jitter, jaggedness.
  • “Nonparametric” ≠ “assumption-free”: trees assume piecewise-constant regions and axis alignment — smooth diagonal truths violate both, and ensembles only approximate the violation at computational cost.
  • Categorical levels: high-cardinality categoricals get greedy advantage in impurity ranking (many ways to split); group rare levels first (5.1 instability note).
  • min_samples_leaf as uncertainty honesty: leaf means from 3 observations are noise; set it to keep every prediction defensibly backed — or report leaf sizes alongside predictions.
  • Missing values: sklearn’s trees don’t accept NaN (except HistGradientBoosting, 7.6); imputation choices interact with splits — keep them inside the pipeline (0.5).
  • The reading trap: export_text on a deep tree looks interpretable (it’s just rules) but no human tracks 200 nested conditions — interpretation budget is a function of size, not syntax.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Formalize scale-invariance: prove that applying any strictly increasing transform to feature j changes neither the set of possible splits on j nor any impurity computation — hence the fitted tree (up to threshold relabeling) is identical. Contrast with ridge/PCA/KNN, citing the specific mechanism each uses magnitudes for. What residual role does scaling play in tree pipelines (name one real reason you might still standardize)?

Hint Splits are thresholds x_j > c; a monotone transform maps thresholds bijectively and preserves orderings — sorting is all the algorithm uses.
Solution Trees sort feature j and evaluate candidate cuts between consecutive values; g monotone increasing permutes nothing in that order and maps cut c to g(c), preserving both partitions and impurity sums — the tree is invariant. Ridge penalizes β_j (unit-dependent), PCA ranks variance (scale-dependent), KNN distances (scale-dependent): all magnitude-users, all scale-sensitive. Residual reason to standardize trees: numerical reproducibility of tie-breaking, and pipeline uniformity when tree ensembles sit beside linear members (stacking) — never for the tree’s own benefit.

Q2. Tree stability as a function of n: for n ∈ {30, 100, 400}, fit depth-3 trees on 200 bootstrap resamples each and record (a) the SD of predictions at fixed test points, (b) the probability that the root feature is the same across resamples. Sketch both against n and connect the root-stability curve to the sampling-variability of a correlation estimate (1.2 Exercise 3) — why does the root stabilize faster than the full tree’s predictions?

Solution
Root-feature agreement climbs quickly with n (the dominant signal asserts itself — like a correlation estimate whose sign stabilizes faster than its value), while prediction SD declines much more slowly (every leaf threshold keeps wobbling). The asymmetry explains a practical pattern: trees agree on which drivers matter long before they agree on what they predict — a distinction to respect when reporting importances vs predictions.

7. Summary

  • Assets: scale-invariance, mixed data, automatic interactions (with depth), interpretable when small, robustness in classification.
  • Liabilities: instability, axis-parallel bias, no extrapolation, jagged regression fits, poor calibrated probabilities.
  • Bagging/boosting transform the liability sheet — but axis-parallel bias and flat extrapolation are inherited, not cured.
  • “Interpretable” is a statement about the fitted size; export_text on a maze is not interpretability.
  • Classification is outlier-blind; regression leaf means are outlier-sensitive — pick criteria accordingly.
  • Single trees remain the right deliverable when a human must execute the rule.
  • The instability that plagues single trees is exactly what averaging consumes: 7.3 Bagging.
