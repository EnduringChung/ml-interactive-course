7.7 Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART)

BART keeps boosting’s “sum of small trees” architecture but replaces sequential greedy fitting with a Bayesian posterior: a prior over tree shapes and leaf values, MCMC sampling over the space of tree ensembles, and — the payoff — honest uncertainty intervals on predictions from a nonlinear, interaction-friendly model. No BART library runs in Pyodide, so this page builds the Bayesian machinery the model rests on, hands you the concepts, and flags where the production implementations live.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live (the Bayesian core; the full BART sampler is conceptual).

1. Basic Concept

The BART model:

\[y = \sum_{m=1}^M g(x; T_m, M_m) + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\]

— M small trees (T = shape, M = leaf values) with a regularizing prior: shallow trees favored (deep splits penalized), leaf values shrunk toward 0, σ² given its own prior. Fitting = MCMC over (T₁…T_M, M₁…M_M, σ²), sampling tree grows/prunes/swaps and leaf draws.

2. Concept Questions

  1. What does BART’s tree-shape prior (prefer shallow, shrink leaves) accomplish, in regularization language (5.2)?
  2. Contrast fitting: boosting finds one greedy sequential ensemble; BART samples many ensembles from a posterior. What does the second buy at prediction time?
  3. Why is BART’s uncertainty interval meaningfully different from a bootstrap band (4.5) — even though both are intervals around predictions?
  4. The sum-of-trees form with M small trees resembles boosting’s Σνhₘ. What replaces boosting’s learning rate and residual chasing?
  5. Name two practical reasons BART remains niche despite its uncertainty output — and the niches where it wins anyway.
Answers
  1. A prior favoring shallow trees and shrunken leaves is L0-on-structure + shrinkage-on-values simultaneously: the model starts regularized, and the data moves it only as far as evidence pushes — the Bayesian rendering of “keep trees weak” (boosting enforces it by construction via depth limits and ν).
  2. A posterior predictive distribution: every future prediction can carry its own interval, and new-observation uncertainty is separate from fit uncertainty (the σ² layer). Boosting gives a point prediction; intervals must be bolted on (4.5-style), with weaker guarantees.
  3. Bootstrap bands reflect resampling variability of a procedure, assuming the model; posterior credible bands reflect conditional uncertainty given the prior and likelihood — they can differ when the prior matters, and they natively separate parameter-uncertainty from observation-noise layers.
  4. The prior plays ν’s role (shallow+shrunk = small steps are likely a priori), and MCMC’s grow/prune moves play residual-chasing: the chain revises trees wherever the current ensemble’s residuals are largest, stochastically rather than greedily.
    1. MCMC compute: thousands of tree-ensemble draws vs one greedy pass — slow on big n/p; (b) implementation maturity/deployment tooling (boosting libraries are industrial; BART’s are research-grade, R-centric). It wins where uncertainty-qualified predictions are the product — clinical dosing, causal-effect estimation with interactions, small-n scientific inference.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

The first block is the bug worth internalizing: a deterministic learner has no posterior. Setting random_state=0..19 on a depth-2 tree fitted to the same rows produces twenty bit-identical fits, so “averaging over the posterior” silently degenerates to “fitting once” — the printed SDs are equal to three decimals, and any claim about posterior spread built on that is fiction. Posterior variation must come from somewhere real. BART’s source is honest: it grows each tree on a noisy copy of (X, y), perturbing both the split rule and the leaf values, so successive draws genuinely differ. The bootstrap+jitter surrogate above does the same thing cheaply, and the ρ ≈ 0.9 it reports is itself the useful number — posterior draws agree a lot (they all chase the same sine) but not perfectly, and the disagreement is exactly the width of the credible band. The mean over draws is a smoother function than any single draw, which is the mechanism BART relies on.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Full conjugate workflow: given X (n = 50, 3 features incl. intercept), y, prior N(0, τ²I) with τ = 2, σ² = 1: compute (a) posterior mean and covariance, (b) 95% credible intervals for each β, (c) the posterior probability that β₂ > 0 (via draws). Compare the interval for β₂ against the frequentist OLS CI (statsmodels). Where do they differ, and which prior choice drove the difference?

Solution
With τ = 2 the prior is weak — Bayesian and OLS intervals nearly coincide (the data dominates). Shrink τ and the Bayesian intervals contract toward 0 by assumption, while OLS stays data-only; the difference between the two outputs is exactly the prior’s influence — know what you chose. The P(β₂ > 0) statement is the Bayesian bonus: direct probability claims about parameters, no null-hypothesis machinery.

Exercise 2. The sum-of-trees prior, simulated: implement a toy “BART-lite”: fit B = 200 tiny ensembles where each ensemble is 5 depth-2 trees fit on bootstrap data with leaf values shrunk by a factor κ ∈ {0.2, 1.0}; predict as the average. Measure test MSE and the SD of predictions across ensembles at fixed points, for both κ. Shrinkage (small κ) is the prior doing regularization — verify its variance/accuracy signature and connect it to boosting’s learning rate ν (7.5).

Solution
κ = 0.2 (strong prior shrinkage) tames each tree’s contribution — lower variance across ensembles, often better test MSE on noisy truths; κ = 1 lets each tree speak at full volume (variance up). This is ν-as-prior: BART’s leaf-shrinkage prior plays precisely boosting’s learning-rate role, but inside a sampling framework where the “right” amount is informed by the data through σ² and the marginal likelihood.

Exercise 3. Uncertainty layers, separated: using the conjugate model, produce three intervals for a new observation at a test point x₀: (a) the posterior interval of the mean f(x₀), (b) the predictive interval of a new y, (c) the OLS-style interval ignoring parameter uncertainty (just ±1.96σ̂). Plot all three and compute coverage of each over 500 simulated datasets. Which layer does each interval answer — and which would you show a patient (a new individual) vs a review board (the model’s claim)?

Solution
Read these in the order a decision-maker would, because the ordering is the lesson. (b) is the only interval that answers the question you usually have. A predictive interval for a new patient must contain both parameter uncertainty and the new observation’s own noise, so it is the widest of the three and it overcovers here (1.00) because σ = 0.5 is large relative to the parameter spread at n = 40 — harmless conservatism. (a) is narrower and calibrated for a different question, “where is the mean function here” (0.95, as intended). (c) is the trap: it ignores parameter uncertainty entirely and, because σ happens to dwarf that uncertainty at this n, it overcovers (1.00) rather than undercovering. The direction of (c)’s failure is set by the ratio of σ to the posterior SD — with small n, or a noisier signal, that ratio inverts and (c) becomes undercovering. So “it undercovers whenever parameter uncertainty is real” is the wrong claim: the safe statement is that (c) is un-calibrated in an n-dependent direction, which is a stronger reason to avoid it than pessimism would be. BART’s headline feature is delivering (a) and (b) for nonlinear tree ensembles — the same layering, no conjugate shortcut required.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Priors are design decisions: BART’s defaults (depth-limiting probabilities, β-shrinkage quantiles, σ prior) encode “sparse, weak interactions” — check them against your problem instead of outsourcing the choice to the library default.
  • MCMC diagnostics are mandatory: run multiple chains, check mixing of σ² and tree counts; BART’s credible intervals are only as trustworthy as the chain’s convergence — the Bayesian tax for the uncertainty feature.
  • BART is not fast: minutes on n = 1,000 where boosting takes milliseconds; parallel chains help but the algorithm is inherently sequential. For speed-uncertainty trade-offs, quantile-regression forests are the cheaper cousin.
  • Variable selection leanings: BART underuses weak features under strong priors (like all shrinkage) — presence in the model ≠ importance; use inclusion frequencies across MCMC draws as the diagnostic.
  • Causal inference is BART’s flagship niche (treatment-effect estimation with exchangeability assumptions): the posterior over heterogeneous effects is exactly what applied micro is after — but the causal validity lives in the design, as always (2.2 Q2).
  • Binary/count responses: BART extends via probit/logit link versions (MCMC gets latent-variable steps) — the additive-GAM-to-GLM move (6.7) replayed in Bayesian key.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Precision/convention flip: the conjugate posterior is (XᵀX/σ² + I/τ²)⁻¹ — multiplying by τ² (instead of dividing) strengthens the prior as τ² grows, backwards. Get the precision algebra right; it’s the entire estimator.
  2. Posterior SD ≠ rescaled mean: it’s √diag(posterior covariance). Hand-waving SDs make every downstream interval fiction.
  3. Predictive intervals need σ²: new observations add the noise layer — parameter uncertainty alone undercovers new points (Exercise 3’s layer separation).

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the conjugate update: prior β ~ N(0, τ²I), likelihood y ~ N(Xβ, σ²I) ⇒ posterior N(μₙ, Σₙ) with Σₙ = (XᵀX/σ² + I/τ²)⁻¹, μₙ = ΣₙXᵀy/σ². Verify that as τ² → ∞ you recover OLS and as τ² → 0 you recover β = 0 — the two endpoints matching ridge’s λ → 0/∞ (5.2 Q2). Then compute the posterior probability P(β_j > 0) via draws and show it equals Φ(μⱼ/√Σⱼⱼ) — no simulation needed.

Hint Complete the square on β in the joint density; Gaussian arithmetic; the marginal of a Gaussian is Gaussian.
Solution

Joint ∝ exp(−‖y − Xβ‖²/2σ² − ‖β‖²/2τ²); expanding in β and completing the square gives precision Λ = XᵀX/σ² + I/τ² and mean μₙ = Λ⁻¹Xᵀy/σ². Limits: τ² → ∞ removes the prior term → Λ = XᵀX/σ², μₙ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy = OLS; τ² → 0 forces μₙ → 0. Since marginals of Gaussians are Gaussian, β_j | y ~ N(μⱼ, Σⱼⱼ), so P(β_j > 0) = Φ(μⱼ/√Σⱼⱼ):

The closed form is the sampling-based one at infinite draws — Bayesian inference for conjugate models is exactly computed, and BART’s MCMC exists because tree ensembles forfeit conjugacy, not because sampling is inherently superior.

Q2. BART vs boosting uncertainty, head-to-head design: on 200 simulated datasets (n = 60, smooth truth), compare three interval producers — (a) boosted trees + residual ± band, (b) bootstrap-banded forest (4.5), (c) the conjugate Bayesian interval on the correct basis — by nominal-95% coverage of the truth and of new points. Which achieves nominal coverage for the function, which for observations, and what structural feature does (c) have that (a)/(b) must borrow? Write the practical guidance you’d give before someone reaches for bands.

Solution
The correctly-specified Bayesian interval hits nominal coverage for both layers because it carries the two uncertainty sources separately. Boosted trees ± residual band covers observations roughly but has no parameter-uncertainty layer (function claims undercoverage); bootstrap bands estimate procedure variability (often near-nominal for the function on stable learners) but can’t separate the layers without adding σ̂. Guidance: decide which claim you’re making first — function vs new point — then pick the machinery that natively states it; and remember (c)’s advantage came from knowing the basis, i.e., from model correctness, which no interval machinery can grant.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (BART × 7.5 boosting × 4.5 bootstrap). The uncertainty triathlon on one dataset: point predictions from boosting, bootstrap bands from a forest, and (on the correct linear basis, as BART-stand-in) posterior bands. Compare widths and calibration of the three intervals for both functions and new points over 200 simulations. Write the decision tree a practitioner should follow: “if you need ___ and can assume , use ” — three branches, honestly conditioned.

C2 (BART × 2.5 confounding × 12.x stub). Treatment effects, BART’s home turf: simulate an observational study with a binary treatment, confounders, and heterogeneous effects (effect varies with x₁). Using the conjugate Bayesian machinery on a basis that includes treatment × covariate terms, compute the posterior distribution of the effect at several x₁ values. Report the effect curve with credible bands, and state precisely what design assumption (unconfoundedness) the bands still require — the Bayesian machinery quantifies uncertainty, not identification.

7. Summary

  • BART = boosting’s sum-of-small-trees, fitted by MCMC under a regularizing prior — the Bayesian answer to nonlinear tabular regression.
  • The prize: posterior predictive distributions — function-uncertainty and new-observation uncertainty as separate, honest layers.
  • The conjugate Bayesian model is the runnable core: same updates, same credible intervals, closed-form (drills).
  • Priors replace boosting’s ν; MCMC replaces greedy residual-chasing; tree-depth preferences replace depth caps.
  • Costs: MCMC compute, implementation maturity, and mandatory chain diagnostics.
  • Niche of strength: small-to-mid n, uncertainty-qualified predictions, causal effect estimation — not the 100M-row leaderboard.
