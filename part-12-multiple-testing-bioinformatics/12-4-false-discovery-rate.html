12.4 False Discovery Rate

FDR reframes the guarantee: instead of “no false positives” (FWER, brutal at scale), control the expected proportion of false discoveries among your rejections. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure delivers exactly that — and it is the operating standard of genomics, where discovery lists, not single verdicts, are the product.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Definitions with R = number of rejections, V = false rejections:

\[\text{FDR} = E\left[\frac{V}{\max(R, 1)}\right] \le q\]

Benjamini–Hochberg: sort p-values; find the largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ (k/m)·q; reject the k smallest. Implemented from the definition:

2. Concept Questions

  1. State the exact difference between FWER = P(V ≥ 1) and FDR = E[V/max(R, 1)] — and compute both for a study with R = 100, V = 3.
  2. Why is FDR ≤ q weaker than FWER ≤ q, yet often the better guarantee? Give the discovery-screen argument.
  3. BH’s threshold line (k/m)·q grows with rank — what is the procedure “spending” on the largest p-values, and why doesn’t that blow up the FDR?
  4. Under what two conditions does BH control FDR exactly, and what happens to the guarantee under strong negative dependence?
  5. Your q = 0.05 analysis rejects 60 of 20,000 genes. Translate “q-value of gene 17 is 0.03” into a precise frequentist sentence — and contrast it with a Bonferroni sentence for the same gene.
Answers
  1. FWER: P(V ≥ 1) = 1 here (some false exist). FDR: E[V/max(R,1)] = 3/100 = 0.03 — a 3% expected contamination rate among the list. FWER says “any falsehood counts once”; FDR says “the list’s purity, on average.”
  2. Weaker: FDR ≤ q permits some studies with contaminated lists (compensated by clean ones). Better: at discovery scale, demanding zero false positives sacrifices enormous power; controlling list purity at 5% keeps most true discoveries while capping contamination — the only workable contract for 10⁴-test screens.
  3. The line spends more allowance on larger p-values (the (k/m)q budget grows with rank) — rejections at the tail are the cheapest tests of the “more signal exists” hypothesis (if the k-th smallest p is that small, the alternative is strongly implicated there). BH’s proof shows the budget stays calibrated because the tail rejections are backed by the accumulated evidence of all smaller p’s.
  4. Independent p-values, or positively regression-dependent on the truth (PRDS — roughly: bigger |signal| → stochastically smaller other p’s, true for one-sided normal/χ² settings). Under strong negative dependence BH can exceed q; adapted procedures (BY: q·Σ1/k) restore control at a power price.
  5. “Gene 17’s q-value 0.03: among discovery lists produced at threshold q = 0.03 by this procedure, an expected 3% of rejections are false — gene 17 is in such a list.” Bonferroni sentence: “gene 17’s p ≤ 0.05/20,000, i.e., the family contained no false positive with 95% probability” — a far harder bar, answering a different question.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. FDR calibration across the truth-proportion axis: at q = 0.05, simulate m = 5,000 tests with true-signal fraction ∈ {0%, 5%, 20%, 50%} (strong effects for the signal block); measure realized FDP among rejections and power. Verify: FDP ≤ q in every regime, power rises with signal fraction. Then compute the Bayesian-flavored quantity — among rejections, the posterior-ish probability a rejection is true (= power·π₁/FDR-ish decomposition) — and note where the frequentist guarantee and the intuition align.

Solution
FDP ≤ 0.05 in all four regimes — BH’s guarantee is regime-robust; power climbs from 0 (no signal) to near-total (50% signal). The alignment: with π₁ true alternatives, an FDR-of-0.05 list’s rejections are ~true with probability related to power·π₁/(power·π₁ + q·(stuff)) — the frequentist list-purity and the Bayesian “probability this hit is real” track each other closely when the signal is dense, which is why q-values read so naturally.

Exercise 2. BH vs Bonferroni vs Holm — the power face-off at realistic omics scale: m = 10,000, 100 true effects (two sizes: 50 strong, 50 weak), n = 15/group. Compare all three procedures at family level 0.05: true discoveries, false discoveries, and FDP. Then repeat at n = 40/group. Which procedure’s advantage grows with n, and why does the weak-effect block decide it?

Solution
At n = 15 neither finds the weak block; BH beats Bonferroni modestly. At n = 40 the weak effects become detectable: BH picks up a large share of them (its rank-growing threshold rewards a mass of moderate p-values) while Bonferroni’s flat 5 × 10⁻⁶ wall still blocks every weak effect — the gap explodes. Weak-but-real effects are BH’s comparative advantage; that’s the genomics use case.

Exercise 3. Storey’s π₀ and adaptive BH: estimate π₀ (Storey, λ = 0.5) on a mixture with 80% nulls, then run adaptive BH with effective m′ = π̂₀·m (threshold (k/m′)q) — rejecting more when many nulls are suspected. Compare adaptive vs standard BH by realized FDP and power on (a) 90%-null and (b) 20%-null families. When does adaptivity pay, when is it dangerous (π̂₀ under-estimated → FDR inflated), and what guard (capped π̂₀, bootstrap-smoothed λ) would you code?

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Three summary languages: rejection sets, BH-adjusted p-values, q-values — related, not identical; label which you printed.
  • BH assumes independence/PRDS: verify via histograms and null simulations; use BY (Benjamini–Yekutieli, thresholds q·k/(m·Σ1/j)) under arbitrary dependence at a power cost.
  • Storey’s λ: the π₀ estimator’s tuning point; λ too small over-counts true nulls’ p-values above it… check sensitivity across λ ∈ {0.3, 0.5, 0.7} before believing π̂₀.
  • FDR vs per-gene error: “q = 0.03” is not “3% chance this gene is null” — it’s the expected false proportion in the discovery list at that threshold.
  • Power at fixed q rises with π₁ (the truth fraction): sparse-signal regimes need stricter pre-filtering or larger n to make lists worth reporting.
  • Replication remains king: a q-value is a calibration claim about a procedure; an independent-cohort replication is the biological confirmation.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Flat q threshold: that’s a per-test α (and here it even rejects p = 0.75… no — 0.75 > 0.05, but the set semantics are Bonferroni-lite, not BH). FDR thresholds grow with rank.
  2. Unsorted rank alignment: BH compares the k-th smallest p to (k/m)q — after sorting, with set-monotonicity (reject all up to the last passing rank). The unsorted version matches ranks to the wrong order statistics.
  3. “FDR” from non-rejections: FDR is about the rejection list’s expected contamination — estimated by simulation with known truth, or reported as the q-guarantee; the fraction of large p-values is π₀-territory (Exercise 3’s estimator), not FDR.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove BH controls FDR at q under independent p-values: with m₀ true nulls, show E[V/max(R,1)] ≤ (m₀/m)·q ≤ q — the famous m₀/m factor (Storey’s sharpening). The proof’s engine: for each true null i, P(pᵢ ≤ (k/m)q) = (k/m)q, and the rejection-rank argument conditions on R = k. Verify the m₀/m sharpening numerically: simulate m = 1000, m₀ = 800 (80% nulls) at q = 0.1 and confirm realized FDR ≈ 0.08 < 0.1.

Hint E[V/max(R,1)] = Σₖ E[V·1(R=k)]/k and each true null’s rejection probability at BH’s k-th threshold is exactly (k/m)q; sum the geometric-ish series and factor m₀/m.
Solution

BH rejects 1..k where k = max{i: p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ iq/m}. For a true null i, P(pᵢ ≤ iq/m) = iq/m; summing over the rejection event and dividing by k (the max(R,1) denominators telescope the sum): E[V/max(R,1)] = (m₀/m)·q ≤ q. Simulation:

Realized FDR lands near 0.08 = (m₀/m)·q — the sharpening is real, and Storey’s adaptive q-values exploit exactly this factor. The theorem’s reach explains BH’s dominance: a guarantee that tightens automatically as true signal accumulates.

Q2. Dependent p-values, quantified: simulate m = 500 tests as correlated z-scores via a one-factor model (correlation ρ ∈ {0, 0.4, 0.8}), with 5% true signals. Measure BH’s realized FDR at q = 0.05 across ρ. BH’s theory (PRDS) predicts control holds under positive dependence — verify, then break it deliberately with negative dependence (z₂ = Z − ε structure) and show FDR inflation. What practical dependence diagnostics would you run before trusting BH on a real omics dataset?

Solution
Positive dependence (PRDS): FDR holds at ≈ q — BH is robust to the good kind of correlation. Negative dependence: FDR inflates past q — the guarantee’s actual boundary. Diagnostics before trusting BH: p-value histogram shape (12.2 Exercise 3), pairwise correlation of test statistics, permutation-based null p-values (12.5) which automatically inherit the true dependence structure.

Measure realized FDR and power against the complete-data truth. Which route keeps the FDR guarantee — and what does the answer imply about running BH on silently imputed omics matrices?

7. Summary

  • FDR = E[V/max(R,1)] ≤ q: list-purity control — the operating contract of discovery science at scale.
  • BH: sort p, threshold (k/m)q, reject up to the last crossing; q-values give each hypothesis its own threshold.
  • BH’s guarantee is regime-robust (independence/PRDS), sharpens with signal (m₀/m factor), and is weaker but cheaper than FWER by design.
  • Power over Bonferroni concentrates on weak-but-real effects at scale — the genomics payoff.
  • Purity is an expectation: single studies can be fully contaminated; report null simulations alongside.
  • Permutation p-values feed BH the dependence-honest nulls it deserves: 12.5 Resampling for p-values and FDR.
