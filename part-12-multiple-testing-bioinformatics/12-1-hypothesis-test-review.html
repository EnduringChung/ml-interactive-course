12.1 Hypothesis-Test Review

Before controlling the error rates of many tests (12.2–12.4), the machinery of one test must be exact machinery: null distribution, test statistic, α, p-value, power. This review page rebuilds the single-test engine by simulation — the calibration habits that Part 12 will stress-test at scale.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

One test’s anatomy, in the form Part 12 needs:

  • H₀ — the no-effect model that generates the null distribution of the statistic.
  • Test statistic T — a number measuring departure from H₀ (t, χ², |correlation|…).
  • α — the pre-registered false-positive rate if H₀ is true: P(reject | H₀) = α.
  • p-value — P(statistic at least this extreme | H₀); uniform under H₀.
  • Power — P(reject | H₁): depends on effect size, n, α (1.4).

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why is the p-value’s null-uniformity the load-bearing fact for every multiple-testing procedure in Part 12?
  2. α is chosen before the test. What exactly is being controlled — and what does a p = 0.03 not tell you?
  3. Power depends on four quantities. Name them and their directions, then state which one Part 12’s corrections will deliberately sacrifice.
  4. A one-sided test at α = 0.05 vs a two-sided at α = 0.05: which has more power in its declared direction, and what does the two-sided test protect against?
  5. Why does the t-test’s null distribution depend on n (df = 2n − 2), and what replaces the t-distribution in the permutation-based tests of 12.5?
Answers
  1. Every correction (Bonferroni, Holm, BH — 12.3, 12.4) works by transforming or thresholding p-values; they’re calibrated exactly because raw p-values are Uniform(0,1) under their nulls. Break uniformity (dependent tests, wrong model) and the corrections miscalibrate.
  2. The long-run frequency of false rejections when H₀ is true — a property of the procedure. p = 0.03 does not give P(H₀ true), effect size, or replication probability (1.4’s misreading list).
  3. Effect size (↑), n (↑), α (↑), noise σ (↓). Part 12’s corrections effectively raise the per-comparison bar — sacrificing per-test power to control family-level error.
  4. One-sided: more power in the declared direction (all α in one tail). Two-sided: protection against surprises in either direction — and it’s what “p < 0.05” conventionally means. One-sided must be pre-registered, never chosen after seeing the data.
  5. The t-statistic’s exact null is t-distributed only when scaling by the sample SD (estimated nuisance parameter; df = degrees of freedom consumed). Permutation tests replace the distributional reference with the empirical null from label shuffling — no distributional form at all.

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Build the single-test simulator simulate_test(n, effect, alpha, reps) returning (empirical alpha, empirical power) for a two-sample t-test. Run a grid: n ∈ {20, 50, 100} × effect ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8}. Verify three laws: alpha stays ≈ 0.05 regardless of effect; power rises with n; power rises with effect. Then state which cell corresponds to “the standard underpowered study” (power ≈ 0.2–0.3).

Solution
α ≈ 0.05 in every row (calibration is effect-independent); power climbs along both n and effect. The underpowered classics: n = 20–50 at effect 0.3 — power ≈ 0.2–0.4, exactly the regime where Part 12’s corrections hurt most and where “p = 0.04” findings fail to replicate.

Exercise 2. The p-value misconception clinic: for the scenario “observed p = 0.03, n = 60”, simulate 20,000 replications of the same experiment and report: (a) the fraction of replications with p < 0.05 (the replication probability at α), (b) the median p of replications, (c) the fraction significant at α = 0.01. Compare each against what “p = 0.03” tempts people to believe. Write the one-sentence corrective for each of the three classic misreadings.

Solution
Correctives: (1) “p = 0.03” does not mean 97% replication chance — replication-at-α here is ~50–60%; (2) the median replication p is ≈ 0.03’s neighborhood, not 0.001 (no compounding of evidence by rerunning); (3) stricter thresholds slash replication probability — tighter α isn’t free. Each sentence pre-empts a real reviewer argument.

Exercise 3. Nonparametric vs parametric, calibration and power: on (a) normal data and (b) heavy-tailed t₂ data, compare the t-test and Mann–Whitney U (both two-sided, n = 25/group): empirical α under null, power under a shift. Report the 2×2×2 summary. Which test is calibrated in both worlds? Which has more power where its assumptions hold — and which would you pre-register for skewed biomarker data?

Solution
Both are calibrated on normal data; on heavy tails the t-test’s α inflates (CLT needs larger n) while Mann–Whitney stays calibrated (rank-based). Power: t wins on normal; U wins or ties on heavy tails. Pre-registration answer: skewed/heavy-tailed biomarker data → rank-based tests as the default, with the t-test as the normal-theory cross-check — and note that Part 12’s corrections apply to whichever p-values you feed them.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Test choice is a loss/distribution choice: t (means, normal-ish), Mann–Whitney (stochastic dominance, any shape), chi-square (proportions), log-rank (11.3) — the Part 12 corrections don’t care which, only that the p-values are valid.
  • One-sided requires pre-registration; two-sided is the reproducible default. Switching sides post hoc halves p-values by fiat — p-hacking’s oldest trick.
  • Paired vs unpaired is a design fact — pairing removes subject-level variance but only when the pairing is real (1.4 drill 4’s logic).
  • p = 0.049 vs 0.051 — no evidential boundary; α = 0.05 is a convention for procedure calibration, and Part 12 will move the threshold per-family anyway.
  • Test many → the α guarantee is per test only: this entire page’s calibration says nothing about a family of tests — the gap Part 12 exists to close.
  • Power analysis before data (prospective) vs after (retrospective power on nonsignificant results — near-meaningless): pre-register the former, skip the latter.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the t-test’s calibration by simulation and by theory: show that under H₀ with pooled variance s_p², T = (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/(s_p√(2/n)) follows t_{2n−2} (Gaussian data), and verify that the empirical rejection rate at the t-quantile matches α to Monte Carlo error for n ∈ {10, 30}. Then break it: replace Gaussian with t₂ data and measure α inflation at n = 10 and n = 100 — at which n does CLT rescue the t-test, and what does that mean for small-n biomarker studies?

Hint Under H₀ with equal variance, T = √n(x̄₁ − x̄₂)/s_p is exactly t_{2n−2} for Gaussian data; heavy tails break the variance-stability the denominator needs.
Solution
Gaussian: α matches 0.05 at both n. t₂ at n = 10: α inflates well past nominal (heavy tails break s_p’s stability); by n = 100, CLT mostly rescues it. Small-n biomarker studies with skewed/heavy-tailed readouts: the t-test’s 5% is a fiction — rank tests, transformations, or larger n (1.4 Q1’s direction, empirically grounded here).

Q2. Pre-registration as a selection firewall: simulate a lab testing 10 pre-registered hypotheses (6 with true effects of varying sizes) at α = 0.05, and estimate: family-wise false positives, expected false “discoveries,” and the false-discovery proportion among significant results. Then rerun with the lab also testing 40 post-hoc subgroups. Quantify what the post-hoc expansion does to the false-discovery proportion — and write the lab-policy sentence that would have prevented it (this is 12.2’s opening experiment, run with your own hands).

Solution
Adding 40 null searches inflates the false-significant count from ≈ 0.2 (6 pre-registered nulls × 0.05) to ≈ 2 per study — a ten-fold lie inflation from “we also looked at subgroups.” Policy sentence: “All hypotheses and analysis subgroups were pre-registered; post-hoc findings are labeled exploratory and require independent replication” — the sentence Part 12 exists to enforce.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (review × 0.5 splitting × 3.7 comparison). Model comparison as hypothesis testing: two models’ CV scores, tested by paired fold-wise t-test (3.7) — build the full simulation with known-equal models (same algorithm, different seeds) and measure the false-positive rate of “model A is better” across 500 dataset draws, at 5 folds vs 20 folds. Connect the inflated rate to fold-correlation (4.3 Q1) and state the comparison protocol your papers will use.

C2 (review × 11.x survival × 12.5 preview). The log-rank test’s p-values under non-normal censoring: simulate survival data with heavy-tailed censoring and verify the log-rank’s α calibration (it should hold — risk-set arithmetic, not parametric). Contrast with a naive t-test on censored times (it won’t). This foreshadows 12.5: permutation/rank machinery stays calibrated where parametric tests bend.

7. Summary

  • One test: H₀ → statistic → null distribution → α → p-value; calibration (uniform p under H₀) is checkable and load-bearing.
  • Power = effect × n × α × noise; pre-register α, direction, and design; retrospective power is noise.
  • p-values are procedure properties, not effect statements; replication probability ≪ naive hope.
  • Heavy tails break small-n parametric α; rank tests hold the line.
  • Per-test α is the only guarantee — families of tests are Part 12’s problem, starting now: 12.2 Why multiple testing is dangerous.
