12.2 Why Multiple Testing Is Dangerous

Every test is a small false-positive machine (rate α). Run many and the machines compound: 100 perfect-null tests at α = 0.05 will produce ≈ 5 “discoveries” — guaranteed, with no bug, no misconduct. This page makes the danger visceral with the simulations that should be run before every large-scale analysis.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

For m independent tests of true nulls at per-test level α:

\[P(\text{at least one false positive}) = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^m\]

— which hits 0.92 at m = 50 and ≈ 1 at m = 500. And the expected number of false positives is m·α — linear, unstoppable without correction:

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why doesn’t “each test is valid at 5%” defend a paper reporting 3 of 200 findings as significant?
  2. The “look-elsewhere effect”: you test one biomarker, p = 0.06, then try 7 subgroups until p < 0.05. What’s the actual family size, and what does that make your p = 0.049?
  3. Why do p-value histograms diagnose the danger (uniform blob vs left-spiked)? Sketch both and what each implies.
  4. A big-data analysis reports 500 significant findings. Give two scenarios — one benign, one damning — consistent with that number.
  5. Why is the danger worst exactly where multiple testing is most tempting (high-dimensional biology)?
Answers
  1. “Each test valid at 5%” is a per-test guarantee. The paper’s claim rests on the selected findings — selection among 200 tests at α = 0.05 has family-wise error near 1 − 0.95²⁰⁰ ≈ 1.0: essentially all “significant” trios are noise-shaped. Selection voids per-test calibration (4.1’s selection bonus, p-value edition).
  2. The family is all tests considered: 1 + 7 = 8 (at least — the subgroups tried-and-abandoned count too). The “p = 0.049” is the minimum of 8 draws, not a 4.9% event — its honest multiplicity-adjusted p is several times larger.
  3. Under H₀, p-values are uniform (12.1); under H₁ they pile up near 0. Histogram: flat = nothing but noise; left spike + flat remainder = real signal riding on noise (the spike’s mass = discoveries, the flat part = the multiplicity battlefield). No left spike + “significant findings” = findings are noise.
  4. Benign: large true effects, strong power — 500 of 20,000 tests genuinely differential (with left-spiked histogram, effect-size consistency). Damning: pure noise with “significance” manufactured by testing 20,000 × many subtypes/corrections-until-significant — the count alone is uninformative without the null comparison (Section 3’s exercises).
  5. Temptation scales with tests available (20,000 genes × subgroups × timepoints) and with per-test noise (small n, high variance) — both push m·α up while weakening each test. The frontier of biology is the frontier of this problem.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. The candidate-gene era vs the genome era: simulate screening 20 candidate genes (5 truly differential, effect 1.0) vs 20,000 genome-wide genes (same 5 real). At α = 0.05 per test, report for both: expected true discoveries, expected false discoveries, and the false discovery proportion (FDP). Show that the same per-test α is a reasonable policy in one world and a catastrophe in the other — the quantitative case for FDR (12.4).

Solution
Candidates: 5 tests, ~0.75 false hits among ~3 true — FDP tolerable. Genome-wide: 20,000 tests manufacture ~1,000 false hits to accompany 3–4 true — FDP ≈ 99.7%. Same α, opposite conclusions: the proportion of tests that are true governs whether uncorrected testing is defensible, which is precisely the quantity FDR methods control.

Exercise 2. The subgroup expedition: one treatment trial (n = 400, tiny true effect 0.15 — nearly null), tested overall plus in 16 post-hoc subgroups (defined after the fact, some overlapping). Simulate 2,000 such trials; report: P(overall test significant), P(at least one subgroup significant), P(at least one significant and overall not). Then compute the probability that the pattern looks like “overall null but a subgroup benefits” — the exact shape that launches a junk hypothesis.

Solution
Overall: ≈ 12–15% (the 0.15 effect barely misses). Any-subgroup: ≈ 60–80% — sixteen chances at α = 0.05 with correlated tests. “Subgroup hit with null overall” — the most publishable-looking accident — occurs in a third or more of truly-null-ish trials. The fix is structural: pre-registered subgroups, hierarchical testing (overall → subgroups only if overall passes, 12.3), and replication.

Exercise 3. Build the “null calibration report”: given any vector of p-values from an analysis, produce (a) the p-value histogram with expected-uniform line, (b) an estimate of π₀ (the proportion of true nulls) via the simple Storey method (π̂₀ = #{p > λ}/((1−λ)m) for λ = 0.5, smoothed), (c) the observed-vs-expected count of p < 0.05. Run it on three synthetic scenarios — all-null, 5% signal, 40% signal — and write the one-line readout your report would show for each.

Solution
Readouts: all-null → π̂₀ ≈ 1, P(p < .05) ≈ 0.05 (pure multiplicity noise); 5% signal → π̂₀ < 1 with a modest left spike; 40% signal → π̂₀ drops far below 1 with a heavy spike. The report converts “we found hits” into “here is the evidence calibration of the whole family” — the report a reviewer can actually judge, and the input that Storey-style q-values (12.4) formalize.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • The garden of forking paths (Gelman): multiplicity includes analysis choices never written down — exclusions, transforms, outlier rules, model forms. The tested-family count is usually understated because the unrecorded attempts aren’t counted (4.1’s hidden selection).
  • Correlated tests soften the explosion but don’t remove it: 1−(1−α)^m is the independent bound; strongly correlated tests behave like fewer effective tests — better than independence, still compounding.
  • “We replicated internally” on the same dataset (split by site/batch and re-test): only a true independent replication counts; internal splits share the selection history.
  • Exploratory vs confirmatory is a design label, not a vibe: exploratory analyses are legitimate — as hypothesis generators requiring pre-registered replication, never as confirmatory findings (3.7’s selection-vs-reporting split).
  • Big n doesn’t fix multiplicity: larger n powers more tiny true effects (good) but the null tests still produce m·α false positives (bad); the FDP depends on the true-signal proportion, not on n.
  • The correction is cheap; the discipline is the hard part: Bonferroni is one line of code — the institutional practice of pre-specification is what Part 12 actually teaches.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove the expected number of false positives is exactly mα under independent nulls and under arbitrary dependence (linearity of expectation needs no independence) — while the P(any) bound requires independence (or Bonferroni: P(any) ≤ mα, Markov). Verify both claims by simulation under (a) independent and (b) perfectly correlated p-values. What does this asymmetry (expectation robust, tail bound fragile) predict about FDR vs FWER control under dependence?

Hint E[Σ1ᵢ] = ΣE[1ᵢ] = mα always; P(∪Aᵢ) ≤ ΣP(Aᵢ) is Markov, tight only under disjointness/independence.
Solution

E[count] = ΣᵢP(pᵢ ≤ α) = mα by linearity — holds under any dependence structure (this is why FDR, which targets the expectation, generalizes gracefully). The tail P(any) needs the union bound: ≤ mα (Bonferroni), equality-ish only under (near-)independence; perfectly correlated p-values act as ONE test — P(any) = α, far below mα. Simulation:

Prediction: FDR (expectation-based, BH) is robust to common dependence forms (BH holds under PRDS); FWER procedures calibrated under independence can over- or under-cover under exotic dependence — the reason resampling-based FWER control exists (12.5).

Q2. The inverted-detection trap: with 20,000 tests at FDR q = 0.05 and zero true effects, E[false discoveries] = 0.05·20,000·(something)? No — BH’s guarantee is FDR ≤ q, i.e., E[V/R] ≤ q. Design the simulation showing BH’s behavior on pure nulls (how many rejections, what realized FDP) and explain why “BH rejected 47 genes so ~47 are false” is wrong, while “expected FDP among rejections ≤ 5%” is right — including what FDP means when R = 0.

Solution
BH on pure nulls rejects a handful of genes in some studies (realized FDP = 1 in those studies — every rejection false!) while the expected proportion stays ≤ q across studies. The guarantee is about the long-run contamination rate of your discovery lists, not about any single list’s content — and R = 0 studies (FDP defined as 0) keep the average down. This subtlety is exactly why FDR is “the expected false discovery proportion,” and why single-study FDP claims need the simulation-based augmentation of 12.5.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (danger × 9.x × 4.6). The unsupervised multiplicity census, measured: simulate data with no clusters; run the full clustering circus (K-means k = 2..8, hierarchical with 4 linkages, two scalings — 56 analyses); declare “structure found” by your favorite internal index. Estimate the false-discovery rate of “we found clusters” across 500 null datasets. Then estimate it again with the pre-registered discipline of 9.6 (fixed k, fixed algorithm, stability gate). Quantify the discipline’s FDR reduction — the experiment that justifies pre-registration in unsupervised biology.

C2 (danger × 7.4 forest importances × 12.4). Importance lists are multiple testing too: on null data (no signal), fit a random forest 100 times (resamples, seeds) and record which features enter the top-5 importances at least once. What fraction of features earn “top-5 at least once” status across 40 pure-noise features? Then run the same with a real signal. Design the permutation-test-based importance gate that separates the two worlds — implementing the null first, as 12.5 teaches.

7. Summary

  • m tests at α ⇒ ≈ mα false positives (always) and P(any) → 1 (fast): multiplicity is arithmetic, not misconduct.
  • Selection (min-p, subgroup hunting, best-of-many) converts calibration into optimism — before any model is fit.
  • p-value histograms and π₀ estimates diagnose how much of your family is noise vs signal.
  • Per-test validity survives; family-level claims die without correction — the corrections are Part 12’s next three pages.
  • Exploratory ≠ confirmatory: the label must match the design, and replication must be independent.
