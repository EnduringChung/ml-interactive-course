12.3 Family-Wise Error Rate

The family-wise error rate (FWER) is P(at least one false positive in the whole family) — the strictest multiplicity guarantee: no false finding, with probability 1 − α, no matter how many tests fail. Bonferroni, Šidák, and Holm deliver it; this page implements all three, measures their power price, and shows when the price is worth paying.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

For m hypotheses and family level α:

  • Bonferroni: reject pᵢ ≤ α/m — P(any false) ≤ m·(α/m) = α by Markov/union bound. Simple, valid under any dependence.
  • Šidák: reject pᵢ ≤ 1 − (1 − α)^{1/m} — exact under independence (slightly less conservative).
  • Holm step-down: order p₍₁₎ ≤ ⋯ ≤ p₍ₘ₎; reject while p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ α/(m − i + 1); stop at the first failure. Uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni, still FWER-controlling under any dependence.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Bonferroni’s guarantee comes from an inequality, not an exact calculation — when is it conservative (FWER < α), and give the perfectly-correlated extreme.
  2. Why is Holm uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni, and what does “uniformly” mean here exactly?
  3. Šidák requires independence (or positive dependence of a specific kind) — what breaks under arbitrary dependence that Bonferroni survives?
  4. FWER control at α = 0.05 with 20,000 tests: what per-test threshold results, and what kind of effect can still pass it?
  5. When is FWER the right guarantee at all — name two decision contexts where any false finding is unacceptable, versus a discovery context where FDR (12.4) is the better target.
Answers
  1. Markov’s bound is tight only when p-values are near-α simultaneously (disjoint events); under perfect correlation (all p equal) the family behaves as one test — FWER = α exactly with Bonferroni rejecting at α/m… actually with all p identical, either all pass or none: FWER is 0 or α — maximally conservative on average. Conservative = real α below nominal, power thrown away.
  2. Holm’s later thresholds are larger (α/(m−i+1) grows as small p’s consume rejections) whenever any hypothesis is rejected, and equal otherwise — so every Bonferroni rejection is also a Holm rejection, plus possibly more: “uniformly” = for every p-vector and every α.
  3. Šidák’s exactness computes P(no false) = (1 − α/m)^m ≈ 1 − α — true only if the per-test errors are independent (or positively dependent in the Slepian sense). Under arbitrary negative dependence the product formula fails and Šidák can exceed α; Bonferroni’s inequality is dependence-free.
  4. α/m = 0.05/20,000 = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ — only enormous effects with tiny p-values (p ≈ 10⁻⁷–10⁻⁸, genome-wide-association territory) can clear it. Everything weaker is sacrificed: that’s the FWER bargain.
  5. FWER fits: confirmatory clinical trials (any false claim harms patients), regulatory decisions, safety signals — “zero false findings” mandates. FDR fits: exploratory discovery screens (genomics, proteomics) where following up a few false leads is affordable and missing true leads is the costlier error.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Implement fwer_sim(m, n_true, effect, procedure, reps) measuring empirical FWER and power for the three procedures + uncorrected, under independent tests. Run m = 100, n_true = 5, effect sized to give raw power 0.3, α = 0.05. Report the 4×2 table and verify the two theoretical facts: all three corrections hold FWER ≤ 0.05, and Holm ≥ Bonferroni in power everywhere.

Solution
All corrections hold FWER ≤ 0.05 (uncorrected ≈ 0.99+); power ordering uncorrected > Holm ≥ Šidák ≥ Bonferroni, with Holm–Bonferroni gaps visible but modest. The table is the whole Part 12 trade in four rows: guarantees cost power, and better algorithms return some of it.

Exercise 2. Dependence stress-test: generate p-value vectors with controlled dependence via a common latent factor (pᵢ = 2Φ(−|√w·Z + √(1−w)·εᵢ|), w ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.95}). For each w, measure Bonferroni’s and Šidák’s empirical FWER under the global null. Verify: Bonferroni flat at ≤ 0.05 for all w; Šidák inflation grows with w. Then state the practical rule for which procedure is safe under unknown dependence (foreshadowing resampling-based FWER in 12.5).

Solution
Bonferroni: flat ≤ 0.05 at every w (union bound is dependence-blind). Šidák: nominal at w = 0, inflating toward (and past) 0.05 as the common factor concentrates the p-values. Rule: under unknown or negative dependence, Bonferroni/Holm are the safe FWER tools; Šidák’s extra power requires justified near-independence; fully general dependence calls for permutation-based FWER (12.5’s maxT).

Exercise 3. Hierarchical testing (the clinical-trials pattern): a family has one primary hypothesis and 9 secondaries. Design and implement the gatekeeper procedure: test primary at α = 0.05; only if significant, test the 9 secondaries with Bonferroni at α = 0.05/9. Simulate (a) all-null and (b) primary-true + 2 secondary-true scenarios; measure FWER of any false claim and power for the true secondaries. Compare against flat Bonferroni over all 10. When does the hierarchy win, and what does it encode about scientific priority?

Solution
The gatekeeper concentrates power: passing the primary releases α = 0.05/9 on secondaries (vs 0.05/10 flat) — and structurally forbids secondary claims when the primary fails, which flat Bonferroni permits. It wins whenever scientific priority is real (a failed primary makes secondary claims uninterpretable); its cost is the primary’s gate — a false negative up top silences everything below. The procedure encodes the trial’s epistemology.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Bonferroni on correlated tests is conservative, not invalid — FWER ≤ α regardless; the cost is power, and Šidák/Holm claw some back. The dependence failure mode belongs to Šidák and to naive “exact” resampling.
  • Holm ≠ “Bonferroni with smaller thresholds” only: its step-down stops at the first non-rejection — small p’s unlock larger thresholds for the rest, but a single large p halts the cascade (later hypotheses can’t be rejected even if tiny… they’d already have been tested earlier — order matters).
  • Weighted FWER: unequal α_i/mᵢ allocations encode prior importance (primary vs exploratory) while keeping Σ weights = α — the formal version of Exercise 3’s gatekeeper.
  • FWER at q = 0.05 with m = 10⁶: per-test α = 5 × 10⁻⁸ (GWAS standard) — only monumental effects survive; if your science needs lists not certainties, FWER is the wrong target (12.4).
  • FWER ≠ per-family “one error ever” across studies: the guarantee is within the declared family/analysis; a lab running 50 analyses has 50 families — the reporting and pre-registration discipline of 12.2 governs the meta-family.
  • Selective reporting of FWER-significant subfamilies re-inflates FWER: splitting the family post hoc to make Bonferroni look powerful is the same selection game in correction clothing.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove Holm controls FWER under arbitrary dependence: suppose H₍₁₎…H₍ₖ₎ are the true nulls with p-values p₍₁₎ ≤ ⋯ ≤ p₍ₖ₎ (among the true ones). Show that if any true null is rejected, then Holm must have rejected all smaller-p hypotheses first, and each step’s threshold α/(m−i+1) < α/k for i < k… conclude that rejecting a true null requires p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ α/(m − k + 1) for some i ≤ k, hence P(any true null rejected) ≤ k·α/(m−k+1) ≤ α. Where does the argument fail for Šidák’s product form?

Hint The step-down stops at the first failure; for a true null to be rejected, every earlier (smaller-p) test must have been rejected, each under its own stricter threshold — chain the inequalities.
Solution Order all m p-values; suppose some true null (among k true) is rejected at step i with threshold α/(m − i + 1). Since all k true nulls have p ≤ that rejected one’s p… specifically the k-th smallest true-null p satisfies p₍ₖ₎ ≤ p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ α/(m − i + 1) with i ≤ m − k + 1 (at most m − k false nulls precede it), giving p₍ₖ₎ ≤ α/(m − k + 1) ≤ α/k. Under H₀ (joint), P(min true p ≤ α/k) ≤ k·α/k = α by union bound over the k true nulls — FWER ≤ α under any dependence. Šidák’s (1−α/m)^m-form argument needs the per-test errors to multiply — i.e., independence — so arbitrary dependence breaks it while Holm’s union-bound chain survives. That’s precisely Holm’s theoretical advantage over Šidák (and the dependence-safety Bonferroni advertises).

Q2. Power-optimal FWER is a design question, not just a procedure question: with m = 100, α = 0.05, and a fixed experiment budget, compare three designs by power on the true effects: (a) split α evenly (plain Holm), (b) weighted Holm (α/2 on 1 primary, α/2 spread over 99 secondaries), (c) gatekeeper (primary at 0.05, secondaries at 0.05/99 only if primary passes). Simulate with a known primary effect and heterogeneous secondary effects; report each design’s power on primary and secondaries. Write the design principle: how should scientific priority structure the correction?

Solution
Weighted and gatekeeper designs concentrate α where prior knowledge expects effects: primary power rises several points; secondary power on true modest effects holds or improves vs even splitting (fewer hypotheses competing for the secondary α budget). Principle: the correction should encode the hypothesis hierarchy — α flows toward pre-registered priorities under a family-wise guarantee, and the flow chart is the study design (12.2’s discipline, made formal).

6. Complex Questions

C1 (FWER × 7.4 forest importances × 9.6). FWER for importance lists: on null data, compute permutation importances for 40 features 200 times; build each feature’s null importance distribution; then apply Holm across the 40 features to a new forest’s importances (z-scored against the null). Report how many features pass FWER-corrected significance on pure noise (should be ~0) and with a planted signal (should be the planted ones). This gives tree-based feature selection a multiplicity guarantee — implement and defend the family definition.

C2 (FWER × 11.3 subgroup log-ranks × 9.x clustering subgroups). The subgroup galaxy: one survival trial, 24 subgroup log-rank tests (biomarker × site × age bands), all null; measure FWER of uncorrected subgroup testing, Bonferroni, and Holm. Then design the “overall-first, subgroup-second, FWER-within” cascade and verify its FWER and its power under a true subgroup effect. Write the clinical-trials-style reporting paragraph for the cascade — with the pre-specification language that makes it credible.

7. Summary

  • FWER = P(any false finding in the family); Bonferroni/Šidák/Holm deliver it — Bonferroni/Holm under any dependence.
  • Holm ≥ Bonferroni in power always (growing thresholds, step-down stop); Šidák adds power only under justified near-independence.
  • The price: per-test thresholds like 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ at m = 20,000 — FWER suits confirmatory contexts where any false claim is unacceptable.
  • Hierarchical/weighted/gatekeeper procedures encode scientific priority while preserving the guarantee.
  • Dependence stress-tests belong in the workflow: Bonferroni flat, Šidák inflation — resampling FWER (12.5) for the general case.
  • Discovery screens need lists, not certainty: the FDR re-framing — 12.4 False discovery rate.
