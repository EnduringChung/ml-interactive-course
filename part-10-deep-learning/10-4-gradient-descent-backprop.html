10.4 Gradient Descent and Backpropagation

Training a network = descending a loss surface by repeated chain rule. This page builds the whole machinery by hand in NumPy — gradient descent, then backpropagation as dynamic programming on the computation graph — and gradient-checks it. Everything runs live; the companion Colab re-runs the same network in PyTorch so you can see autograd reproduce your manual derivatives line for line.

Tier 1 (NumPy manual version) — every cell below runs live. The PyTorch/autograd companion is Tier 3:

1. Basic Concept

Gradient descent: to minimize a differentiable loss \(L(\theta)\), iterate

\[\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \, \nabla L(\theta_t)\]

  • \(\eta\) (learning rate) trades stability against speed: too small crawls, too large overshoots and diverges.
  • Stochastic GD approximates \(\nabla L\) from a mini-batch — cheap steps with noisy gradients; the noise itself regularizes (10.5).
  • Backpropagation computes \(\nabla L\) efficiently: the chain rule applied in reverse topological order over the computation graph, reusing each node’s stored forward value once. It is dynamic programming: each node’s gradient is computed exactly once from its children’s gradients.

Worked example — GD on a 1-D quadratic, where every step can be checked in closed form (\(x_{t+1} = (1-2\eta)x_t\)):

For a network, \(L\) has millions of coordinates and \(\nabla L\) must be assembled by the chain rule across layers — that assembly is backprop, built next.

2. Concept Questions

  1. In the quadratic demo, why does the closed form \(x_t = (1-2\eta)^t x_0\) predict divergence exactly at \(\eta > 1\)? What is the analogous closed-form condition for general quadratic losses in terms of the Hessian’s eigenvalues?
  2. Backprop visits each edge once. What two quantities must each node store from the forward pass, and what single operation combines them with downstream gradients?
  3. Mini-batch SGD’s gradient is unbiased but noisy. Name one good and one bad consequence of the noise.
  4. Why is backprop (reverse mode) the right choice for training — what would forward mode’s cost be for a net with \(P\) parameters? (If unsure, 10.1 Q1.)
  5. Momentum adds \(v_{t+1} = \beta v_t + \nabla L\), \(\theta \mathrel{-}= \eta v_{t+1}\). What does \(\beta = 0.9\) average over, and which two pathologies does this smooth away?
Answers
  1. \(|1-2\eta| < 1\) ⇔ \(0 < \eta < 1\); the update is a geometric shrink factor. General diagonalizable quadratic: convergence iff \(\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}\) (per-mode shrink \(|1-\eta\lambda_i|\)) — hence the practical rule “η near \(1/\lambda_{\max}\), i.e., near the reciprocal of the largest curvature”.
  2. Its forward output (activation) and its local Jacobian (∂output/∂input). Combine: multiply the incoming upstream gradient by the local Jacobian, then route pieces to parents (vector–Jacobian products).
  3. Good: escapes saddle regions/sharp minima, acts as implicit regularization, lets you afford n-sized epochs. Bad: gradient estimates jitter — needs lr decay or averaging for fine convergence; noisy estimates of gradient norms complicate clipping (10.7).
  4. Reverse mode: all \(P\) parameter gradients in ~2 forward-pass costs. Forward mode: \(P\) passes (one tangent each) — impossible at \(P\) = millions.
  5. An exponentially-weighted average of recent gradients (effective window ≈ \(1/(1-\beta) = 10\) steps). Smooths oscillation across ravines (high-curvature direction keeps cancelling) and accelerates consistent directions; doesn’t fix per-step noise entirely, but decorrelates the steps’ zig-zag.

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1 — the centerpiece. Implement a 1-16-1 MLP with tanh hidden activation and MSE loss with manual backpropagation; fit \(\sin\) on \([0, 2\pi)\) from 64 noisy points; plot the loss curve and the fit. Structure the code as forward (storing activations), backward (chain rule, layer by layer), update. Verify gradients by finite differences once, then trust the loop.

Solution
Every line of backward is 10.1 Exercise 1’s chain rule in matrix clothes: dz2 (residual) → dW2/db2 (outer products) → dh (transpose matmul) → dz1 (times local slope 1-h²) → dW1/db1. The gradient check is the habit that catches sign errors, missing 1-h², and transposed matmuls before they cost an afternoon.

Exercise 2. Gradient descent vs momentum vs “too-large lr” on the Exercise-1 problem: rerun the fit with (a) plain GD, lr = 0.1; (b) momentum β = 0.9, lr = 0.02; (c) plain GD, lr = 1.0. Plot the three loss curves on one log-scale axis. Which converges furthest in 2000 steps, and what does (c)’s curve teach about diagnosing divergence?

Solution
Momentum wins on this ravine-shaped loss (the oscillating tanh curvatures average out); lr = 1.0 diverges — the signature is a loss curve that steps upward or NaNs within a few steps, not a plateau. Diagnosis order: (1) diverges? cut lr by 10; (2) plateaus high? raise lr or add momentum; (3) noisy but descending? it’s just SGD.

Exercise 3. Batch-size anatomy: using the Exercise-1 network, train with batch sizes {8, 16, 32, 64} (lr 0.05, 3000 epochs-worth of updates normalized so each setting sees the data equally). Compare final loss and compute cost. Then state the linear-scaling heuristic and when it breaks.

Solution
Smaller batches descend further here (more updates, noise helps escape tanh plateaus) at the same sample-passes; the classic linear-scaling rule (“scale lr ∝ batch size”) holds near small batches for large-scale vision workloads but breaks when lr approaches the stability edge (\(2/\lambda_{\max}\) of the batch Hessian) or with normalization layers whose statistics depend on batch size. Rule of thumb: tune lr per batch size, or keep batch small enough that noise remains useful.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Sum vs mean reduction changes the effective learning rate by the batch size: gradients from a summed loss are n× larger. Mixing reductions between train/eval or across batches silently rescales your lr.
  • Accumulation across steps (torch’s default): forgetting to zero gradients between steps adds stale gradients — equivalent to an unintended momentum of 1.0 with garbage memory (10.1).
  • Vanishing/exploding products: backprop through L layers multiplies L Jacobians; with tanh slopes < 1 the product collapses exponentially (10.7 lives in this regime), with large init weights it explodes — gradient clipping (rescale when the norm exceeds a threshold) is the practical tourniquet.
  • Learning-rate schedules are optimization, not decoration: step decay, cosine, warmup — all acknowledge that the useful η shrinks as you approach the minimum (the quadratic analysis: optimal fixed η is only optimal at the minimum’s curvature).
  • Numerical gradient checks: use central differences (O(ε²) error), ε ≈ 1e-6 for float64 (1e-2 relative for float32), compare relative error |a−n| / max(|a|+|n|, tiny) < 1e-7 — and disable any stochastic parts (dropout) first.
  • GD ≠ Newton: first-order methods ignore curvature; on badly scaled problems (features with wildly different scales) GD’s isotropic step is the bottleneck — standardization is cheap second-order medicine.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove backprop’s cost claim on the matrix-chain problem: computing \(\nabla_{W_1,\dots,W_L} \|(\cdots((XW_1)W_2)\cdots W_L)\|^2\) — show that right-to-left (reverse mode) costs one extra pass equivalent to the forward pass, while left-to-right (forward mode) costs one pass per parameter entry. Where in your Exercise-1 code is the “reverse” visible as an index pattern?

Hint Reverse mode carries one matrix (the adjoint) backward; forward mode carries one tangent direction per parameter entry forward. Count matrix multiplications of both plans.
Solution Forward pass: L matrix products, storing each \(A_\ell\). Reverse: \(\delta_L = 2A_L\), then \(\delta_{\ell-1} = \delta_\ell W_\ell^\top\) (one product per layer), gradients \(A_{\ell-1}^\top \delta_\ell\) (one product per layer) — total ≈ 2L products, i.e., ~2× forward. Forward mode with tangent direction \(E_{ij}\) for parameter entry \((i,j)\): one full L-layer pass per entry → \(P\) passes (\(P\) = total entries). In Exercise 1, reverse mode is visible as the pattern gradients use stored activations from the same step (dW2 = h.T @ dz2 reuses h; dW1 = X.T @ dz1 reuses X) and the adjoint dz flows input-ward (dz2 → dz1), never recomputing a forward quantity. That reuse — each edge’s contribution computed once — is the dynamic-programming content of backprop.

Q2. Learning-rate theory, made empirical: for the Exercise-1 network at initialization, estimate the largest Hessian eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\max}\) of the loss by power iteration on finite-difference Hessian–vector products, and check that GD diverges precisely when \(\eta > 2/\lambda_{\max}\). Then explain why practitioners still tune η by grid/search rather than computing \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) — and why the optimal η changes during training.

Hint Hessian–vector products need only gradients: \(v^\top H v \approx \frac{\|\nabla L(\theta + \epsilon v)\| \text{ directional}}{\epsilon}\) via central differences of the directional derivative. Power-iterate a random vector.
Solution
Power iteration on Hessian–vector products (gradient differences — no n² Hessian ever formed) recovers \(\lambda_{\max}\); GD with η just over \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) explodes, just below it converges slowly — matching the quadratic theory. Practitioners still search η because (i) the Hessian changes as the network trains (loss surfaces are only locally quadratic; \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) at init doesn’t bound later curvature), (ii) mini-batch noise regularizes in ways the exact Hessian ignores, and (iii) schedules (decay/warmup) deliberately traverse regimes. The theory gives the scale of η; search finds the constant.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (× 4.6 hyperparameter tuning × 5.2 ridge). The learning rate is a hyperparameter like any other — but unlike ridge’s λ it interacts with time. Design and run an honest tuning protocol for (η, β) on the Exercise-1 problem: a coarse random search over 20 (η, β) pairs on a validation split, then report the test loss of the winner. How different is the winner from the best test pair (the one you must never select)? Quantify the tuning optimism, and connect to 4.3’s discussion.

C2 (× 9.2 PCA). Gradient descent on whitened inputs: whiten the Exercise-1 X (PCA-whitening, 9.2) and retrain at the same η. Measure speed-to-loss-threshold vs raw inputs, and explain via the Hessian’s condition number — then state why modern architectures instead normalize internally (batch/layer norm, 10.5 and 10.9) rather than preprocessing once.

7. Summary

  • GD iterates \(\theta \mathrel{-}= \eta\nabla L\); stability requires \(\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}\) — divergence within a few steps is the diagnostic.
  • Backprop = reverse-mode chain rule over the recorded graph: each node multiplies the upstream gradient by its local Jacobian; each edge computed once (dynamic programming).
  • Momentum averages recent gradients: tames ravines, accelerates consistent directions.
  • Mini-batching trades gradient quality for update frequency; the noise is regularization, not just cost.
  • Gradient-check every hand-written backward pass once — central differences, relative error < 1e-7.
  • Sum-vs-mean reductions, stale gradients, and lr schedules are where training loops actually break.
  • A net that trains is not a net that generalizes — the machinery for not overfitting is next: 10.5 regularization for neural networks.
