10.5 Regularization for Neural Networks

An MLP with enough units can memorize any labelling — train accuracy 1.0 means nothing (4.1). Neural-network regularization is classical statistics wearing new clothes: weight decay is ridge (5.2), early stopping is the validation curve, data augmentation is a prior on transformations, dropout is ensemble averaging in disguise. This page measures each one on a net that genuinely overfits — the NumPy network of 10.4, retrained live.

Tier 3 — the PyTorch lab (dropout/weight-decay/batchnorm modules) runs in Colab; every measurement below is live:

1. Basic Concept

tool mechanism classical twin
L2 / weight decay penalize \(\lambda\|\theta\|^2\) in the loss; gradients shrink weights multiplicatively ridge 5.2
Dropout randomly zero units with prob \(p\) per step (rescaling by \(1/(1-p)\)); the net can’t co-adapt bagging / model averaging 7.3
Early stopping halt when validation loss stops improving validation curves 4.2
Data augmentation train on label-preserving transforms a prior over invariances
Batch/Layer norm normalize activations; adds noise via batch statistics + smooths the loss surface standardization 0.3, internalized

Worked example — overfitting, then repairing. A wide NumPy MLP (manual backprop, 10.4 machinery) trained on 40 noisy moon points memorizes them; L2 and early stopping recover generalization:

All three rows share the same striking fact: with only 40 training points, validation CE bottoms at initialization — every step of unregularized training overfits. L2 is what makes training safe: it bends the final-step validation CE from 3.17 (no L2) down to 0.75 (λ = 1e-2) while training the full 3000 steps. Early stopping and weight decay are two dials on the same train–validation gap; watch the validation curve, not the training curve.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Weight-decay gradient = gradient + \(2\lambda w\). Why does this shrink large weights proportionally more than small ones, and what geometric belief does that encode?
  2. Dropout with \(p = 0.5\) at train time: why must activations be rescaled, and what does the network become at test time when rescaling is folded in (“inverted dropout”)?
  3. Early stopping picks one point on a 1-D path traced by training. Why is it “L2’s cheap cousin” — and when do the two not substitute for each other?
  4. Why does data augmentation regularize without touching the loss function — what exactly is being added to the training distribution?
  5. model.eval() — which two layer types change behavior, and what goes wrong metric-wise if you forget it?
Answers
  1. Update \(w \mathrel{-}= \eta(\nabla_w L + 2\lambda w)\): per step every weight loses a \(2\eta\lambda\) fraction — big weights shrink by more absolute amount and the same relative amount; small ones toward-but-never-exactly zero (unlike Lasso 5.3). Encodes: solutions live near the origin / functions with small weights are smoother (bounded derivatives).
  2. Zeroing half the units halves a layer’s expected output; without rescaling the next layer’s inputs shrink and drift. Inverted dropout divides kept activations by \((1-p)\) during training, so test time needs no change — the expected activation matches. The un-inverted version (original paper) rescales at test time — a deployment foot-gun.
  3. Both restrict the effective model capacity along a 1-D index (steps ↔︎ \(\lambda\)): the early-stopped net approximates the L2 solution of a matched \(\lambda\) for simple quadratic losses. They diverge when the loss surface is non-convex/path-dependent: stopping picks one point of one trajectory (depends on lr, batch order), L2 changes the objective itself; combined they are not redundant in practice.
  4. Augmentation enlarges the empirical distribution by the invariance group (rotations/flip/noise): the model is optimized over more of input space for the same labels — equivalent to a prior that those transformations don’t change class. It can hurt if the transform isn’t label-preserving (horizontal flip on ‘b’ vs ‘d’).
  5. Dropout (switches from random zeroing to identity) and batchnorm (running statistics instead of batch statistics). Forgetting eval() makes validation metrics noisy (dropout resampled per batch) and shifted (batch stats of the val set) — classic “validation loss jitter” bug.

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. λ-sweep with an honest protocol: using the Section-1 training function, sweep λ ∈ {0, 1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1} on 200 noisy moons (75/25 split); plot validation loss vs λ and report test accuracy of the λ chosen by validation. Then refit at the best λ on train+validation and report that test accuracy — why is the second number the more honest estimate, and what is this protocol called (4.6)?

Solution
Selecting λ on validation then reporting that same split’s accuracy double-dips; the refit-on-train+val → test protocol is the standard hyperparameter loop (CV in 4.3 averages it further). Expect the sweep to favor an intermediate λ with the extremes (0: overfit; 1e-1: underfit) losing — the U-curve of 4.4 reappearing in neural clothing.

Exercise 2. Dropout by hand: implement inverted dropout inside the Section-1 network’s hidden layer (mask after tanh, divide kept units by \(1-p\)), train at p ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.5} on the 40-point overfit set, and compare validation loss. Then verify the theoretical claim E[test output] ≈ train-time expected output: for a fixed input, average 2000 dropped forward passes and compare with the no-drop pass through the same weights.

Solution
Moderate dropout (p = 0.2) tames the 40-point overfit; p = 0.5 on 30 training points can underfit (few effective units). The Monte-Carlo mean of dropped passes matches the no-drop pass up to sampling error — that equality is inverted dropout’s design: train-time randomness, test-time determinism, matched expectations.

Exercise 3. scikit-learn’s knobs, live: on 200-point noisy moons compare MLPClassifier(alpha ∈ {1e-5, 1e-2, 1}) (that’s L2) and early_stopping=True vs default, under a fixed 75/25 split. Which knob buys more generalization here, and what does best_validation_score_ report when early_stopping=True?

Solution
Both knobs matter; their relative value is dataset-dependent — tiny noisy data rewards early stopping strongly, larger cleaner data rewards α tuning. early_stopping=True makes sklearn carve an internal validation split (see validation_fraction) and reports best_validation_score_ at the stopping iteration — i.e., the page’s Section-1 plot, automated. Either way: the number to quote is on held-out data, never on the training set.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Weight decay ≠ L2 under adaptive optimizers: with plain SGD, penalty-λ and update-time decay are algebraically identical; under Adam, adding λ to the loss gets preconditioned per-coordinate, while decoupled weight decay (AdamW) shrinks weights directly — Adam + L2-in-loss is a subtly different, worse-behaved regularizer. When reading torch code, weight_decay= in the optimizer is AdamW-style only if you use AdamW.
  • Dropout placement: on activations between layers, not on inputs (usually) and not after the final logits; combined with batchnorm it distorts the running statistics — modern practice prefers one or the other per block.
  • Early stopping needs an honest, untouched validation stream: if the val set leaked into training (scaler fitted on it, augmentation derived from it), the stopping point is biased optimistic.
  • Augmentation must preserve labels and match deployment: heavy random crops on small medical images can erase the lesion; simulate the transform before shipping it.
  • λ and lr interact: weight decay’s per-step fraction is \(2\eta\lambda\) — retune λ after changing lr; sweeps that vary both simultaneously without a grid conflate them.
  • The best regularizer is often a smaller model: on 40 points, halving the width beats any λ. Capacity is a hyperparameter (10.10).

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI training loop has three regularization bugs. Diagnose before opening the fix.

import torch
model = Net(dropout_p=0.5)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-2)
for X_batch, y_batch in train_loader:
    model.train()
    loss = ce(model(X_batch), y_batch)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

model.train()                                   # BUG 1
with torch.no_grad():
    preds = [model(X_val) for _ in range(5)]    # BUG 2
val_loss = ce(torch.stack(preds).mean(0), y_val)
best = None
if val_loss < best_loss:                        # BUG 3
    best_loss = val_loss
    torch.save(model.state_dict(), "best.pt")
What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. model.train() at validation time: dropout keeps zeroing units — validation loss is noisy and biased; must call model.eval() (and the batchnorm running stats freeze too).
  2. Averaging dropout passes at eval: an MC-dropout technique — fine for uncertainty estimation, wrong for a plain validation metric; with eval() one deterministic pass is the number.
  3. best_loss never initialized (best = None but comparison uses best_loss): NameError on first epoch — and if initialized to 0.0 instead of inf, nothing ever saves. Initialize best_loss = float("inf") and save state_dict() copies, not files you may overwrite mid-training.
model.eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    val_loss = ce(model(X_val), y_val)
best_loss = float("inf")                        # before the loop
if val_loss < best_loss:
    best_loss = val_loss
    best_state = {k: v.clone() for k, v in model.state_dict().items()}

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive dropout’s ensemble interpretation for a linear network: with input dropout at rate p and a single layer \(\hat y = w^\top \tilde x\), show that test-time scaling by \((1-p)\) makes the expected prediction equal an average over \(2^d\) sub-networks, and that the training-time gradient is an unbiased estimate of that ensemble’s gradient. Where does the exact equivalence break for deep nonlinear nets — and what replaces it (two words: sampling; averaging)?

Hint Linearity lets you swap expectation and matmul; write E[˜x ˜xᵀ] for the dropped input and compare with (1−p)xxᵀ.
Solution For \(\tilde x = m \odot x\), \(m_i \sim \text{Bern}(1-p)\): \(E[\hat y] = w^\top E[\tilde x] = (1-p)\,w^\top x\) — hence test-time multiply by \((1-p)\) (or train-time \(1/(1-p)\), inverted). The training objective \(E_m[(y - w^\top(m \odot x))^2]\) is exactly the average squared error over all \(2^d\) masked sub-models — dropout = bagging over unit subsets with shared weights. Gradients: \(\nabla_w = E_m[2(w^\top \tilde x - y)\tilde x]\), and each minibatch sample of m is an unbiased Monte-Carlo draw of it. For deep nets the product of nonlinearities makes “expectation of the function” ≠ “function of the expectation”: exact equivalence dies at the first nonlinearity; what remains is (i) training samples random sub-networks (regularizing co-adaptation) and (ii) at test time the deterministic scaled pass approximates the ensemble average — the gap between approximation and truth is why MC-dropout at eval (many stochastic passes, averaged) can outperform the standard pass for uncertainty, at 5–100× the compute.

Q2. Double descent, demonstrated in miniature: on 150 noisy moon points, sweep a 1-hidden-layer NumPy net’s width over {4, 16, 64, 256, 1024} with no L2 and early stopping disabled, plotting both test error and train error vs width (log x). Identify the interpolation threshold’s neighborhood (train error → 0), and state the two regimes around it. Then re-run the largest width with λ = 1e-2: where does the minimum of test error move, and what does that say about “bigger models overfit more”?

Hint Test error peaks somewhere near where train error first hits 0 (the classical U-curve’s cliff), then can decline again into the overparameterized regime.
Solution
Reading: as width grows, train CE → 0 (interpolation); around the threshold, test error peaks (variance max: the model is just rich enough to fit noise exactly); beyond it, test error declines again — the “modern” overparameterized regime where implicit regularization of GD prefers smooth interpolators (10.4). Explicit L2 at width 1024 moves the minimum earlier and lower — the two mechanisms compose. The classical “bigger = overfit” curve is the left half of the picture only; the honest statement is regularize something (width, λ, stopping, or nothing-but-SGD-noise) and validate — never assume which regime you’re in.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (× 4.3 cross-validation × 7.4 random forests). Dropout and random forests both average randomized sub-models. Design an experiment on 200 noisy moons: (a) an MLP with dropout swept over {0, .1, .3, .5}, (b) a random forest with max_features swept over {0.2, 0.5, 1.0}, under identical 5-fold CV. For each, plot CV accuracy vs its randomness knob and locate the optimum. Then answer: why does the forest benefit from more randomness (up to a point) while the net’s benefit peaks and decays — connect to base-learner strength (deep, jointly trained features vs shallow CARTs).

C2 (× 5.2 ridge × 9.2 PCA × 10.4). Three regularizations, one network, one budget: on the 40-point overfit set, find the best (width, λ, early-stop step) via a small grid, and compare against (i) PCA to 5 features → ridge (the classical pipeline), and (ii) the full net with no regularization. Report test accuracies and variance across 10 seeds. Which regularizer buys the most stability (lowest seed-variance), and what does that suggest about where neural nets actually fail — variance or bias?

7. Summary

  • Overfitting is the default outcome of training: the validation curve, not train loss, is the instrument.
  • Weight decay = ridge by gradient (per-step shrink \(2\eta\lambda w\)); under Adam prefer decoupled AdamW.
  • Dropout = training-time ensemble sampling with test-time expectation matching (inverted scaling); model.eval() is not optional.
  • Early stopping = 1-D capacity control on the training path; needs an honest validation stream.
  • Augmentation injects invariances as data; transforms must be label-preserving and deployment-realistic.
  • Around the interpolation threshold, more width can help — “bigger overfits more” is half-true, and regularization composes across all levers.
