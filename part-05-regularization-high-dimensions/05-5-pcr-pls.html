5.5 PCR and PLS

Instead of shrinking coefficients, shrink the feature space: summarize correlated predictors by a few derived directions, then regress on those. PCR builds directions from the predictors alone (PCA); PLS builds them using y. Both trade interpretability of individual coefficients for stability under extreme collinearity — and their contrast is a masterclass in supervised vs unsupervised thinking.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

PCR recipe: standardize X → PCA on X → keep the first M principal components → regress y on those M scores. The only new hyperparameter is M. PLS recipe: same pipeline, but directions are chosen to maximize covariance with y, not variance within X.

2. Concept Questions

  1. PCR with M = p (all components, after standardizing) reproduces which estimator exactly? What does that make M, in bias–variance language?
  2. Why must PCA inside PCR be fit on training folds only? Which page’s law is this?
  3. PLS’s first direction maximizes Cov(Xw, y). In one sentence, why can that find the tiny-variance signal that PCR’s first component cannot?
  4. You interpret “component 1 loading +0.5 on gene A” — what stops you from reading it as “gene A’s effect is +0.5”?
  5. Ridge also damps low-variance directions (5.2 SVD view). What is PCR’s version of the same move, and what’s the essential difference?
Answers
  1. OLS on standardized features: keeping all p orthogonal components loses no information, and the regression on full PCA scores equals OLS. M is a pure flexibility dial — M = 0 is the mean-model, M = p is OLS (4.4’s dial again).
  2. PCA is fitted from X — including validation rows’ means/structure; fitting it on all data leaks fold information into training (0.5, 4.3). The Pipeline mechanic enforces it.
  3. PCR ranks directions by X’s internal variance; PLS’s criterion explicitly includes y, so a direction with little X-variance but strong y-relationship wins PLS’s first slot while it lands at the bottom of PCR’s list.
  4. Components are rotations: loadings mix all features with sign/convention freedom, and the component’s coefficient (not the loading) carries the y-relationship, itself shrunken by the regression on scores. Individual-feature effects are unrecoverable without rotating back — interpret the component as a measurement direction, not a mechanism.
  5. PCR truncates small-variance directions (keeps M, drops the rest); ridge damps them smoothly (factor s²/(s²+λ)). Same SVD backdrop (5.2 Q1), hard vs soft; both are blind to y when choosing what to suppress — PLS is the supervised cousin.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. PCR vs OLS under extreme collinearity: n = 50, p = 15, built from 5 noisy copies of each of 2 latent factors plus 5 pure-noise columns (so the effective rank is ≈ 2–4). Compare: OLS, PCR with M chosen by CV, and ridge with α chosen by CV. Report nested test MSEs via the outer-loop discipline of 4.6. Rank them and explain the ordering in terms of what each does to the noisy small directions.

Solution
Ridge wins (0.902), OLS is essentially tied with it (0.953), and PCR is far and away the worst (2.604) — the opposite of the textbook intuition that PCR “truncates away the noise directions.” The diagnosis is in the last line: inner CV selects M = 6, not the effective rank of 2. The five pure-noise columns were drawn from N(0, 1), the same marginal variance as the latent columns, so they sit high in the variance ordering indistinguishable from signal. PCR’s cutoff is a hard decision made on variance alone, and variance order is not relevance order — it spends its budget on noise columns it cannot tell apart from signal. Ridge never has to make that call: it damps every direction smoothly, so the noise columns are shrunk without ever being adjudicated. This is the first hint of the theme that dominates the rest of the page — when variance ≠ relevance, unsupervised truncation is the wrong tool and a supervised direction method (PLS, next exercise) is the right one.

Exercise 2. The supervised-vs-unsupervised contrast, swept: construct the Section 1 pathological geometry at three signal-to-noise ratios (tiny-direction coefficient c ∈ {5, 20, 60} with direction SD 0.1). For each, compute CV-MSE of PCR(M=1), PCR(M chosen by CV), PLS(M=1). At which c does PCR’s CV finally pick M = 2 — and what does that tell you about using X-internal criteria to hunt y-relevant structure?

Solution
Only when the tiny direction’s signal grows loud enough does CV promote M = 2 (and PCR’s error collapses to PLS-like levels) — the X-internal criterion needed rescue from the y to notice relevance. PLS(1) is insensitive to c’s scale (it locks onto the covariance immediately). Moral: unsupervised compression is a bet that variance = relevance; PLS/weak supervision is the hedge.

Exercise 3. Interpretation audit: fit PCR (M = 2) on a dataset with known truth (y = 3x₁ − x₄ + noise, all features independent so loadings are readable). Report (a) the component loadings, (b) the regression coefficients on components, (c) the reconstructed per-feature coefficients β̂ = V·γ (rotate back), compared to OLS’s coefficients. Then explain to a collaborator why (c) is legitimate for describing the fitted model while per-feature p-values remain unavailable.

Solution
Rotation back through V is exact algebra for the fitted model — the two parameterizations predict identically, so (c) is the honest per-feature description of what PCR actually fit (and with independent features it lands close to OLS, modulo the dropped components’ shrinkage). What’s not available: standard errors/p-values per feature — the components were data-chosen, and the truncation shares information across features (5.1’s post-selection caution, rotated).

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • The Pipeline discipline is existential here: PCA is fitted from X, so fitting it before splitting leaks validation structure into training — the most common PCR bug in the wild (0.5).
  • Standardize before PCA unless features share natural units and scale: PCA’s variance-ranking is unit-dependent (5.2’s standardization law, inherited).
  • M is chosen by CV, not by scree plots: “keep components covering 90% variance” optimizes X-description, not y-prediction — the whole lesson of Exercise 2.
  • PLS with M = p ≈ OLS but is numerically optimized sequentially; don’t treat its M = p endpoint as an identity to 12 decimals.
  • PLS’s coefficients and loadings are even harder to read than PCR’s (directions tilted toward y, deflation between steps); report predictions and component meanings, not per-feature stories.
  • y-scaling does not move PLS’s path — a counterintuitive one: PLS component directions are invariant to multiplying y by a constant (x_weights_ on y and on 1000·y agree to 2.6e−16), because every criterion in the NIPALS iteration is a ratio that cancels the scale. Only the final coefficients scale (exactly 1000×). So rescaling y changes the units of the coefficients, never the subspace you are looking at — contrast with PCR, where the y-scale never enters at all. What does matter is scale=True on the X side, since that decides the variance ordering PLS inherits.
  • Both are linear-in-components: nonlinear structure needs basis expansion first (2.8) or the tree/kernel machinery of later parts.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove PCR’s M = p equivalence with OLS (after standardization): the PCA scores are X·V where V is the orthogonal loading matrix, and regressing y on X·V with all p components yields coefficients γ = Vᵀβ̂_OLS, so predictions V·γ = β̂_OLS·X exactly. Then explain why the equivalence breaks when M < p is chosen by CV on the same data used to report OLS’s R² — and what that asymmetry means for comparing methods on training data.

Hint Rotations preserve column spaces: span(XV) = span(X). For M < p, the fitted models are different projections — and any comparison computed in-sample is dominated by each model’s own optimism.
Solution With standardized X = USVᵀ, scores Z = XV span the same space as X; OLS on Z fits the same projection of y, hence identical fitted values/R² (γ = Vᵀβ̂ rotates back exactly). For M < p, PCR projects y onto a subspace — a different, shrunken model; comparing its training R² to OLS’s is meaningless because each number carries its own optimism (p + 1 vs M + 1 parameters’ worth, per 4.1 Q1). All method comparisons must happen out-of-fold — the asymmetric lesson being that in-sample tables systematically flatter the most flexible method (OLS) precisely where regularization is needed most.

Q2. PLS’s first direction solves \(\max_w \text{Cov}^2(Xw, y)\) subject to ‖w‖ = 1 (NIPALS essentially does power iteration on XᵀyyᵀX). Verify numerically on a small problem: compare sklearn’s first x_weights_ to the dominant eigenvector of XᵀyyᵀX (standardized), and to the OLS direction β̂_OLS/‖β̂_OLS‖. When do the PLS and OLS directions coincide (construct the case), and what does the mismatch measure?

Hint XᵀyyᵀX = (Xᵀy)(Xᵀy)ᵀ is rank one → its dominant eigenvector IS Xᵀy (∝ the OLS direction when columns are standardized and uncorrelated).
Solution

For a single component, PLS’s criterion has closed form w ∝ Xᵀy — exactly the OLS direction’s shape. With uncorrelated standardized columns, XᵀX = I makes Xᵀy the OLS coefficient vector itself, so PLS(1) and OLS point the same way; correlations tilt PLS toward high-covariance blocks and away from OLS’s precision.

The alignment to XᵀyyᵀX’s eigenvector is exact (both are ±Xᵀy); the OLS alignment is exact only under uncorrelated design — the discrepancy is PLS trading OLS’s per-coordinate optimality for direction stability under collinearity, which is precisely its selling point.

6. Complex Questions

Which pair would you present to a review board that demands “number of dimensions used” — and why does that question favor PCR/PLS even at equal MSE?

Confirm the U-curve in M, identify whether bias or variance dominates at M = 1 for (a) latent-factor truths (Section 1 style), (b) the pathological low-variance-signal truth. The two panels are the argument for PLS — write the caption.

7. Summary

  • PCR: PCA on X, regress on the top M scores; M = p recovers OLS, M is the flexibility dial.
  • PLS: directions chosen by covariance with y — supervised compression that finds low-variance signal PCR cannot.
  • Both need standardization, in-pipeline fitting, and CV-chosen M (never scree).
  • Hard truncation vs ridge’s soft damping — same SVD stage, different smoothing of the same idea.
  • Component loadings are rotations, not effects; per-feature inference stays unavailable (or rotated and caveated).
  • The pathological direction experiment is the method-selection decision tree in miniature: variance-blind (PCR/ridge) vs y-aware (PLS/lasso).
  • When p truly dwarfs n — thousands of features, dozens of rows — every choice here gets sharper consequences: 5.6 High-dimensional data.
