5.6 High-Dimensional Data

Genomics asked statistics a question it wasn’t built for: 20,000 features, 40 samples. In the p ≫ n regime every rule from Part 2 inverts — OLS fits perfectly and means nothing, R² = 1 is the default state of noise, and the disciplines of Parts 4–5 stop being best practice and become survival equipment.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Three structural facts define the regime:

  1. Interpolation is guaranteed: p ≥ n means a hyperplane through every training point exists — training error = 0 always (4.1).
  2. Classical inference collapses: OLS SEs need df = n − p − 1 > 0; with p ≥ n there are no residual degrees of freedom, so no σ̂², no t-tests, no F-tests.
  3. Selection is unavoidable: you cannot “use all features” — some reduction (penalty, components, filters) is mathematically mandatory.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why exactly are there no residual degrees of freedom when p ≥ n, and which downstream quantities die first as p approaches n?
  2. Your colleague reports an OLS on 200 samples with 500 covariates and “significant” adjusted R² of 0.95. Name the three disqualifications.
  3. Why does CV become more important in p ≫ n — and what special care does stratification/selection need here?
  4. Lasso can select at most n features when p ≫ n. You need “the” biomarker panel of 12. Is lasso’s 12-feature selection from 5,000 candidates trustworthy as a discovery list?
  5. Distance-based methods (KNN, 2.9) fail hardest in high dimensions. Why, and which Part 5 tools repair them?
Answers
  1. df = n − (p + 1); it hits 0 at p = n − 1. σ̂² = RSS/(n − p − 1) becomes 0/0 or negative → every t-test, F-test, CI, RSE, and Cp/AIC penalty built on σ̂² dies in sequence.
    1. p > n: the fit interpolates — R² is 1 by construction, not by skill; (2) adjusted R²’s formula assumes p ≪ n and is garbage in this regime; (3) “significant” claims require SEs, which don’t exist (Concept 1). The 0.95 is the interpolation identity wearing a suit.
  3. Because model selection (which penalty, which features, which M) now carries the whole burden — with no unpenalized fallback, tuning choices dominate the outcome, so the selection must be honest and repeated (4.3). Selection stability across folds/resamples must be reported because single supports are noise.
  4. No. It’s one arbitrary draw from a near-tie set of correlated candidates (5.1 instability, 5.3 twins); the defensible products are (a) its predictive performance (nested CV), (b) selection frequencies across resamples — never a fixed “panel of 12” narrative.
  5. High-dimensional distances concentrate: nearest and farthest neighbors converge (2.9’s curse), so neighborhoods stop being local. Repair: reduce dimension first (PCR directions, 5.5), or select features so distances are computed in the signal subspace — both are Part 5 tools.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. The p/n frontier, mapped: fix n = 100 and sweep p ∈ {10, 50, 100, 200, 500} with a fixed 5-signal truth (signals in the first 5 features when p allows; extra features pure noise). For each p, report OLS train R², OLS test R², and RidgeCV’s test R². Where does OLS’s train R² hit 1.0, and how does each method’s test performance degrade with p? State the “usable dimensionality” rule of thumb this table supports.

Solution
Train R² pins to 1.0 exactly at p = n; OLS’s test R² turns negative beyond and stays dead. Ridge degrades gracefully, retaining real predictive value even at p = 5n. Rule of thumb: unpenalized regression is defensible while p stays a small fraction of n (and effects are strong); the moment p/n ≳ 0.5, penalized methods are the toolset, full stop.

Exercise 2. Selection stability at genomic scale: n = 50, p = 300, 8 true genes. Run LassoCV selection on 100 bootstrap resamples; report (a) how often each true gene is selected, (b) how often each of the top-5 most frequently selected noise genes is selected, (c) the median support size. Then compute the overlap of consecutive supports (Jaccard). Write the two-sentence methods paragraph reporting this honestly — the paragraph your genomics paper needs.

Solution
True genes are selected often (but not always — power at n = 50); several noise genes cross 20–40% selection by chance, and consecutive supports overlap only partially. Honest paragraph: “Lasso selection at CV-chosen penalization was repeated across 100 bootstrap resamples. Features are ranked by selection frequency; we report the full frequency table rather than a fixed panel, noting that individual supports are unstable under resampling (median support size X, mean pairwise Jaccard Y). Predictive performance was assessed by nested cross-validation.”

Exercise 3. The full high-dimensional workflow, end to end: n = 80 patients, p = 600 genes, outcome binary (case/control, 40/40), driven by 5 genes with moderate effect. Build and honestly evaluate: standardize → lasso-logistic (C tuned by inner CV) → outer 5-fold stratified CV → report AUC, plus selection frequencies per gene across outer folds. Then answer: which single number in your report is the performance claim, and which artifact is the exploratory output?

Solution
The performance claim is the outer-CV AUC with its SD — that number prices the whole procedure including feature selection. The gene list (and its frequencies) is exploratory: it generates hypotheses for validation in fresh cohorts, and its instability across folds is exactly why frequencies, not a fixed list, get reported. Conflating the two — quoting the AUC and presenting the support as discovered truth — is the p ≫ n era’s signature overreach.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • No unpenalized fallback exists: in p ≥ n, “just fit OLS” is not an option, so the entire modeling decision space is Part 5’s — penalty family, strength, and preprocessing are the model.
  • Batch effects and unwanted variation are high-dimensional biology’s confounders: they correlate with thousands of features at once and dominate PCA/lasso if uncorrected — normalization/combat-style adjustment precedes everything (and must itself live inside CV for honest evaluation).
  • Multiplicity is ambient: 20,000 genes ⇒ 20,000 implicit tests even before you “test” anything; every marginal screen needs FDR machinery (12.4 stub), and lasso-style joint selection needs stability reporting (5.1).
  • Prediction ≠ discovery, twice over: the selected genes are not validated by the model’s AUC (they rode along with it); conversely, a well-validated model can consist mostly of non-causal tags. State which claim each result supports.
  • Effective sample size may be smaller than n: correlated samples (related patients, batches, repeated measures) shrink n below its nominal value exactly when you need every row — the grouped-splitting discipline of 4.3 applies to the units, not rows.
  • Double descent’s home turf: at p ≈ n test error peaks, and overparameterized interpolators can descend again (4.4 Q2) — a real phenomenon, but not a license: the descent region’s gains are model-specific and modest relative to just collecting more samples.
  • Reporting standards: n, p, prevalence, penalty family, tuning grid, inner/outer CV protocol, selection frequencies, and a null-simulation calibration check. If any is missing, the result isn’t reproducible.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. OLS at p ≫ n: the 1.0000 train R² is the interpolation identity; no SEs exist; the fit is meaningless.
  2. Screen-then-fit on the same rows: with a null outcome, this manufactures “hits” with inflated in-sample R² — the entire result is the selection’s shadow (5.1 Exercise 2).
  3. Discovery claim from marginal p < 0.05 over 800 tests: expect ~40 false positives at this rate; no FDR control, no validation, no stability — nothing supports the word “identified.”

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the expected training R² under the null for OLS with n rows and p ≤ n−1: using E[RSS] = (n − p − 1)σ² (errors dominate), show E[R²] ≈ p/(n − 1). Verify by simulation across a p-sweep, and connect the formula to (a) the adjusted-R² penalty, (b) why train R² = p/n-lookalikes certify nothing in high dimensions.

Hint TSS ≈ (n−1)σ² under the null; RSS ≈ (n−p−1)σ²; R² = 1 − RSS/TSS → p/(n−1).
Solution

Under the null (y independent of X), OLS consumes p + 1 df on noise: E[RSS] = (n − p − 1)σ², and TSS ≈ (n − 1)σ², so E[R²] = 1 − (n − p − 1)/(n − 1) = p/(n − 1).

  1. Adjusted R² subtracts exactly this noise inflation (that’s its whole job — and why its derivation assumes p ≪ n). (b) In the p ≈ n regime the “explained variance” of a null fit approaches 1 — every in-sample goodness number is the complexity charge in disguise, and only out-of-sample estimates retain meaning.

Q2. You must choose between two p ≫ n studies to trust: Study A reports a 15-gene panel selected by lasso with CV AUC 0.84; Study B reports the same genes independently validated (AUC 0.71) in an external cohort of 200 samples. Construct the argument (with a mini-simulation if useful) for why B’s lower AUC is the stronger scientific claim, and design the combined analysis you’d actually run given both datasets (selection in A, frozen evaluation in B, then pooled refit with stability reporting).

Solution

A’s 0.84 prices selection adaptivity but lives on n = 40-ish effective samples and resampling within one dataset — its CI covers optimistic territory (selection bonus + small-n noise + any batch structure). B’s 0.71 is a pre-registered (frozen-panel) evaluation on fresh rows: no selection degrees of freedom, so 0.71 is a claim about the world, not the procedure. Simulation sketch:

The transfer number typically sits below the internal number (selection + small-n optimism) yet carries more evidential weight. Combined analysis: tune/choose on A (or pooled inner folds), freeze, evaluate on B untouched; finally refit on all data and report per-gene selection frequencies across resamples — with the two datasets’ batch structures declared. The hierarchy of evidence: frozen external validation > internal nested CV > internal CV of the selected model > anything with “significant” and p ≫ n in the same sentence.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (p ≫ n × 5.2–5.5 family reunion). At n = 60, p = 800 (latent-factor truth, 8 active features, duplicate-block structure): run the complete Part 5 tournament — ridge, lasso, net, PCR, PLS — each tuned inside an outer CV. Deliver (a) the accuracy table, (b) each winner’s complexity measure (effective df / support size / M), (c) selection stability for the sparse methods. Write the discussion paragraph: which method would you pre-register for the next cohort, and what information would justify changing?

C2 (p ≫ n × 12.4 FDR stub × 3.1.5 AUPRC). Connect the two selection languages: run a marginal screen (p-values for 1,000 features under a sparse truth), apply BH-FDR at q = 0.05 to get a “significant” set, then run LassoCV selection on the same data. Compare the two feature sets (size, truth overlap) and their precision (fraction true). Show that lasso’s support behaves like a data-adaptive FDR-thresholded set — and identify the condition (strong signal, weak correlation) under which the vocabularies coincide, previewing Part 12’s formal treatment.

7. Summary

  • p ≥ n: interpolation guaranteed, R² = 1 meaningless, residual df gone — inference and unpenalized fitting are off the table.
  • E[train R²] ≈ p/(n−1) under the null: in-sample numbers measure complexity, not discovery.
  • Survival kit: penalized regression (ridge/lasso/net), direction methods (PCR/PLS), filters — all inside honest nested CV.
  • Selection in this regime is unstable by nature; report frequencies and validation, never fixed panels as discoveries.
  • Frozen external validation outranks every internal number; batch/unwanted variation and unit-level splitting are design-level duties.
  • The p ≫ n workflow’s product is a calibrated claim: performance for prediction, hypotheses for discovery, never both from one number.
  • Part 5 closes the classical toolkit. Next the models bend shape — polynomials, splines and smoothing in Part 6 — then trees, SVMs, and unsupervised structure beginning with the PCA depths at 9.2 PCA.
