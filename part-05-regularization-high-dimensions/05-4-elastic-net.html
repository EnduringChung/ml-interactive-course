5.4 Elastic Net

Ridge groups correlated features but never zeroes them; lasso zeroes features but coin-flips among correlated twins (5.2, 5.3). The elastic net mixes both penalties, getting sparsity and grouping — and introduces a second hyperparameter that captures a real modeling question: how much do you believe features are interchangeable?

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

\[\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \Big\{ \sum_i (y_i - \beta_0 - x_i^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda \big( \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\|\beta\|_2^2 + \alpha\|\beta\|_1 \big) \Big\}\]

Two dials: λ (overall penalty — sklearn’s alpha) and α (the L1 share — sklearn’s l1_ratio). Endpoints recover the classics: α = 1 → lasso, α = 0 → ridge.

2. Concept Questions

  1. In sklearn’s parameterization, what do alpha = 0.1, l1_ratio = 0.7 mean — and what does alpha = 0 give you exactly, regardless of l1_ratio?
  2. Why does the ridge component stabilize selection among correlated twins? Explain via the penalty’s geometry (edges vs corners).
  3. The elastic net has two tuning parameters. What does the CV surface over (λ, α) typically look like, and what does a flat ridge along α tell you?
  4. Your lasso selects 3 of 30 features; the net selects 8 (including near-duplicates). Which model would you deploy for (a) a $1-per-feature measurement budget, (b) pure prediction with no measurement cost?
  5. Why does elastic net sometimes select more features than n (unlike lasso’s ≤ n cap)? When does that matter?
Answers
  1. The objective penalizes 0.15·‖β‖² + 0.7·‖β‖₁ (times λ = 0.1): mostly-L1 with a ridge floor. alpha = 0 zeroes the whole penalty → plain OLS at any l1_ratio — the ratio only matters when a penalty exists.
  2. The L1 diamond has sharp corners/edges where optima sit — among equal twins the fit picks one edge/vertex (a winner). The L2 ball’s boundary is round: moving along the twin-subspace costs (nearly) nothing, so both twins can share the coefficient smoothly. Ridge’s grouping + L1’s sparsity = twins selected jointly with shrunken, stable values.
  3. Typically a long, flat valley along λ at moderate-to-high α: many (λ, α) pairs score within noise (4.6’s landscape flatness). A flat ridge along α says the data doesn’t care about the mixing share — pick by parsimony of the selected set, not the argmax.
    1. The 3-feature lasso — measurement cost is real money and the twins carry no extra signal. (b) Either on prediction grounds (indistinguishable by CV); the net is safer under future drift/collinearity because it doesn’t bet everything on one twin.
  5. The ridge component lets correlated groups enter jointly; a group of size g can share one “selection ticket” per member, so p_selected can exceed n. It matters when you’re interpreting support size as complexity, or in extreme p ≫ n where the net’s support can balloon — cap with stronger λ.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Map the two-dial landscape by hand: on a fixed dataset (n = 120, p = 25, 4 true signals, one duplicated pair), fit ElasticNet over α ∈ logspace(−3, 0, 10) × l1_ratio ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.0}, recording 5-fold CV MSE and support size per cell. Display the two heatmaps side by side. Locate: (a) the MSE argmin, (b) the smallest support within 1 SE of the best MSE. How different are their supports?

Solution
The landscape is the familiar flat valley (4.6): many cells tie within noise; the parsimony pick usually has a third of the support at statistically identical MSE — the one-SE rule, two parameters deep.

Exercise 2. The grouping staircase: build 8 features in 4 correlated pairs (within-pair ρ = 0.95, between pairs independent), with one pair truly active. Trace each method’s selected set across α (lasso: l1_ratio = 1) vs net (l1_ratio = 0.3) at matched CV-chosen λ. Report the “family selection rate”: how often does each method select both or neither member of the active pair, over 100 bootstrap resamples? This number is the operational definition of grouping.

Solution
The lasso’s rate hovers near coin-flip territory (it takes one twin and ignores the other), while the net’s rate approaches 1 — it treats the pair as the atom. When features come in natural groups (gene pathways, duplicate sensors), that rate is the property you’re buying.

Exercise 3. Method bake-off with honest evaluation (4.6): on one dataset (p = 40, sparse truth with a duplicated pair), compare OLS-with-top-k-filter (5.1), ridge, lasso, and elastic net — each tuned by inner 5-fold CV — evaluated by outer 5-fold nested CV. Report nested test MSE per procedure and the paired fold differences between the winner and runner-up. Write the two-sentence conclusion for a methods section.

Solution
Typical outcome: the penalized methods cluster within fold noise while filter+OLS trails (hard selection pays a variance tax). The write-up writes itself: “All shrinkage procedures performed equivalently (paired CV p > 0.05); we selected [method] for its [sparsity/stability] properties rather than predictive superiority.”

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Two dials, twice the selection tax: tuning (λ, α) jointly searches a bigger space than either parent — expect a slightly larger selection bonus and use nested CV accordingly (4.6).
  • sklearn’s alpha rescales with l1_ratio (the mixing formula divides by 1 − α/2-terms): comparing alphas across l1_ratio values is meaningless — compare CV scores, not parameter values.
  • l1_ratio = 1 is lasso, 0 is ridge — but tiny nonzero ratios aren’t pure anything: a 0.95 net is “lasso with insurance”; a 0.05 net is “ridge with occasional zeros.” Name the operating point, don’t just cite the class.
  • Standardization remains mandatory — the mixed penalty still counts coefficient magnitudes.
  • Groups need not be prescribed: the net groups correlated features automatically; for known groups (pathways), the group lasso exists beyond sklearn’s core — same spirit, structured penalty.
  • Support size ≠ degrees of freedom for the net: with twins jointly selected, df grows less than support (shared signal) — don’t reuse the lasso’s df = |support| identity here (5.3 Q2).
  • Convergence warnings matter: the mixed penalty is harder to optimize; watch max_iter, and prefer the CV wrappers that warm-start across the path.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Geometric derivation: draw (for p = 2) the lasso diamond, ridge disk, and the elastic net’s rounded diamond. Show that the net’s boundary has (a) corners on the axes (producing exact zeros like lasso) and (b) straight edges between corners that are flattened arcs (allowing twins to ride together like ridge). Explain why a fit at an edge gives equal coefficients to twins while a fit at a diamond’s edge forces |β₁| + |β₂| = c — one winner.

Hint The penalty region {(β₁,β₂): λ[(1−r)/2·‖β‖² + r‖β‖₁] ≤ c} mixes the diamond’s linear edges with quadratic arcs; the L1 part dominates near axes, the L2 part dominates near the diagonal.
Solution The net’s constraint region is a superellipse between diamond and disk. Near an axis (one coefficient large), the L1 term’s linear boundary dominates → sharp corner → solutions sit at β₂ = 0 exactly (sparsity). Along the diagonal (equal coefficients), for twins the L1 term still contributes 2r·λ|β| but the L2 arc’s curvature — driven by (1−r)/2·(β₁²+β₂²) — rounds the boundary; a tangency with correlated data’s elliptical RSS contours can occur mid-edge with β₁ = β₂ ≠ 0 (grouping). The pure diamond’s edge is dead straight: its tangency logic forces the fit to a vertex (one twin zero). Geometry is the whole story: corners select, curves share, and l1_ratio interpolates the boundary.

Q2. Design and run the stability-driven α choice: for the twins dataset, choose α_mix not by CV-MSE argmin but by maximizing selection stability (Jaccard overlap of supports across 50 bootstrap resamples) among candidates whose CV MSE is within 1 SE of the best. Report the chosen α_mix, its CV MSE vs the argmin’s, and the stability scores of both. When does this procedure change the answer, and what principle is it an instance of?

Solution
High-α (lasso-like) values show lower overlap — the twin coin-flip decorrelates supports; mid ratios stabilize. The procedure only changes the answer when the CV valley is flat (elsewhere it wastes stability on worse fits); it is the one-standard-error rule (4.6) with a second-order criterion swapped in: among statistically tied models, pick by a property you actually care about — here, reproducibility of the selected set.

7. Summary

  • Elastic net = λ[(1−r)/2·‖β‖² + r‖β‖₁]: sparsity from L1, grouping from L2; r = l1_ratio interpolates the parents.
  • Two dials mean two-dial selection tax and flatter CV valleys — one-SE/parsimony reasoning applies twice over.
  • Twins: lasso splits, net shares — the family-selection rate is the operational difference.
  • Endpoint sanity: l1_ratio 1/0 recover lasso/ridge exactly; alpha = 0 is OLS at any ratio.
  • Compare CV scores across (λ, α), never parameter values; standardization is still law.
  • Stability-driven choice of α (within 1 SE) buys reproducible supports when prediction can’t decide.
  • What if you never regress on features at all — regress on their principal directions instead? 5.5 PCR and PLS.
