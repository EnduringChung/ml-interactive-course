3.1.5 AUPRC for Skewed Datasets

When positives are rare (1% disease, 0.1% hits in a screen), ROC-AUC keeps flattering weak classifiers — the huge negative pool absorbs false alarms invisibly. The precision–recall curve and its area (AUPRC / average precision) put the cost of false alarms in a sparse world back in the picture.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

Sweeping the threshold from strict to loose traces a curve in precision (PPV = TP/(TP+FP)) vs recall (TPR) space. The area under it — computed as sklearn’s average precision,

\[\text{AP} = \sum_n (R_n - R_{n-1})\, P_n\]

i.e., each precision value weighted by the increase in recall it purchased — is the standard scalar (AUPRC).

Two facts make AUPRC the right lens under skew:

  1. The baseline is the prevalence, not 0.5: a random classifier’s PR curve hovers at precision = prevalence. AUPRC = 0.55 at 1% prevalence is massive; AUC = 0.55 would be negligible.
  2. False positives are priced in the metric: at low prevalence, each FP slashes precision immediately (denominator TP+FP), while in ROC-land an FP just nudges FPR by 1/(huge N).

A small worked example, by hand — 5 positives, 5 negatives, ranked by score:

rank score label precision@k recall@k
1 .9 + 1.000 0.2
2 .8 + 1.000 0.4
3 .7 − 0.667 0.4
4 .6 + 0.750 0.6
5 .5 − 0.600 0.6
6 .4 + 0.667 0.8
7 .3 − 0.571 0.8
8 .2 − 0.500 0.8
9 .1 − 0.444 0.8
10 .0 + 0.500 1.0

\[\text{AP} = 0.2(1.0) + 0.2(1.0) + 0.2(0.750) + 0.2(0.667) + 0.2(0.500) = 0.783\]

Each of the 5 recall increments (0.2) is weighted by the precision at which it was achieved. Same ranking’s ROC-AUC (concordant pairs, 3.1) = 18/25 = 0.72 — the two lenses already disagree.

2. Concept Questions

  1. Why is a random classifier’s PR baseline the prevalence while its ROC baseline is the diagonal?
  2. Two datasets have identical classifier quality; one has 1% prevalence, one 30%. Which metric is comparable across them — AUC, AP, or neither?
  3. AP = 0.35 at 5% prevalence: translate that into “how much better than random” in one sentence.
  4. Your model’s PR curve is strictly below the prevalence line everywhere. What does that tell you, and what single transformation rescues the model?
  5. Why does AP have no nice probabilistic interpretation like AUC’s concordance probability?
Answers
  1. Random scoring means precision ≈ prevalence at every recall level (each retrieved batch is prevalence-random), so the PR “curve” is the horizontal line y = prevalence. In ROC space, random scoring moves TPR and FPR together — the diagonal.
  2. AUC (it’s a within-pair probability, prevalence-independent in expectation). AP is prevalence-dependent by design — its baseline moves with prevalence — so absolute AP values don’t transfer across datasets with different class balance. Neither is a substitute for reporting the dataset’s prevalence alongside.
  3. Random retrieval gets precision 0.05; this model’s precision-recall trade is worth AP = 0.35, i.e. its average precision across recall levels is 7× the random baseline — strong for a 1-in-20 problem.
  4. Precision below prevalence at all recalls means the score is anti-ranked: top-scored items are less likely to be positive than random. Negate the scores (or flip the sign of the decision function) and the same information becomes a good model — always check this before discarding a model.
  5. AUC counts a specific, symmetric random experiment (draw one of each, who wins?). AP is a weighted sum of precisions with data-dependent weights (recall increments) — a different, asymmetric summary with no single natural random-pair story; its “meaning” is operational: expected precision averaged over recall levels you choose to chase.

Drills

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. By hand, build the full precision–recall table for the ranked list below (compute P@k, R@k for every k), compute AP from the table, and verify against average_precision_score. Then state the single threshold (top-k) you would choose if the budget allows exactly 3 follow-ups, and its precision.

Solution
The budget answer is a decision the curve exists to inform: with 3 slots you catch 2 of 5 positives (precision 0.67); the curve lets management see exactly what the 4th and 5th slot would buy.

Exercise 2. Prevalence surgery: fix one classifier and one score distribution; vary prevalence from 0.5% to 40% by subsampling negatives, and plot AUC and AP against prevalence. Which curve moves, which holds steady, and what does this experiment say about comparing your AP to a published AP from a different cohort?

Solution
AUC is flat (prevalence-free by construction); AP falls as prevalence falls — the baseline sinks with it. Consequence: AP values are cohort-specific; compare APs only within the same prevalence regime, or convert to “multiple of prevalence baseline” (AP / prevalence) for a rough cross-cohort normalized statement.

Exercise 3. Metric face-off on a realistic screen: build a 1%-positive dataset with a mildly informative score; compute (a) accuracy at 0.5, (b) balanced accuracy, (c) AUC, (d) AP, (e) precision at recall = 0.5. Rank the metrics by how usefully each describes the model for a screening manager who can only follow up 50 samples. Justify the ranking in two sentences per metric.

Solution
Usefulness ranking for the manager: (e) precision@recall-0.5 — an operating point, exactly what “we follow up 50” means; (d) AP — the full-curve summary, prevalence-anchored; (c) AUC — ranks well but abstracts away the FP flood; (b) balanced accuracy — treats classes symmetrically when the budget doesn’t; (a) accuracy — tells the manager nothing (it’s the prevalence in disguise). The general law: the closer a metric sits to the actual decision (budget, costs, threshold), the more decisions it can improve.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Three names, one thing (mostly): AUPRC (area under PR curve), AP (sklearn’s interpolated area), mean average precision (mAP — averaged over queries, the retrieval convention). sklearn’s average_precision_score uses the recall-increment weighting — not the trapezoidal rule — so hand-trapezoiding your PR points gives slightly different numbers (the worked table uses the sklearn definition; the trapezoid variant appears in Appendix A.7’s worked example).
  • AP is not comparable across cohorts with different prevalence (Exercise 2) — report prevalence next to every AP, always.
  • The last point of the PR curve: precision_recall_curve appends the anchor (recall 0, precision 1); the thresholds array is one shorter — off-by-one errors when zip()-ing live here.
  • Interpolated “best PR” curves (as in some leaderboards) paint an upper envelope that no actual threshold achieves; be explicit which convention you report.
  • AUPRC noise: with few positives the curve is jagged and AP’s sampling variance is large — bootstrap it (1.5) before declaring one model better than another.
  • PR wins under skew; ROC still wins for: cost-symmetric ranking questions, prevalence-robust reporting, and comparing against chance (0.5) — the metrics answer different questions; report both under skew.

What’s wrong, and the fix

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove the AP formula \(\text{AP} = \sum_n (R_n - R_{n-1})P_n\) equals the precision averaged over the recall axis for the step-function PR curve, and show that for a single threshold operating point (R₀, P₀) the formula contributes \(R_0 P_0 = TP_0 / N_+\) — connecting AP to the raw hit count. What does this imply about AP’s sensitivity to the last positives retrieved (high recall, low precision region)?

Hint Write each recall increment as ΔTP/N₊ and multiply by precision = TP/(TP+FP) at that step.
Solution \(R_n - R_{n-1} = \Delta TP_n / N_+\) and \(P_n = TP_n/(TP_n + FP_n)\), so each term counts positives gained weighted by their purity; summed, AP = (1/N₊) Σ ΔTP·P = mean precision per positive retrieved. For a model that puts all TPs first at one shot: one increment with \(R_0 = TP_0/N_+\), \(P_0 = 1\), contributing \(TP_0/N_+\) = fraction of positives found perfectly. Consequence: the tail matters — retrieving the final positives at precision ~prevalence adds \((R=1 - R_{prev}) \times \text{prevalence}\)-ish mass; with 5 positives, each late hit adds ~0.2 × 0.1 = 0.02. AP rewards getting most positives cleanly; it is forgiving of the last scrappy recoveries exactly in proportion to how few positives remain — a feature under skew, and the reason AP > AUC Rankings shuffles rarely change AP’s tail.

Q2. Your team must choose between Model A (AP = 0.21, AUC = 0.85) and Model B (AP = 0.24, AUC = 0.80) at 2% prevalence, with a fixed budget of 100 follow-ups per 10,000 screened. Design the decision experiment: what single metric or plot settles it, how do you quantify uncertainty (bootstrap), and how should the budget constraint enter the comparison? Implement it.

Solution
The budget turns the comparison into precision@k (top-100 hits) — AUC and AP both summarize the whole curve, but only k = 100 will be operated. The bootstrap CI on the hit difference says whether B’s sharper head is real or noise (here B wins at the operating point despite losing on AUC — a decisive, budget-aware answer). Generalize the pattern: choose the metric that mirrors the deployment constraint, then attach uncertainty to that metric, not to a curve-wide scalar that no one will ever operate.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (AUPRC × 3.1 metrics × 1.4 inference). Generate 200 resampled test sets (bootstrap the same test data) and compute AP and AUC distributions for one fixed model. Which metric’s CI is wider at 1% prevalence, and does the ordering of two competing models (by either metric) survive the uncertainty? Write the reporting rule that follows: when do you need the bootstrap before comparing two classifiers, and what sample size of positives makes single-number AP comparisons defensible?

C2 (AUPRC × 12.x multiple testing preview). A biomarker screen tests 20,000 candidates and reports “14 significant hits.” Reinterpret the screen as a classifier: define scores, prevalence, and AP for the hit list, then compute the precision you’d need for the list to beat random selection by a factor of 10. Connect to the FDR viewpoint: precision of a hit list at fixed size is 1 − FDR of that list — the two vocabularies are the same object seen from the ranking and the testing side.

7. Summary

  • Under class skew, PR curves price false alarms honestly; ROC’s huge negative pool hides them.
  • AP = Σ Δrecall × precision: mean precision weighted by positives gained; baseline = prevalence.
  • AUC stays comparable across prevalences; AP does not — report prevalence beside every AP.
  • Below-baseline PR curve = anti-ranked scores: flip before you discard.
  • The metric that mirrors the deployment constraint (precision@budget) beats any curve-wide scalar for decisions.
  • With few positives, bootstrap AP differences before believing them.
  • With both metric families in hand, ask the orthogonal question — why not just use linear regression on 0/1 labels? — 3.2 Why not linear regression for categories?
