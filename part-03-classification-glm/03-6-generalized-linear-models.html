3.6 Generalized Linear Models

Logistic regression was not a one-off trick — it is one instance of a general recipe: pick a response distribution, a link between its mean and the linear predictor, fit by maximum likelihood. That recipe — the GLM — handles counts, rates, proportions, and skewed positives with the same coefficient-interpretation and inference machinery as Part 2.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

A GLM has three ingredients:

  1. Random component — the response’s distribution family: Gaussian (linear regression), Binomial (logistic), Poisson (counts), Gamma (positive skewed), etc.
  2. Systematic component — the linear predictor \(\eta = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots\)
  3. Link function — \(g(\mu) = \eta\), connecting the mean μ to the predictor. Identity for Gaussian; logit for Binomial; log for Poisson.

The log link makes coefficients multiplicative: \(e^{\beta}\) is a rate ratio per unit of x. Fitting maximizes the family’s likelihood (IRLS again — same engine as 3.3).

2. Concept Questions

  1. Name the three ingredients of a GLM, and identify all three for (a) linear regression, (b) logistic regression, (c) Poisson regression.
  2. β = 0.25 with a log link. State the effect in rate-ratio language, and in plain words if the baseline rate is 2.0.
  3. Why does the Poisson assumption Var = mean matter inferentially — and what is the standard diagnostic when counts are more spread than that?
  4. What does an offset do, when is it required, and why is log(depth) the offset rather than depth itself?
  5. Your response is a proportion of successes out of varying n per row. Which GLM family and what structural detail of the fit?
Answers
    1. Gaussian; η = β₀+β₁x; identity. (b) Bernoulli/Binomial; η; logit. (c) Poisson; η; log. Every classical regression is this triple — memorize the table, and new response types stop being new methods.
  2. Each +1 in x multiplies the mean rate by e^0.25 ≈ 1.284 (+28.4%). At baseline 2.0: new rate ≈ 2.57 — the absolute jump grows with the baseline, which is exactly what multiplicative language encodes.
  3. Poisson’s Var = mean enters the likelihood AND the SEs: if real counts are overdispersed (Var ≫ mean, near-universal in biology), GLM SEs are too small and p-values overconfident — by the same mechanism as 2.10’s equal-variance violation. Diagnostic: Pearson χ²/df ≫ 1; remedies: quasi-Poisson (robust SEs) or negative binomial — the default in differential-expression pipelines (Part 12’s T2 workflow).
  4. An offset is a predictor with its coefficient fixed at 1: log λ = η + log(depth) ⇒ λ = depth × e^η — modeling rate per unit exposure. Required whenever observation effort/size varies (depths, person-years, areas). The log appears because the model is multiplicative: rate × exposure = count on the log scale becomes addition.
  5. Binomial with a logit link, as a 2-column response (successes, failures) — sm.GLM(y, X, family=sm.families.Binomial()) with y = [successes, failures] per row — so each row’s variance follows its own n. A plain 0/1 logistic (3.3) is the n = 1 special case.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Recover a rate model end-to-end: simulate hospital readmissions where each patient has an exposure (days, 1–30) and a covariate x (age, 30–80) with log-rate = −4 + 0.03·age. Fit the Poisson GLM with offset, interpret both parameters in plain words, predict the expected readmission count for a 65-year-old admitted for 10 days, and verify against the true rate.

Solution
Reading: baseline rate ≈ e^−4 ≈ 0.018/day at age 30 (intercept’s reference); each decade multiplies the daily rate by ≈ 1.35. The offset does the bookkeeping for follow-up time — the model speaks in rates, the data arrive as counts.

Exercise 2. Overdispersion forensics: generate counts with Var = 4× mean (negative binomial), fit (a) Poisson GLM, (b) quasi-Poisson (sm.families.Poisson() + scale='X2' — Pearson-scaled SEs), (c) negative binomial GLM. Compare the slope estimates (all ≈ unbiased) and the three SEs, and report each model’s p-value for the slope. Which models “know” the truth about uncertainty?

Solution
All three slopes agree (the mean model is fine); the Poisson SE is the liar — small p-value manufactured by pretending Var = mean. Quasi-Poisson (scaled SEs) and negative binomial (explicit variance function) restore honest uncertainty. In biology, always run the dispersion diagnostic before trusting a Poisson p-value.

Exercise 3. GLM diagnostics, Poisson edition: for the Exercise 1 setup, build the randomized-quantile or deviance-residual diagnostics: plot deviance residuals vs fitted and vs age; QQ-plot them; verify approximate normality of deviance residuals under correct specification. Then inject a mis-fit (drop the offset) and show which diagnostic screams first.

Solution
With the offset, deviance residuals look like N(0,1) noise (dispersion ≈ 1); without it, dispersion explodes (counts without their exposure correction look wildly inconsistent) and the residual-vs-fitted panel develops structure. The first diagnostic to scream: the dispersion ratio — it is the Poisson world’s heteroscedasticity check (2.10 analog).

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Log-link counts and zero observations: log(0) never appears in the model (the link acts on the mean, not on y) — but a manual log(y+1) OLS “fix” changes the estimand and breaks at different y’s. Model the counts with the right likelihood instead.
  • Overdispersion is the default state of biological counts — quasi-Poisson or negative binomial before any p-value is read (Exercise 2); the Poisson is a special-case claim, not a default.
  • Rate ratios vs risk ratios: with log links, e^β multiplies rates; converting to risk differences/ratios needs the baseline and horizon — the same translation discipline as 3.3’s odds ratios.
  • Offsets vs exposure covariates: an offset has coefficient fixed at 1; leaving exposure out entirely (or as a free coefficient) answers a different question — model the design, don’t average over it.
  • Complete separation’s cousin — zero cells: grouped binomial tables with all-success/all-failure strata destabilize fits; the same regularization/futility logic as 3.3 Exercise 2.
  • GLM SEs are inverse-Hessian (asymptotic): valid for n reasonably large; small-sample counts (few events) need exact/penalized methods — the rare-event caveat again.

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated count analysis has three bugs. Run, diagnose, fix.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. log(y+1) OLS: changes the estimand (now modeling log-counts, with the +1 bias), mishandles the count nature, and mixes depth in as a free coefficient on the wrong scale. Model rates with log link + offset.
  2. Depth ignored: dose effect is confounded with sequencing effort — samples with higher depth have more counts regardless of dose. Fix: offset=np.log(depth) (or include log-depth as a covariate with free coefficient if effort itself needs modeling).
  3. Dispersion unchecked: with Poisson SEs, significance claims are premature. Run Pearson chi²/df, scale or go negative binomial if ≫ 1.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the Poisson GLM’s IRLS update and show its working response is \(z = \eta + (y - \mu)/\mu\) and its weight is \(w = \mu\). Use this to explain (a) why zero-count observations get high weight in the fit relative to OLS’s treatment of residuals, and (b) why the variance structure (w = μ) automatically produces the funnel-shaped raw residuals of Exercise-drill 2 — and why that funnel is correct behavior, not a violation.

Hint For Poisson, Var(y) = μ; the IRLS weight is Var⁻¹ evaluated at the current μ; the working response is the linearization of the link.
Solution With g = log, g′(μ) = 1/μ: working response z = g(μ) + (y − μ)g′(μ) = η + (y−μ)/μ; IRLS weight w = 1/(g′(μ)²Var) = μ. (a) A zero count at μ = 4 gives working residual (0−4)/4 = −1 — the same pull as a count of 8 (+1): the link scales residuals by 1/μ, so relative misses weigh equally; OLS would treat the count-0 miss (−4) as four times the count-8 miss — inverting the information content for heteroscedastic counts. (b) Raw residuals y−μ have SD √μ by construction: the funnel in raw residuals is the model passing its own variance assumption; only deviance/Pearson residuals (normalized) should look homoscedastic. Diagnosing raw GLM residuals with OLS eyes inverts the verdict.

Q2. A colleague fits a Poisson GLM to single-cell UMI counts (dropped-out, overdispersed, zero-inflated) and reports significant genes via Poisson p-values. Design the critique experiment: simulate zero-inflated negative-binomial counts with (a) no real genes and (b) 100 truly differential genes; run the Poisson pipeline; report false-positive rate and sensitivity. Then state which layer of the pipeline each problem (overdispersion, zero-inflation) belongs to, and what the standard production answer is (negative binomial with empirical dispersion — the DESeq2/edgeR view, previewing Part 12’s T2 workflow).

Solution
The Poisson pipeline flags null genes far above 5% (overdispersion + zero-inflation inflate the statistic) while missing many true effects. Layering: overdispersion is a variance-function misspecification (fix: NB/quasi-likelihood); zero-inflation is a mean-process mixture (fix: ZINB hurdle models, or aggregates/normalization strategies). Production pipelines (DESeq2/edgeR) use empirical-Bayes negative binomial dispersions across genes — the principle: share variance information across the feature family rather than trusting per-gene Poisson. Full treatment in 12.6 (T2).

What plays the role of R² here (hint: compare against the intercept-only GLM’s deviance)? This “explained deviance” is how GLM model accuracy is reported — practice computing it honestly.

7. Summary

  • GLM = family × linear predictor × link; logistic and linear regression are members; Poisson (counts, log link) is the workhorse you didn’t know you needed.
  • e^β = rate ratio; offsets convert counts to rates when exposure varies.
  • OLS on counts predicts negatives, misweights residuals, and misstates variance — the likelihood is the fix, not a transform.
  • Overdispersion (Pearson χ²/df ≫ 1) is biology’s default; quasi-Poisson/negative binomial before believing p-values.
  • Diagnostics: deviance residuals (not raw), dispersion ratio as the first alarm.
  • p ≫ n and rare events strain GLM SEs — regularization and exact methods await in Part 5.
  • Every model so far needs the same referee: how well does it predict new data, really? Part 4 begins: 4.1 The generalization problem.
