3.3 Logistic Regression

Logistic regression models P(y = 1) as a sigmoid of a linear score — bounded, Bernoulli-likelihood-honest, and the workhorse classifier of applied statistics. Its coefficients speak in log-odds, which is either its greatest interpretive feature or its greatest PR problem, depending on how well you’ve drilled this page.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The model:

\[p(x) = \frac{e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x}}{1 + e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \log\frac{p(x)}{1 - p(x)} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\]

The logit (log-odds) is linear in x; the sigmoid maps it back to [0, 1]. Coefficients are log-odds ratios: \(e^{\beta}\) multiplies the odds of the outcome per unit of x. Fitting maximizes the Bernoulli log-likelihood — no closed form, solved by iteration (Newton/IRLS):

\[\ell(\beta) = \sum_i \big[ y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i) \big]\]

Note penalty=None: sklearn’s default penalizes the fit (C = 1.0) — worth knowing before comparing coefficients against statsmodels’ unpenalized MLE:

2. Concept Questions

  1. β = 0.693 for a binary treatment. Translate twice: once into odds, once into a sentence a patient understands.
  2. Where is the decision boundary in terms of the linear score, and why does moving β₀ shift it but moving β₁ rotate it?
  3. sklearn’s default C = 1.0 penalizes coefficients. What happens to the fitted coefficients as C → ∞ and as C → 0, and what real-world failure does the C → 0 extreme prevent?
  4. Logistic regression has no closed-form solution. Why (in one sentence), and what does the algorithm actually optimize?
  5. Your model’s predicted probabilities are well-ranked but poorly calibrated (everything too extreme). Which metric family is blind to this, and what might have caused it?
Answers
  1. Odds multiply by e^0.693 = 2.0 — treatment doubles the odds. For a patient: “if your baseline chance were 1-in-10 (odds 1:9), treatment moves you to about 2-in-9 ≈ 18% chance.” (Odds doubling ≠ probability doubling — the translation must go through the patient’s baseline.)
  2. Boundary = where p = 0.5 = where linear score = 0 (a line, β₀ + β₁x = 0). β₀ shifts the line (offset); β₁ changes its direction/steepness (in 2-D: the normal vector). β₁’s magnitude sets how quickly probability transitions across the boundary.
  3. C → ∞ = no penalty = unpenalized MLE (coefficients as large as likelihood demands); C → 0 = coefficients crushed toward 0 (predicting base rate everywhere). Small C prevents coefficient blow-up under separation or p ≫ n — a ridge penalty in disguise (Part 5).
  4. The log-likelihood is nonlinear in β (β sits inside a sigmoid), so no algebraic zero exists; software maximizes ℓ(β) iteratively — Newton–Raphson/IRLS — the same machinery statsmodels’ summary then reuses for SEs (inverse Hessian).
  5. AUC/AP (3.1, 3.1.5) — rank-based, calibration-blind. Causes: training/serving prevalence mismatch, regularization, resampled training data (oversampling positives), or a drifted population — the fixes live in 3.7’s calibration section.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. Hand-compute a prediction: given fitted β₀ = −4.0, β₁ = 0.08 (age, in years) for disease status — compute P(disease) at ages 40, 50, 60 by hand (sigmoid), the odds at each age, and verify the odds ratio between consecutive ages equals e^(0.08×5) ≈ 1.49. Then compute the risk ratio between ages 50 and 60 and explain why it differs from the odds ratio. (Keep β₁ small on purpose: a steep slope plus a large negative intercept would push every age to P ≈ 1 and flatten the whole demonstration.)

Solution
The odds ratio is constant (1.49 per 5 years — the model’s defining constraint: the log-odds is linear, so equal log-score steps mean equal multiplicative odds steps). The risk ratio depends on where you are on the curve: 1.38 for 50→60 but 1.61 for 40→50, even though the underlying odds ratio is identical in both intervals. Odds ratios are portable and log-symmetric; risk ratios are what many audiences think they’re hearing, and they shrink toward 1 as risk approaches 0 or 1 — always know which one you quoted.

Exercise 2. The separation problem: construct x where all y = 1 for x > 0 and y = 0 for x < 0 (perfect separation). Fit unpenalized (penalty=None) and watch the coefficients; then fit with C = 0.1 and C = 100. Report coefficient magnitudes, and explain why the MLE “does not exist” here and what the small-C fit is implicitly doing about it.

Solution
Under perfect separation, likelihood increases forever as the slope grows (the model can approach zero training error by steepening the sigmoid) — the MLE diverges to ±∞. The penalty (small C) caps the climb, acting as the prior/regularization that makes the problem well-posed: a finite, conservative boundary. Symptoms in the wild: “convergence warnings” + absurdly large coefficients + predicted probabilities at exactly 0/1 — fix with regularization or better data, not more iterations.

Exercise 3. Logistic regression in a pipeline with honest tuning: build make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000)), tune C over a log grid with 5-fold CV (GridSearchCV), and report (a) the chosen C, (b) CV AUC, (c) test AUC on a held-out set, and (d) the odds ratios from the refit model. Then re-run everything with random_state changed — how stable are (a)–(d)?

Solution
Everything is stable to ~a decimal place except C’s exact position on the grid (neighboring Cs perform nearly identically — see the one-standard-error discussion in 4.6). The pipeline guarantees scaling is fit per-fold; the test set answers once, at the end.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • Odds ratios vs risk ratios vs percentage points — three languages for one coefficient. e^β multiplies odds; converting to risk changes requires knowing the baseline (Exercise 1); quoting “30% increase” for e^β = 1.3 is wrong unless p is small.
  • sklearn penalizes by default — penalty=None (or huge C) for MLE-comparable inference; statsmodels Logit for SEs/p-values. Mixing the two without checking is a silent-model-swap bug.
  • Regularized coefficients are biased — their SEs are not the inverse-Hessian values; statsmodels-style inference on a penalized fit is invalid (bootstrap if you must).
  • Separation/quasi-separation → diverging coefficients and zero-width CIs; recognize the convergence warnings, regularize or collect data (Exercise 2).
  • Complete-data quirks: sklearn’s classes are order-sensitive in coef_ sign conventions; predict_proba[:, 1] is P(class 1) — with string labels, “class 1” is classes_[1], alphabetically determined. Check model.classes_.
  • Logistic is additive in log-odds — interactions and nonlinearities enter exactly as in Part 2 (2.7, 2.8) but their interpretation happens on the odds scale, which multiplies rather than adds.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Penalized coefficients read as MLE odds ratios: default C = 1 shrinks the coefficient, so the “odds ratio” understates the association. Use penalty=None for interpretable coefficients (or disclose the penalty).
  2. Saturated probabilities described with certainty language: predicted probabilities are never “100% risk” — 0.9997 ≠ certainty; and the 0.5 threshold has no clinical meaning by default. Report ranges and set thresholds from costs (3.1).
  3. Unit-dependent coefficient comparison: multiplying x by 1000 divides the coefficient by 1000 — neither magnitude means anything without units. Standardize (per-SD odds ratios) to compare.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Derive the gradient of the Bernoulli log-likelihood for logistic regression and show it equals \(X^\top (y - p)\) — the same form as linear regression’s normal equations. Two consequences to explain: (a) why Newton–Raphson for logistic = iteratively reweighted least squares, (b) why a perfect-separation dataset makes both the gradient and the Hessian misbehave (relate to Exercise 2).

Hint ℓ = Σ[y log p + (1−y) log(1−p)]; dp/dz = p(1−p) with z = x^Tβ; then differentiate ℓ in β via the chain rule.
Solution With z_i = x_i^Tβ and p_i = σ(z_i): ∂ℓ/∂β = Σ (y_i − p_i) x_i = X^T(y − p). Setting it to zero mirrors OLS’s X^T(y − Xβ) = 0, but p depends on β through sigmoids, so the equation is transcendental — no closed form. Newton–Raphson needs the Hessian H = −X^T W X with W = diag(p(1−p)); the update solves the weighted least-squares problem X^TWX β_new = X^TW z_working — IRLS: iteratively reweighted least squares with working response z = x^Tβ + (y−p)/(p(1−p)). Under separation, as ‖β‖→∞, p_i → 0/1 matching y_i, the gradient X^T(y−p) → 0 along the diverging direction while the log-likelihood itself → its supremum: the iteration drifts to ‖β‖ = ∞ and W’s entries vanish (Hessian degenerates) — numerically rendered as “failure to converge,” statistically as a non-existent MLE.

Q2. LDA vs logistic — the famous equivalence: for two Gaussian classes with shared covariance, both models yield parallel linear boundaries, and their coefficients are proportional. Verify numerically (fit both, compare coefficient directions), then break the equivalence twice — (a) unequal covariances, (b) heavy-tailed features (t-distributed) — and report which model degrades more in each case and why (generative assumption vs discriminative flexibility).

Solution
Equal covariances: directions align (cos ≈ 1) — the two are near-proportional estimates of the same boundary. Unequal covariances: LDA’s shared-covariance assumption is false → its single pooled boundary is mis-specified (QDA is the fix), while logistic, modeling only P(y|x), flexibly fits some linear boundary (though now the Bayes boundary itself is quadratic — both are limited). Heavy tails: LDA’s means/covariances are moment-based and outlier-driven; the logistic likelihood, depending only on y given the score, is typically more robust. Moral (Efron/Friedman): generative models win when their density assumptions hold (faster convergence, fewer parameters); discriminative models are safer when assumptions are questionable.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (logistic × 2.6–2.7). Build a treatment × biomarker model: logit(P(response)) ~ treatment * biomarker_high. (a) Fit and interpret each coefficient in odds-ratio language, including the interaction. (b) Convert the interaction into a difference of log-odds — then show numerically that a positive interaction in log-odds can correspond to a negative difference in absolute risk change at high baselines. What does this imply for presenting “effect modification” to non-statistical audiences?

C2 (logistic × 4.6 Tuning × 1.3 Bayes). Train on a case–control dataset with 50% positives (sampled prevalence ≠ population prevalence of 5%). Show that (unpenalized) logistic regression estimates coefficients consistently but its intercept — hence all predicted probabilities — is miscalibrated by the sampling ratio; correct it with the logit-offset correction (subtract log(sampling odds)) and verify calibration before/after on a population-prevalence test set. This intercept-transport trick is standard in epidemiology and worth owning.

7. Summary

  • p = σ(linear score): bounded probabilities, Bernoulli likelihood, MLE by IRLS.
  • Coefficients are log-odds ratios — translate consciously (odds ≠ risk), and standardize before comparing.
  • sklearn penalizes by default; statsmodels gives unpenalized MLE inference — know which model you fitted.
  • Separation breaks the MLE; regularization is the practical answer, warnings the tell.
  • Boundary: score = 0; intercept shifts it, slopes rotate it; probabilities are smooth across it.
  • Additivity holds in log-odds: interactions multiply odds, not risks — mind the audience.
