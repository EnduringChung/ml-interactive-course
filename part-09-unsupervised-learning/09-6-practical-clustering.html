9.6 Practical Clustering Issues

Every previous clustering page deferred the same questions: how do I choose k defensibly, which validation do I report, what do I do about mixed data types, outliers, and the fact that clustering is an optimization game with many legitimate answers? This page assembles the practical machinery — stability, internal indices, external validation — into one honest workflow.

Tier 1 — every cell below runs live.

1. Basic Concept

The validation triad, defined precisely:

  1. Internal — silhouette, inertia-based indices: does the partition fit the chosen metric? (necessary, never sufficient)
  2. Stability — do partitions reproduce under resampling/perturbation/seed changes? (reproducibility evidence)
  3. External — do clusters align with domain-known variables (outcomes, batches, replicated cohorts)? (the only truth-adjacent evidence)

2. Concept Questions

  1. Silhouette’s formula rewards which geometry, and what truth-shape makes it systematically misleading?
  2. Stability selects reproducible partitions. Construct (in one sentence) the case where a partition is perfectly stable and scientifically wrong.
  3. External validation correlates clusters with outcomes. What does a strong cluster–outcome association not prove (two answers: one statistical, one causal)?
  4. Mixed data types (continuous + categorical): why does one-hot + Euclidean quietly distort, and what does the distortion favor?
  5. You report “we found 4 clusters (silhouette-selected among k = 2..9).” Name the two selection games you just played and the evidence a reviewer should demand.
Answers
  1. Silhouette = (b − a)/max(a, b): cohesion-to-nearest-other-neighbor ratio. It rewards convex, well-separated, similarly-sized clusters and penalizes elongated/ring/uneven truths even when perfectly recovered (9.4’s rings; spectral methods score “poorly” on silhouettes they solve perfectly).
  2. Stable-but-wrong: K-means with k = 4 on two true blobs at a strong scale artifact — resamples reproduce the same 4-way artifact every time (stability measures the procedure, the truth lives outside it — 9.1 Q2).
  3. Statistical: no multiple-testing control (you tested many clusterings/covariates until one “worked” — 12.x stub). Causal: cluster membership is a derived feature; association with outcome may ride on the same confounders that shaped the clustering — clusters are not interventions.
  4. One-hot categorical columns contribute 0/1 coordinates whose scale (k levels → unit spacing) silently weights them against standardized continuous features (or for them, with many levels); distances become an accidental blend. Favor: whichever block the encoding happened to amplify — dominant-axis behavior (9.1’s sensitivity, encoded).
    1. The k-selection game (9 values tried, best reported — the 4.6 selection bonus); (2) the index-selection game (silhouette chosen from the index zoo). Demanded: stability at reported k, index-agreement, external validation, and all tried values disclosed.

3. Drills & Exercises

Drills

Predict, then run.

Exercises (with solutions)

Exercise 1. The full k-selection tribunal: implement select_k(X, k_range, B=40) producing a DataFrame with, per k: inertia, silhouette, bootstrap stability (mean ARI), and the gap-style null comparison (mini). Run it on (a) 4 planted blobs, (b) null uniform data. Report both tables. Define the selection policy you’d pre-register (e.g., “k must maximize ≥2 indices, achieve stability > 0.8, and beat the null silhouette by ≥ 0.1”) and check what each dataset yields.

Solution
Pre-registered policy example: “k must have stability ≥ 0.75, silhouette within 0.05 of the max, and gap > 0.1; ties resolved toward smaller k.” Planted data: k = 4 passes all gates; null data: nothing passes (stability low, gap ~0) — the pipeline’s most important output is often refusing to claim clusters. That refusal must be a pre-registered outcome, not a failure.

Exercise 2. External validation done right: on a dataset with a known binary covariate (treatment arm) and a known continuous outcome, cluster into k = 3 and test cluster–treatment (chi-square) and cluster–outcome (ANOVA/Kruskal) associations with multiplicity control over k ∈ 2..6 (report best p-value vs Bonferroni threshold). Then invert the causal framing: the clusters were built from features including the outcome-correlated ones — what does that contaminate? Rebuild excluding those features and re-test. Write the association-claim sentence that survives.

Solution
The leaky features manufacture cluster–outcome association by construction (the outcome shaped the clustering) — no causal or even associational claim survives; the multiplicity correction alone doesn’t save it. Rebuilt without outcome-correlated features, any surviving association is at least arguable. Surviving sentence: “Clusters derived from baseline features (excluding outcome-correlated measurements) were associated with the outcome at Bonferroni-corrected p = …, requiring replication in an independent cohort” — associations, replicated, with the construction disclosed.

Exercise 3. Mixed-type and outlier-safe workflow: on a cohort with 3 numeric + 2 categorical features (some rows also outliers), implement (a) one-hot + standardized-numerics K-means with categorical weight as a tuned parameter (compare weight 1 vs √(levels)), (b) outlier screen (IsolationForest or IQR) before clustering with the removed rows reported separately, (c) full validation battery on the final k. Report the pipeline’s choices as a reproducible spec — the “methods paragraph” for mixed-data clustering.

Solution
Methods paragraph: “Numeric features were standardized; categorical features were one-hot encoded and weighted at w = √(levels) (comparable total mass to the numeric block; w tuned by silhouette sensitivity). Isolation-forest outliers (n = …) were flagged and analyzed separately. K was selected by combined silhouette/stability criteria over k = 2–4, and the resulting partition was tested against treatment and outcome associations with multiplicity correction.” Every number in that paragraph came from a check you’ve now implemented — that’s what makes it reproducible rather than narrative.

4. Advanced Information & Pitfalls

  • The index zoo is not the law: dozens of internal indices (Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz, …) exist; they disagree systematically by geometry — pre-commit to your index set and policy, or report the zoo (12.x stub’s multiplicity, again).
  • Subsampling changes k: stability curves can cross — k stable at bootstrap B₁ unstable at B₂ means your “clusters” live at one sampling density; report the stability curve, not one point.
  • Outliers both create and destroy clusters: one outlier = one hijacked centroid; a dense outlier pocket = a “real” cluster of artifacts. Detect, report, and decide by mechanism (7.2’s ledger for trees; here for points).
  • Batch effects outrun biology (5.6): audit clusters against batch/site first — a cluster that’s 95% one batch is a process finding, not a discovery.
  • Clustering ≠ classification of a latent truth unless you validate externally: the “confusion matrix” against a known labeling is a bonus analysis (ARI), not the definition of success (9.1 Q1’s impossibility).
  • Reproducibility spec: algorithm, version, distance, scaling, k (and how chosen), initialization/restarts, seed, outlier policy — a clustering without this spec is a rumor.

Read & Refactor AI Code

This AI-generated “validated clustering” has three bugs. Run, diagnose, fix.

What’s wrong, and the fix
  1. Argmax over the k × metric zoo: the selection bonus (4.1 Q2) plus index-shopping — report all tried values and pre-commit the policy.
  2. Validation by the selection metric: circular by construction; validation must be a different evidence family (stability, external).
  3. Cluster–batch agreement rebranded as subtypes: high agreement means the clustering reproduced the artifact. Audit, disclose, batch-correct, and re-cluster — then look for structure that isn’t the batch.

5. Advanced Questions

Q1. Prove the silhouette–stability independence: construct two datasets with identical mean silhouette for k = 3 but different bootstrap stability (one with a genuinely separated 3-group geometry, one with three coin-flip partitions of uniform data). Implement both and verify. What does this construction prove about composing internal and stability evidence — and why is the composition necessary rather than redundant?

Hint</summary»

Silhouette is a fixed-dataset geometric average; stability is a resampling property. Uniform data can be carved into 3 well-separated-by-chance regions in one draw.

Solution</summary»

Uniform data’s one draw can show decent silhouette by luck (three separated-looking patches from the triangulation of randomness), while resampling reshuffles the patches — stability collapses. Conversely a stable partition can have modest silhouette (elongated truth). Construction:

The composition is necessary because the two evidences measure orthogonal failure modes: internal indices can’t detect sampling fragility; stability can’t detect geometry mismatch with the truth. Any single-number validation claim (“silhouette 0.6, good clustering”) commits at least one of the two errors this construction isolates.

Q2. Consensus clustering, built and judged: implement the consensus matrix for K-means across 200 subsampled runs at a given k — C_ij = fraction of runs where i and j co-occur. Visualize C (sorted by consensus ordering) for the planted-blob dataset at k ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5}: the true k shows a clean block-diagonal C, others show ragged off-blocks. Define the summary statistic (mean within-block vs between-block consensus gap) and verify it peaks at the true k. Then state the one caution: consensus measures clustering-algorithm agreement, not truth — connect to 9.1 Q1.

Solution
The gap peaks at the planted k: within-consensus near 1, between near 0 only when k matches the true block structure. Caution: consensus quantifies how self-consistently the algorithm partitions this dataset — a memorizable-but-wrong structure is equally capable of high consensus (stable-artifact case, 9.1 Q2). Consensus is the strongest internal reproducibility evidence available; external validation still outranks it.

6. Complex Questions

C1 (practical × 7.x ensembles × 9.2). Ensemble-clustering pipeline: build a co-association matrix from 100 K-means runs (varying seeds, subsamples, feature subsets) instead of one run; cluster the co-association matrix (hierarchical, ward) for the final partition. On planted data with an ambiguous k, compare consensus-ensemble partition vs single-run K-means by ARI and stability. Quantify the ensemble dividend, name its cost (compute + lost per-run interpretability), and connect to why bagging works for trees (7.3) — variance reduction by averaging unstable structures.

C2 (practical × 2.5 confounding × 9.1). Clusters as covariates, honestly: derive cluster dummies from an unsupervised run, then use them as covariates in a regression predicting an outcome. Simulate data where (a) clusters genuinely moderate the outcome-gradient and (b) clusters are pure noise. Compare: treating cluster dummies as fixed known covariates (ignoring the estimation of clusters) vs the uncertainty-aware view (clusters are estimated — the classic classification-measurement-error issue). Quantify the SE overconfidence of the naive route across 300 simulations, and write the reporting sentence that acknowledges the two-stage estimation.

7. Summary

  • Validation is a triad: internal (metric fit), stability (resampling reproducibility), external (domain evidence) — claims need the members their language implies.
  • Silhouette rewards convex geometry; stability rewards procedure reproducibility; neither certifies truth — compose them and add external evidence.
  • Choose k by pre-registered multi-evidence policy; refusal (“no defensible clusters”) is a publishable outcome.
  • Mixed data: encoding weight is a hyperparameter; outliers are audited before k; batch is audited before biology.
  • Consensus/co-association matrices are the strongest internal reproducibility evidence — algorithm agreement, not truth.
  • Clusters as downstream covariates are estimated quantities: their uncertainty propagates; disclose the two-stage design.
  • The unsupervised toolkit is complete. From here the course’s remaining parts (survival analysis, multiple testing, LLMs) lean on it — and on everything Parts 0–8 drilled: this was the checklist chapter.
